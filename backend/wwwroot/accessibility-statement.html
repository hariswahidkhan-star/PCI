Accessibility Statement.

Our commitment to a website usable by everyone — and how to tell us when it isn’t.

Overview

PCI wants its website to be usable by as many people as possible, regardless of ability or device. This statement sets out our approach, the standards we aim for, and how to report a barrier so we can fix it.

Why this matters

Clear policies matter because they are how fairness is made real. A certification body that treats everyone consistently — on eligibility, conduct, security, impartiality and appeals — is one whose decisions can be trusted, and whose credential therefore means the same thing for everyone who holds it. These pages set out those commitments plainly.

PCI states its position honestly, including where processes are still being established. The aim is not legal cover for its own sake but genuine, transparent governance — the substance that lets a credential, and the body behind it, earn and keep professional trust.

  • Substance over marketing
  • Fair, transparent process
  • Honesty about our status
  • Responsible, governed use of AI

Built-in measures

How accessibility is designed in

Accessibility works best when it is part of how pages are built, not a repair applied afterwards. The practices below run through the whole site, not a handful of showcase pages.

Keyboard first

Navigation, search, forms and expandable questions can all be reached and operated with a keyboard alone, with a visible focus state and a skip link straight to the main content.

Semantic structure

Pages use proper landmarks, a single heading hierarchy and labelled form fields, so screen readers can announce where you are and what each control does.

Readable by default

Text is real text, never an image of text. Contrast and sizing aim at WCAG 2.1 AA, and layouts reflow rather than break when you zoom.

Reduced motion

If your device asks for reduced motion, decorative scroll animations are switched off entirely rather than merely toned down.

Descriptive links

Link text is written to make sense out of context, so a list of links read aloud by assistive technology stays meaningful.

Honest limitations

Where a page falls short of the standard we aim for, we treat that as a defect to be fixed and prioritised — not a footnote.

Report a barrier

Tell us what is not working

If any part of this site is hard to use with your device or assistive technology, we want to know about it. The most useful reports include:

  • The address of the page where you hit the barrier
  • What you were trying to do, and what happened instead
  • Your device, browser and any assistive technology in use
  • How the barrier affected you — blocked completely, or made harder

Send this through the contact page. Reports are acknowledged promptly and prioritised by severity: a barrier that prevents someone enrolling or reading a policy is treated with more urgency than a cosmetic fault. If you need the substance of a page or document in a different format, ask — we will provide an accessible alternative wherever practicable. And if you are not satisfied with how a report is handled, the complaints policy applies to accessibility exactly as it does to everything else.

Do I need an account to report an accessibility problem?

No. Anyone can report a barrier, whether or not they are a member or candidate. The contact form and email route are open to all visitors, and reports are reviewed regardless of who sends them.

In practice

The standard we build to, and the loop when we miss

The site is built toward WCAG 2.1 AA: semantic structure, keyboard operability, visible focus, alt text, sufficient contrast, motion that respects reduced-motion preferences, and forms with real labels. Examination accessibility runs further, through individually-assessed accommodations.

Where we fall short, the loop is simple: report the barrier via contact, we acknowledge it, and fixes enter corrective action with an owner. Accessibility feedback is treated as a defect report, not a favour.
