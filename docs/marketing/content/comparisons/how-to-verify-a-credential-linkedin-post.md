# Four things a candidate can hand you that prove nothing

*LinkedIn text post — 1,890 characters including hashtags. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

Four things a candidate can hand you that prove nothing.

A line on a CV. A PDF certificate. A badge image in an email signature. A screenshot of a register entry.

Each proves that the artefact exists. That is all. And none of this implies bad faith — most people who send a certificate believe it settles the matter, and most of the time they are right about the underlying fact. It only matters in the case where the claim is wrong, which is the case you are checking for.

Four steps, about four minutes.

One. Ask for the full credential title as it appears on the award, plus the holder's registry reference. In writing, from the person, not transcribed off the CV.

Two. Reach the register by typing the awarding body's address yourself. Not a link in the candidate's email, not a sponsored search result. Lookalike domains are the commonest failure in this whole procedure, and they work because the person checking is in a hurry.

Three. Read status and dates, not existence. Active, suspended, withdrawn, lapsed. A credential that lapsed in March is not a credential in November, whatever the certificate says.

Four. Check whether you are reading a certification or a certificate of attendance. Attendance proves presence at a course. Certification proves an assessed outcome against a published standard. Both are real documents; they are not the same document, and the reader usually supplies the confusion.

What a clean verification will not tell you: what the examination covered, at what depth, or whether it fits the role. That is the syllabus, and it is a separate hour.
