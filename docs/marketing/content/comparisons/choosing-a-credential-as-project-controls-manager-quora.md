# What certification should a project controls manager take?

*Quora answer. Question it answers: "I manage a project controls team. What certification should
I take next, and is a second one a waste of money?" Answer completely, then one link at the end.*

---

Declare the interest first: I work for Project Controls Institute Global, which operates
credentialfinder.org and awards the credential I name at the end. Read the rest with that in
mind; it is written to be useful whether or not you ever look at ours.

A second credential is a waste of money when it examines what you already do, at the depth you
already do it. It is not a waste when it examines the ground your signature has moved onto. So
work out which of those you are buying before you look at any scheme.

Here is the test I would use, and it takes one evening.

Budget at completion £40.0m. Actual cost to date £18.0m. Earned value £16.4m. Cost performance
index = 16.4 ÷ 18.0 = 0.911. Take a schedule performance index of 0.95.

Treat the overrun as a one-off and the forecast is 18.0 + (40.0 − 16.4) = £41.6m. Assume
performance to date holds and it is 40.0 ÷ 0.911 = £43.9m. Assume both cost and schedule pressure
run to completion and it is 18.0 + 23.6 ÷ (0.911 × 0.95) = £45.3m. A fourth route, the bottom-up
re-estimate, is yours to build line by line.

That is a £3.7m spread across three defensible answers from one dataset. Nothing separates them
but an assumption a person chooses and then defends, which is why selecting among them is the
competence and reciting the formulas is not.

Now the second half, which is where most controls managers find their actual gap. Where progress
is measured by costs incurred against total expected costs, your forecast at completion is the
denominator. Move it and the measured percentage moves; move that and recognised revenue moves
with it. So a £3.7m forecast spread is also a spread in reported margin.

Present £43.9m and the question back is rarely "how did you calculate it". It is "what happens if
it is £45.3m, and has that been provided for". Controls method does not answer the second
question.

Against that, the categories sort themselves quickly. The established project controls
certifications examine measurement, progress, forecasting and the control of delivery, which is
the core of your role and is examined seriously. Cost and scheduling credentials examine
estimating, cost control, planning, schedule analysis and the economics of an asset's life, which
is where the other half gets covered if you came up through one side. Project management
certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment,
which is the right purchase if you are heading for a delivery seat rather than deeper into
controls.

Each examines its own subject thoroughly. The cut-off calendar — which date progress is cut on,
which date the ledger is cut on, who may restate an index afterwards — belongs to none of them,
and it is where the month-end arguments live.

If that join is your recurring problem, PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) covers 13 domains
and 61 knowledge areas across the control cycle and the reporting handover.

Two honest caveats. If your employer's grading matrix names a certification for the band you
want, take that one, because a mandate beats a fit argument. And PCI is a new, independent
certifying body: not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body,
and not claiming to be, though the scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. The
Body of Knowledge is published free and in full, so judge the syllabus rather than my word.
