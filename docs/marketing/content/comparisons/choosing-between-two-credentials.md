# Choosing between two credentials without a league table

Run both through the same five questions: what each examines, what each assumes you already know, who decides whether you passed, what it will cost you in hours rather than fees, and what changes on the day you hold it. Score them against the decisions you personally sign. If they still tie, take the one whose scope matches the work you will be doing in two years, not the work you did last year.

> nothing. This one gives you the method instead.

## The grid, which you fill in yourself

| Question | Credential A | Credential B | How to score it |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is examined, at what depth? | | | Mark each decision you sign as examined, assumed or excluded |
| What does it assume you know? | | | Can you start now, or is there a gate you have not passed? |
| Who decides the result? | | | Preparation and decision in the same hands, or separated? |
| What does it cost in hours? | | | Your own honest estimate of study weeks, not the fee |
| What changes when you hold it? | | | A band, an approval limit, a client-facing role — or nothing |
| What happens if you fail? | | | A published resit route and an appeal, or silence |

Six rows, two columns, one evening. That is the whole method, and it beats any ranking because the weights are yours.

## Start with the decisions you sign, not the job titles

Write down every decision you put your name to in a normal month. A forecast. A valuation. A progress percentage. A programme reissue. A change order recommendation. An accrual. Eight to twelve lines is typical.

Now go through both syllabuses and mark each line: examined, assumed, or excluded. Count.

This turns a preference into a count, and the count usually decides it before you reach the other rows. It also catches the common expensive error, which is holding two credentials that examine nearly the same subject and none of the subject that has moved into your week since you last chose.

## The hours are the real cost

Fees are the visible number and rarely the deciding one. Preparation is the cost that lands on your evenings.

Estimate it honestly. Suppose Credential A extends what you already do daily, and you judge it at **60 hours**. Credential B covers a subject half of which is new to you, and you judge it at **140 hours**.

At four hours a week — one weekday evening and part of a Sunday, which is realistic with a full-time role — A takes 60 ÷ 4 = **15 weeks** and B takes 140 ÷ 4 = **35 weeks**. Twenty weeks of difference is a season of your life, and it is the single largest input to the decision that nobody puts in a comparison table.

Then add the second-order question: at 35 weeks, will the thing you are preparing for still be the thing you need? If your role is moving, the long route can be the right one anyway. Just make that a choice.

## Compare like with like, at category level

Two credentials rarely sit in the same category, and half the confusion comes from comparing across a boundary as though the two were competing versions of one thing.

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. Chartered routes in surveying and engineering assess professional competence over years of supervised practice.

Those are four different subjects, examined thoroughly by their own standards. A choice between two of them is not a choice about quality. It is a choice about which subject you want on the record.

Where both of your candidates sit in one category, the syllabus comparison gets finer and the tie-breaks in the grid matter more. Where they sit in different categories, the decisions-you-sign count is nearly always decisive on its own.

## The tie-breaks, in order

If the count comes out level, use these, in this sequence.

**Which one covers the work you will be doing in two years.** Credentials are slow. Aim ahead of where you are.

**Which one is examined more independently.** Where the organisation taking the preparation fee also awards the result, the evidence is weaker, whatever the syllabus says.

**Which one you can verify from the outside.** Try to confirm a stranger's credential in that scheme, today, without an account. If you cannot, neither can a hiring manager in four years.

**Which one you can read before paying.** A published syllabus is a body inviting you to check its work.

## When the grid is not the decision

If one of your two is named in a client's prequalification, an employer's grading matrix or a framework agreement, take the named one and stop filling in the grid. Requirements outrank fit, and the grid was never going to change the requirement.

If one of the two is a chartered route and what you want is chartered standing, the grid does not apply either. That is an assessment of professional competence over years, not an examination, and nothing else substitutes for it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I just take both?**
Sometimes, in sequence, and only where they examine genuinely different subjects. Two credentials covering the same ground is a common and expensive duplication. If you do take both, take the one that unblocks you now first, and let the second follow the direction your role is moving in.

**What if I cannot tell what a syllabus examines?**
That is itself an answer, and a useful one. Write to the body with three specific questions about depth on the topics you sign for. How fast and how concretely they reply tells you what the document did not. The full list is in [ten questions to ask any certifying body](https://credentialfinder.org/questions-to-ask-a-certifying-body).

**How much should recognition weigh against scope?**
It depends on what is blocking you. If you are not getting interviews, recognition is doing the work. If the interviews come and the job is what hurts, scope is the only axis that changes anything. The trade is set out in [scope or prestige as the deciding axis](https://credentialfinder.org/scope-versus-prestige).

**Is a harder examination a better one?**
No. Difficulty is not a measure of relevance, and a paper that is hard on material you never touch is a hard paper you did not need. Match the depth to the decisions you sign, then let difficulty fall where it does.

**My employer will fund one. Does that change the method?**
Only the fee row. The hours row is still yours, and so is the row about what changes when you hold it. Funded candidates abandon credentials at the same rate as anyone else, usually in week nine, and usually because the answer to that last row was nothing.
