# Choosing a credential as a project accountant

If your qualification already covers recognition, measurement and disclosure, the gap is not accounting. It is the delivery side of the inputs: whether the estimate you divide by is current, whether the progress behind it was earned, and whether the accrual is complete. A credential worth your evenings is one that examines those questions rather than repeating what you can already do.

## What a project accountant signs, and where the risk actually sits

| What you put your name to | Which category examines it | Where the input comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Recognised revenue for the period | Accountancy and finance qualifications | A percentage complete built by delivery |
| Contract assets and liabilities | Accountancy and finance qualifications | Certification and invoicing timing |
| Provisions, including expected losses | Accountancy and finance qualifications | A forecast owned by a project team |
| Cost accruals at period end | Split across two categories | A cut-off calendar somebody else keeps |
| Margin reporting and movement analysis | Accountancy and finance qualifications | Estimate changes you may hear about late |
| Capitalisation and asset readiness | Accountancy and finance qualifications | A completion judgement made on site |

Every row in the first column is examined thoroughly by an accountancy qualification. Every row in the third column is a number arriving from a process that qualification does not set out to examine, and that asymmetry is the whole of this page.

## The arithmetic, and the week it moves in

Contract price **£33.0m**. Costs incurred to date **£12.0m**. The estimate of total cost you were given at the last review is **£30.0m**.

Progress = 12.0 ÷ 30.0 = **40.0%**.
Revenue = 0.40 × 33.0 = **£13.20m**.
Margin = 13.20 − 12.00 = **£1.20m**.

Now the project team revises the estimate to **£31.5m**, for delivery reasons: a rate agreed above the allowance, a subcontractor replaced, three weeks of rework.

Progress = 12.0 ÷ 31.5 = **38.1%**.
Revenue = 0.381 × 33.0 = **£12.57m**.
Margin = 12.57 − 12.00 = **£0.57m**.

**£0.63m** off the reported margin, decided by a number you did not build. If you have invoiced **£11.0m**, the contract asset is 12.57 − 11.00 = **£1.57m**, which then ages, and its ageing is a conversation with a commercial team about entitlement rather than with a ledger.

The accounting here is not difficult and you already know it. The exposure is upstream: whether that estimate is current, whether the trend log behind it is maintained, and whether anybody would have told you in month seven rather than month nine. That is a delivery-side competence, and the mechanism it feeds is set out in [how a forecast becomes reported profit](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/eac-accounting).

## What each category examines, in its own terms

Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. That is a large, deep subject examined properly, and it is the qualification most project accountants already hold.

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. They cover how the estimate you divide by is built and maintained, which is the input side of every row above.

Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process. They cover whether the controls around those inputs operated, which is a third and different question.

None of the three is deficient. They were designed to examine different things, and a project accountant lives where the first meets the second.

## The five questions worth being able to ask

A project accountant who can ask these is worth more than one who can only book what arrives.

When was the estimate at completion last rebuilt from the bottom up, rather than escalated? What earning rule produced this month's progress, and would a different rule have produced a different number? Which costs are accrued on an estimate rather than a valuation? What is in the change register that has not reached the budget? And which of the amounts in the valuation depend on a negotiation that has not concluded?

Each of those has a right answer, each is checkable, and none of them is examined by an accountancy syllabus, because none of them is accountancy.

## When your existing qualification is the whole answer

If you are progressing inside a finance function — group reporting, treasury, financial control, a route to a controller or director role — complete the accountancy qualification and let it do its job. It is the recognised route, it is examined properly, and a project-side credential does not substitute for a stage of it.

The same applies if your projects are short, repeatable and low-variance. Where estimates rarely move and valuations are agreed monthly, the upstream risk this page describes barely exists, and there is nothing for a second credential to fix.

The case for looking further is specific: long contracts, movable estimates, contested variations, and a reporting line where you are the person explaining a margin swing to a board.

## How to decide

Take your last four margin movements. For each, write down what caused it, when the causing event actually happened, and when you found out. If the lag is routinely a month or more, the gap is upstream information rather than accounting judgement, and a credential examining the delivery side is worth costing.

If the lag is short and the causes were all inside your own control, your qualification covers your job. The comparison at category level, if you want to read it properly, is in [where PFL-AI sits against accountancy and finance qualifications](https://credentialfinder.org/pfl-ai-and-accountancy-and-finance-qualifications).

## Frequently asked questions

**Should a qualified accountant take a project credential at all?**
Only for the delivery half. If a syllabus mostly repeats recognition, measurement and disclosure, you are paying to be examined on what you already hold. Read the domain list and count the material that is genuinely new to you before deciding anything.

**Is this not just industry experience rather than certification?**
Experience teaches it, slowly and unevenly, and certification makes it checkable by somebody who has not worked with you. Both matter. If your experience already covers estimating discipline and earning rules, a credential is evidence rather than education, which is a fair reason to take one and a fair reason not to.

**How do I challenge an estimate I did not build?**
By asking how it was built, not whether it is right. Bottom-up or escalated, when it was last rebuilt, what the trend log shows, and what would have to be true for it to hold. Those questions are answerable and they are not confrontational, which matters if you want the answer again next month.

**What about audit — should I take an audit credential instead?**
If you are moving into assurance, yes. Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process, which is a distinct profession rather than an add-on. The category comparison sits in [where PFL-AI sits against audit credentials](https://credentialfinder.org/pfl-ai-and-audit-credentials).

**Does understanding the delivery side change the numbers I report?**
It should not change the treatment; it changes when you know. Knowing in month seven that an estimate is drifting gives you a quarter to explain it. Finding out in month nine gives you a board meeting. Same accounting, different job.
