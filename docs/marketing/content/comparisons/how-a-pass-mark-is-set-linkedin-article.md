# How a pass mark is set, and what that actually means

How a pass mark is set decides whether your result means anything to anybody else. In a defensible scheme the cut is fixed before anybody sits, by a panel judging what a just-competent candidate should be able to do, and re-derived whenever the paper changes materially. The percentage belongs to that paper, and on its own it tells a candidate almost nothing.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials, so this is written by a body that has to answer the same question about itself. It is answered below rather than avoided.

## Two families, answering two different questions

| | Norm-referenced | Criterion-referenced |
|---|---|---|
| What fixes the cut | A share of the cohort | A judgement about competence |
| Who your result depends on | Everyone who sat with you | Only you and the standard |
| When it can be known | After the sitting | Before the sitting |
| What it is good for | Ranking a field for a limited number of places | Deciding whether somebody meets a standard |

Both are legitimate instruments. They are built for different jobs, and a certification decision is the second job, not the first.

## The norm-referenced problem, in numbers

Say a scheme passes the top 30 per cent.

In one sitting, the 70th percentile score is 66. You score 68 and pass.

In the next sitting, a stronger cohort puts the 70th percentile at 71. You score 68 and fail.

Same knowledge, same paper, different month. The number measured the room you sat in. That is the right answer if the scheme is allocating scarce places, and the wrong one if it is certifying competence.

## The panel method, in numbers

The criterion-referenced route starts with a description of the just-competent candidate: the person who should scrape through, described in terms of what they can do rather than what they have read.

A panel of subject experts then works through the paper item by item, each judging the share of just-competent candidates who would answer that item correctly. A hard item might attract judgements around 0.35, a straightforward one around 0.85.

Average the judgements across the paper. If the mean lands at 0.62, the cut score is 62 per cent of the marks available. The panel usually runs a second round after seeing how each other judged, and sometimes after seeing real performance data, and the average moves a little.

Nothing in that process asks how many candidates will pass. That is the point of it.

## Why two schemes' percentages cannot be compared

The cut is a property of the paper it was derived from. Put the same panel in front of a harder paper and the judgements fall, so the cut falls with them.

A cut of 62 on a hard paper can therefore be a **higher** standard than 70 on an easy one. Ranking two credentials by their published percentages compares nothing, and does it with an air of precision.

This is also why a body that quotes a pass mark without saying how it was derived has told you less than it appears to. Seventy per cent of what, judged by whom, against what description of competence?

## Equating, or the version you happened to draw

Where a scheme runs several versions of a paper, the versions will not be identically difficult, however carefully they are built.

Equating is the statistical adjustment that puts scores from different forms on the same scale, so a candidate is not failed for drawing the harder one. Ask whether a scheme does it. On a scheme with one annual paper it is not an issue; on continuous testing with a large item bank it is central.

## The border, and the rule that should already exist

Measurement carries error. A score is an estimate of a candidate's standing, not a reading off a ruler.

Suppose the standard error of measurement is 3 marks. A 61 against a cut of 62 sits comfortably inside it, and so does a 63. That is not a scandal; it is a property of every examination ever set, including the ones you already hold.

What separates a well-run scheme from a badly run one is not whether the border is fuzzy. It is whether the rule for the border was published before you sat. A fixed rule applied to everybody is defensible. A judgement taken about your paper after somebody saw your name is not, and no amount of statistical sophistication rescues it.

## The question behind the question

A candidate asking about the pass mark is usually asking something else: is this thing fair, and will my result mean anything to somebody else.

Four questions get at that better than the percentage does.

Was the cut set before or after the results were seen? Is it re-derived when the paper changes materially? Where several forms exist, are they equated? And who sat on the panel — were they independent of whoever sells the preparation?

A body that moves the cut after seeing results, with no published rule, is managing its pass rate rather than its standard. That is worth knowing before you enrol rather than afterwards.

## Where this question stops being the deciding one

If your employer mandates a specific certification, how its cut score was derived is interesting and not decisive. Take the mandated one.

If a client's procurement rule requires an accredited credential, a beautifully run standard-setting process at an unaccredited body still will not satisfy the rule. Settle that question first.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a lower pass mark mean an easier examination?**
No, and the assumption is the commonest error in this subject. The cut is derived from the difficulty of the paper, so a low percentage usually indicates a hard paper rather than a soft standard. The only way to compare two schemes is to read what each examines and how each derived its cut.

**What is a "just-competent candidate"?**
A written description of the person who should barely pass: what they can do unsupervised, what they would need help with, what they must not get wrong. Panels judge against that description rather than against their own students, which is what keeps the standard about the work instead of about a cohort.

**Can a body change the pass mark after I have sat?**
It can, and whether it should depends entirely on whether a published rule allowed for it. Re-deriving a cut because a faulty item was withdrawn is normal quality control. Moving it because too many people failed is managing a statistic, and the difference is visible in whether the rule existed beforehand.

**What happens if I score one mark below?**
In a scheme with a published rule, the rule applies and you are told which one. Some schemes review borderline scripts, some allow a resit at reduced cost, some simply apply the cut. All three are defensible; deciding case by case afterwards is not, which is why question 6 on any list of questions for a certifying body should be about appeals.

**Why not just publish the pass mark and let people judge?**
Because a percentage without the paper and the method attached invites exactly the false comparison this article is about. A cut score is meaningful published alongside the standard-setting method and the syllabus it applies to, and meaningless as a number in a brochure.
