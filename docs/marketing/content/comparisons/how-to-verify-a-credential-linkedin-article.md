# How to verify a credential properly: a four-step check

Verifying a credential answers one question and no others: does this person hold what they say they hold, today, in good standing. Four steps get you there. Ask for the exact title and the holder's reference in writing, look it up on the awarding body's own register with an address you typed yourself, read the status and the dates rather than the existence, and confirm the body is the one you think it is.

Everything else people call verification is a document somebody handed you.

A disclosure, because this piece tells you to check a body's own register and it is published on a site an awarding body owns: credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials. Read the advice with that on the table.

## What you are handed, and what it is worth

| What you receive | What it proves on its own |
|---|---|
| A line on a CV | Nothing. It is a claim, and a claim is what you are checking |
| A PDF certificate | That a PDF exists |
| A badge image in an email signature | That an image exists |
| A screenshot of a register entry | That a screenshot exists |
| A record you looked up yourself on the awarding body's register | The claim, as at today |

Only the last row is verification. The other four are the input to it.

None of this implies bad faith. Most people who send a certificate believe it settles the matter, and most of the time they are right about the underlying fact. The point is that only one of these five artefacts can be relied on when it is wrong.

## Step one: get the exact title and the reference, in writing

Ask the holder for the full credential title as it appears on the award, and the registry reference or holder identifier. In writing, from them, not transcribed from the CV.

Two things happen. You get the string you need for the search, and you learn how the person responds to a routine, unremarkable request. Holders of genuine credentials do this in a minute and think nothing of it.

## Step two: reach the register yourself

Type the awarding body's address into the browser. Do not follow a link in the candidate's email, and do not use a search result that carries an advertisement label.

Lookalike domains are the commonest failure in the whole procedure, and they work precisely because the person checking is in a hurry and the page looks right.

## Step three: read the status and the dates, not the existence

A register entry has more on it than a name. Read all of it.

**Status.** Active, suspended, withdrawn, lapsed. A withdrawn credential often still appears, which is the point of a register.

**Dates.** When it was awarded and when it expires. A credential that lapsed in March is not a credential in November, whatever the certificate says. Where a body operates a recertification cycle, an expiry date that has never moved across three renewal periods is worth a question.

**Scope.** Which credential, at which level. Bodies with a family of awards often list them in one place, and the difference between two of them can be several years of assessment.

## Step four: confirm the body is the one you think it is

Two failures are common enough to name at category level, and neither involves anybody lying.

The first is a certificate of attendance or completion being read as a certification. Attendance proves presence at a course. Certification proves an assessed outcome against a published standard. Both are legitimate documents doing different jobs, and a training provider issuing the first is not pretending to do the second. The reader supplies the confusion, and the two are told apart in [credential or certificate of attendance](https://credentialfinder.org/credential-or-certificate-of-attendance).

The second is a credential whose title closely resembles a better-known one in the same field. Read the awarding body's name on the record, not the abbreviation on the CV.

## What a positive verification does not tell you

It tells you the person met that body's requirements and stands in good standing today. That is worth having, and it is all of it.

It does not tell you what the examination covered, at what depth, or whether the subject matches the role you are recruiting for. Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment. Chartered routes in surveying and engineering assess professional competence and standing over a period. Each examines its own subject thoroughly, and a register entry tells you which of them you are looking at and nothing about the fit.

For fit, read the syllabus. Verification and scope are different questions and one does not substitute for the other.

Nor does verification tell you anything about the body's own machinery — how questions are developed, how the pass standard is set, who hears an appeal. That is what accreditation assesses, and where a rule requires an accredited credential, verification of an unaccredited one will not satisfy it however clean the record.

## Verifying a PCI credential, with the obvious caveat

PCI credentials are checked on the Institute's own register at [the PCI verification page](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/verify), using the holder's reference. That register is PCI's, it covers PCI credentials only, and pointing you to it from a site PCI also operates is exactly the sort of thing that ought to be disclosed rather than left for you to discover.

The independent check on PCI is not the register. It is the published Body of Knowledge, readable in full before anyone pays, and the 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements that the scheme holds itself to.

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

## If you are the one being verified

Put the exact title, the awarding body's full name and your holder reference in the same line on your CV, and keep the reference somewhere you can find it in under a minute. Track your own expiry date rather than waiting to be told. And if you hold a certificate of attendance, describe it as one; it is a real thing that a real person can assess, and calling it a certification is the only way to make it look worse than it is.

## FAQ

**Is a verification email from the awarding body good enough?**
It is reasonable evidence when it comes from an address on the body's own domain and you initiated the exchange. It is weak when the candidate forwards it to you, because a forwarded message is another document you have been handed. Where a public register exists, use it: it is faster, and it is current at the moment you look.

**What if the awarding body has no public register?**
Ask why, and treat the answer as information about the body rather than about the candidate. Some smaller schemes verify on written request for good reasons, including data protection choices made in the candidate's favour. What should concern you is a body that cannot confirm status at all, because then nobody can tell you whether the credential is still live.

**Does an expired credential mean the person cannot do the work?**
No, and it is worth separating the two. Someone may have let a credential lapse while doing the work at a high standard for a decade. What an expiry does mean is that the body no longer stands behind the holder, so any assurance you were taking from the credential has ended. Assess the person on their record instead, and be clear that is what you are doing. A renewal cycle keeps that assurance current; it does not retest a career.

**How much of this applies to a small in-house hiring decision?**
The whole of steps one to three, and they take about four minutes. The reason to do them on a routine hire is not suspicion — it is that the four minutes are cheapest before the offer, and a verification done after a start date is an awkward conversation you can simply avoid having.

**Can I rely on a professional network profile?**
Treat it as a claim in a nicer typeface. Some networks display credentials issued through an integration with the awarding body, which is stronger, but the display rarely shows current status and almost never shows suspension. Use it to get the exact title, then go to the register.
