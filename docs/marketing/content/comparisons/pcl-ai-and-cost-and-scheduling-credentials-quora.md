# Cost and scheduling credentials: where PCL-AI sits

*Quora answer. Question it answers: "I do cost control and planning on capital projects. Which
kind of certification is actually worth taking?" Answer completely, then one link at the end.*

---

Straight disclosure first, because it changes how you should read this: I work for Project
Controls Institute Global, which operates credentialfinder.org and awards the PCL-AI credential
I mention at the end. The answer is written to be useful whether or not you ever look at ours.

Start by working out which of two questions you are trying to answer, because the categories
split along that line.

**The first question is method.** How is an estimate built and classed? How does a network
behave when the logic changes? How is progress measured against a rule of credit, and how is a
forecast produced and defended? Cost and scheduling credentials examine exactly this, in depth,
and they are built for it. If your day is estimating or planning, that category is the right
answer and nothing else substitutes for it.

**The second question is the handover.** It shows up every month-end and almost nobody is
examined on it.

An example you can check in your head. A pipeline package: 1,000 metres at £1,000 a metre,
budget £1.0m. The planner cuts the schedule on the 25th, with 800 metres in the ground, so
earned value is £800k. Finance cuts the ledger on the 30th, with £950k booked, because five more
days of work went in.

Put them side by side and CPI is 800 ÷ 950 = 0.84, so the estimate at completion is 1,000 ÷ 0.84
= £1.19m. A £190k overrun appears in the pack.

Recut both sides to the 30th: 950 metres in the ground, £950k earned against £950k spent, CPI
1.00, estimate at completion £1.0m. Nothing on the site changed. A date changed.

Change the earning rule and the same package moves again. Forty valves at £10,000 each, twenty
delivered and none installed: a 0/100 rule earns £0, a 50/50 rule earns £100k. Same steel, same
day, £100k of difference in the reported position.

Neither of those is a scheduling question or a cost engineering question. They sit at the join
between a control cycle and a reporting cycle, and that join is outside the scope of the cost
and scheduling category — as it is for every credential in that category. That is a design
boundary, not a shortcoming.

So: if your work is squarely estimating or planning, take a cost and scheduling credential. If
your employer mandates a particular certification, take that one, because a mandate is a
business requirement rather than a scope decision. If you need an accredited credential now, go
to a body that already holds accreditation.

If the argument you keep losing is at the join — the forecast finance restates, the accrual
nobody flagged, the earning rule the auditor queries — then look at a syllabus that examines
both sides. PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) covers 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas
across the control cycle and the reporting handover.

One caveat that matters. PCI is a new, independent certifying body: it is not accredited by
ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body and does not claim to be, though
the scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. The Body of Knowledge is
published free and in full, so judge it on the syllabus rather than on my say-so.
