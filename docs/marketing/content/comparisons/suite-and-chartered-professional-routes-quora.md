# Should I go chartered or take a certification first?

If chartered standing is asked for anywhere in your market — by a client, a contract, or your employer's grade structure — go chartered, and treat everything else as optional until that is done. No examined credential substitutes for it. That includes the one I will disclose an interest in at the end.

The two assessments answer different questions, which is why the choice is usually made for you.

**A chartered route in surveying or engineering assesses practised competence.** You accumulate responsible experience, map it to a published competence framework, assemble evidence that the work was genuinely yours, write it up and defend it in front of assessors who have done similar work. It looks backwards over years. It carries a code of conduct and a duty to practise within your competence. The question behind it is whether a professional peer would trust your judgement on comparable work tomorrow.

**An examination assesses a declared scope.** A published syllabus, the same paper for every candidate, marked against published objectives, on a date you choose. Its strength is precision about what was tested. Its limit is obvious and worth stating: a paper cannot tell anyone whether you ran a difficult job well for six years.

Neither is a longer version of the other, and people who treat them as interchangeable find out in an interview.

Where an examined credential does earn its place is in material your competence framework was never built to cover. The clearest example I know is the join between measurement and money.

Take a fixed-price contract at £24.0m with total expected cost £20.0m, so expected margin £4.0m. Costs incurred to date are £11.6m. On a cost-to-cost measure that is 11.6 ÷ 20.0 = 58% complete, so revenue is 0.58 × 24.0 = £13.92m and margin recognised to date is £2.32m.

The certified valuation for the same month is £12.60m. Retention at 5% withholds £0.63m, leaving £11.97m payable.

Three numbers for one month: £13.92m recognised, £12.60m certified, £11.97m collectible. The £1.32m between the first two is a contract asset — work measured into revenue that is not yet an unconditional right to payment. Every number is right in its own frame, and somebody has to explain the gaps in one sentence a director can act on.

A measurement discipline examines measurement thoroughly. A finance qualification examines recognition, measurement and disclosure thoroughly. The gap between them is where the surprises live.
