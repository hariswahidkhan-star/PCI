# Accountancy and finance qualifications: where PFL-AI sits

If your work is an entity's financial reporting — preparing or signing accounts, financial control across a business, a route into practice — an accountancy or finance qualification is the right thing to sit, and nothing here argues otherwise. If your work is one contract at a time, and the argument you keep having is about a forecast, a cut-off or an accrual, the PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI) is aimed at you.

## The two categories on scope axes

| Axis | Accountancy and finance qualifications | PFL-AI |
|---|---|---|
| Primary subject | Recognition, measurement, disclosure, financial analysis and control | Where a project's delivery data becomes financial data |
| Unit of analysis | The entity and its transactions | The contract and the control account |
| Progress data | Received as an input, then assessed | Examined at source: how it was measured, on which rule, with what evidence |
| The forecast | Assessed for reasonableness and for what it forces in the accounts | Built, challenged and traced through to revenue and margin |
| Cut-off | A ledger discipline | A two-calendar problem between the site and the ledger |
| AI in the reporting chain | Outside the category's scope | An examined subject: what a model may propose, who decides, what record survives |
| Accreditation status | Established routes, many of them accredited | New, independent, not accredited |

The last row is the one a candidate should read twice, and it is in the table because leaving it out would make the rest of the table less trustworthy.

## What accountancy and finance qualifications examine

They examine when revenue may be recognised, how an asset, a liability or a provision is measured, what has to be disclosed, and how financial control operates across an organisation.

That is a large and demanding subject, and these qualifications go a long way into it. A candidate finishes able to hold a position on recognition and defend it against a standard.

What sits outside that scope, by design, is the machinery that produced the project numbers arriving at the ledger: which rule of credit turned work into earned value, whether a quantity was surveyed before or after the ledger closed, and whether an accrual has a goods received note behind it or last month's figure repeated.

## What cost and scheduling credentials examine

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning, schedule analysis and the economics of an asset over its life. They too go a long way into their subject, and a good holder can build a forecast from the bottom up and defend every line of it.

Recognition, disclosure and the point at which an expected overrun becomes an accounting event sit outside that scope, for the same honest reason: a syllabus that covered everything would cover nothing properly.

## Where the two meet, worked

A fixed-price contract of **£18.0m**. Expected cost at completion **£16.2m**, so an expected margin of **£1.8m**. Costs incurred to date **£7.29m**. Progress is measured on a cost-to-cost basis.

**Position one.** Progress = 7.29 ÷ 16.2 = **45.00%**. Cumulative revenue = 0.45 × 18.0 = **£8.10m**. Cost recognised £7.29m. Cumulative margin **£0.81m**, which is 45% of £1.8m — the cross-check ties.

**Position two.** A rate error is found in the remaining scope and the estimate at completion moves to **£18.9m**. Nothing has been spent that was not spent before.

Expected costs now exceed expected consideration: 18.9 − 18.0 = **£0.9m of expected loss**. Where a contract becomes loss-making, the loss is recognised in full in the period it becomes expected, not spread across the months remaining.

Cumulative result moves from **+£0.81m to −£0.90m**. That is a **£1.71m swing in one month**, caused by a forecast movement of £2.7m and no cash at all.

The cross-check is simple enough to do in your head: once the whole expected loss is taken, the cumulative result on the contract is exactly the expected loss, whatever the percentage complete says.

## Why that is a syllabus problem rather than a competence problem

The cost engineer in that example did good work. Finding a rate error and putting it in the forecast is the job.

The accountant did good work too. Applying the loss test when the forecast crossed the price is also the job.

What went wrong, in every version of this I have watched, is the four weeks between the two. The forecast moved in a cost tool, the finance team saw it at close, and the question "does this cross the contract price" belonged to nobody until it was late. Neither category of credential is examined on that handover, because it is not either one's subject.

## The accreditation position, stated plainly

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

If you need an accredited credential — because a client requires one, because a regulator requires one, or because your own risk appetite requires one — that is a complete answer, and it points away from PCI today.

## How to decide

**Take the accountancy or finance route** if you will prepare or sign statutory accounts, if you want the option of practice, if you need a qualification your finance function already recognises, or if accreditation is a condition rather than a preference.

**Sit PFL-AI** if you own a project cost report that has to survive month-end and a hard question afterwards, if you are the person who explains to finance why the forecast moved, or if you have already been the only one in the room who could read both a cost report and a revenue schedule.

**Do both, in either order,** if your career is heading toward commercial or programme finance in capital delivery. They do not compete. One examines the entity's reporting; the other examines the contract-level chain that feeds it.

PFL-AI covers 16 domains and 61 knowledge areas, and the [Body of Knowledge for the PFL-AI credential](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/certifications/pfl-ai) is published in full before anyone pays anything, which is the only sensible way to judge whether a scope matches your work.

## FAQ

**Does PFL-AI make me an accountant?**
No, and it does not claim to. It does not confer any right to prepare, audit or sign statutory accounts, and it is not a route to a practising qualification. It examines the chain between project delivery data and financial reporting, which is a narrower and more specific subject.

**I already hold an accountancy qualification. Is there anything here for me?**
Possibly, if you work on contracts. The parts most holders tell us they were never examined on are rules of credit, cut-off between site survey and ledger close, accrual evidence, and how a schedule change becomes a cost forecast. If you already have those, you do not need us.

**Is PFL-AI accredited?**
No. PCI is a new, independent certifying body, not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. The syllabus is published so that you can judge it directly rather than judging a claim about it.

**Do I need to understand double entry before sitting it?**
You need to be able to read a cost report and follow where its numbers go. The Body of Knowledge sets out the assumed starting point explicitly, which is why it is worth reading before you commit anything.

**My employer names a specific certification. What then?**
Sit the one your employer names. A mandate is not a comparison, and no credential is worth arguing with a procurement condition over. Nothing published here ranks credentials by employer preference, because that is not a claim anyone can honestly make.
