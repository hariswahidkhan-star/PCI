# Choosing a credential as a cost engineer

*LinkedIn post. No link in the body — it goes in the first comment.*

---

A £3.0m exposure held by £0.6m of contingency, and nobody in the room said it out loud.

A sanction estimate of £20.0m, issued with a stated accuracy of ±15%.

The band = 20.0 × 0.15 = ±£3.0m. So the honest statement is a range: £17.0m to £23.0m.

The contingency approved alongside it is £0.6m. Three per cent.

Both are defensible alone. Together they say the organisation holds £0.6m against a stated upside exposure of £3.0m — a risk appetite set by arithmetic rather than by anybody deciding it.

Now add escalation. £8.0m of spend remains, indexation at 4%: 8.0 × 0.04 = £0.32m. More than half the contingency before a single risk event. Draw it down and £0.28m is left against everything else.

None of that is an estimating error. It is what happens when a range, a contingency and an escalation allowance are set in three conversations and never put on one page.

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning, schedule analysis and the economics of an asset's life. That is your subject, examined in depth and by design.

The established project controls certifications examine measurement, progress, forecasting and control of delivery: the frame around your estimate once delivery starts. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement and disclosure, where your out-turn estimate lands.

The reconciliation between those three numbers belongs to none of them in particular, which is why it is so rarely challenged.

If you estimate for a living, take the depth of a cost-focused credential. If your grading matrix names one, take that one. Neither answer is ours.
