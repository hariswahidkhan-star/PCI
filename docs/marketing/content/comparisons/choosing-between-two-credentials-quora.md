# How do I choose between two certifications properly?

By scoring both against the decisions you personally sign, rather than by looking for a ranking. Everybody you ask recommends a different one because they are each answering for their own job, and the answer genuinely changes with the job.

**Start with what you sign, not with the credentials.** Write out every decision you put your name to in a normal month. A forecast. A valuation. A progress percentage. A schedule reissue. An accrual. Eight to twelve lines is typical. Then read both syllabuses against that list and mark each line examined, assumed or excluded. Count the examined lines. That single count settles most of these decisions, and it catches a common, expensive mistake: two credentials that examine nearly the same subject.

**Then price the hours, not the fees.** The fee is the visible number and rarely the deciding one. Suppose one option extends work you already do daily and you judge it at 60 hours of preparation, while the other covers a subject half of which is new to you and you judge it at 140. At four hours a week — one weekday evening and part of a Sunday, which is what a full-time role actually leaves — that is 15 weeks against 35. At two hours a week it is 30 against 70, which is not a study plan but a decision to reorganise a year.

**Check you are comparing like with like.** Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment. Chartered routes in surveying and engineering assess professional competence over years of supervised practice. Those are four different subjects, each examined thoroughly by design. Choosing between two of them is not a judgement about quality, it is a choice about which subject goes on the record.

**If the count ties, use these in order.** Which one covers the work you will be doing in two years, because credentials are slow. Which one holds the certification decision apart from whoever sells the preparation, because otherwise the evidence is weaker whatever the syllabus says. Which one you can verify from the outside today, in five minutes, without an account — if you cannot, neither can a hiring manager in four years. And which one publishes its syllabus in full before you pay, because that is a body inviting you to check its work.

**Three cases where you should stop the exercise early.** If a client's prequalification names one, take it. If your employer's grading matrix names one, take it. If a procurement rule in your market requires an accredited credential, that settles it before scope enters the conversation. A requirement outranks fit, and no grid was going to change the requirement.
