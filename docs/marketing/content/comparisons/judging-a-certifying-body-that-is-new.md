# How to judge a certifying body that is new

How to judge a certifying body with no track record: start from the three things it can show you today — what it publishes before you pay, which decisions it has separated from each other, and what it openly refuses to claim. A body that tells you what it cannot yet claim is telling you the truth about what it can.

> **Who publishes this page.** credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute
> Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here — a body that is
> itself new. Read this page as an argument you are entitled to turn back on its author.

## The seven checks

| Check | What good looks like | How to verify it today |
|---|---|---|
| The syllabus | Published in full, free, before payment | Download it and read the exclusions |
| The pass standard | A written policy on how the standard is set | Ask; a considered answer arrives quickly |
| Separation | Preparation does not decide the result | Ask who marks, and who sets the standard |
| The appeal route | Published grounds, timescale and decision-maker | Read it before you need it |
| The record | A holder's result is verifiable by a third party | Try to verify a credential yourself |
| Renewal | A stated cycle and a stated requirement | Read what it will ask of you in three years |
| The refusals | No accreditation, recognition or outcome claimed that has not happened | Look for the claims that are absent |

The last row is the one that separates a serious new body from a confident one. Everything a new organisation says about itself is easy. What it declines to say is informative.

## Why the refusals matter more than the promises

A body at founding stage has no pass rates worth quoting, no holder population, no employer survey and no salary data. Any of those figures, published in year one, has been manufactured, because there is nothing to compute them from.

So read for absence. A new body that publishes no pass rate, claims no recognition it has not been granted, and states plainly that it is not accredited has made itself checkable. One that arrives with a full set of impressive numbers has told you either that it is older than it looks or that the numbers are decoration.

The same test applies to language. "Globally recognised" is a claim about other people's behaviour that nobody can evidence in year one. "Endorsed by industry" without a named, verifiable endorsement is the same claim wearing a coat.

## What you can actually check before you pay

Three artefacts, and all three are free to inspect.

The syllabus, read for scope, depth and exclusions rather than for topic count. The pass-standard policy, which tells you whether the line is set against the standard or against the cohort — a difference that decides whether your result depends on the people who happened to sit alongside you. And the appeal route, which tells you what happens on the worst day.

If those three are published and coherent, a new body has given you enough to make a judgement. If they are not, the age of the organisation is not the problem.

Verification is the fourth artefact and the one candidates forget. A credential is only as good as a stranger's ability to confirm it in thirty seconds, years later, without your help. Try it before you enrol: [verify a credential the way an employer would](https://credentialfinder.org/how-to-verify-a-credential) and see what the record actually returns.

## What a new body cannot borrow

Longevity does one thing no amount of publishing can substitute for: it demonstrates that the organisation was still there when the questions arrived. An established body has answered appeals, revised a syllabus under pressure and survived a bad cohort. A new one has done none of that, and cannot claim to have.

That is a real difference and it should sit in your decision. It is not a moral point about anybody. It is a fact about elapsed time, and the only cure is elapsed time.

What a new body can do is design for the questions in advance and publish the design. Read the appeal policy as evidence of that. A body that has thought carefully about the day a candidate disputes a result has thought about the thing that will eventually test it, and [what an appeal process should look like](https://credentialfinder.org/what-an-appeal-process-should-look-like) is a short enough document to judge in ten minutes.

## When a new body is the wrong choice

Say this plainly, because a comparison the publisher always wins is an advertisement.

If you need an accredited credential — because a client's prequalification requires one, because a framework agreement lists it, because your employer's policy is written that way, or because a regulated role demands it — take the accredited route. No scope argument beats a procurement condition, and you will lose a year finding that out.

If your next promotion depends on a specific certification named in an internal grading matrix, take the named one. If you want assessed chartered standing, the chartered routes in surveying and engineering assess professional competence over years of supervised practice, and no examination is a substitute for that assessment.

A new body is worth considering when the scope of its examination matches decisions you are already accountable for, and when you can read enough of it in advance to make that judgement yourself. Those cases are real, and they are narrower than any founder would like.

## Frequently asked questions

**How old does a certifying body need to be before it is worth holding?**
There is no threshold, because age is a proxy for things you can inspect directly: a published standard, a separated decision, a working appeal route and a verifiable record. Inspect those. Then treat elapsed time as one input among several rather than the whole judgement.

**Is an unaccredited credential a red flag?**
Not on its own. Accreditation is third-party assurance that a certification process meets a standard, which is valuable and takes years to obtain. Its absence tells you the assurance has not happened yet, so you do the reading yourself. A body that pretends otherwise is the red flag.

**What if the new body disappears?**
Ask what survives it. A published syllabus, an examination record held so a third party can confirm it, and any escrow or successor arrangement the body has committed to. Ask the question directly before you enrol; the answer, or the silence, is informative either way.

**Can I ask a certifying body these questions directly?**
Yes, and how it answers is part of the evidence. Written questions, written answers, kept. The full set is in [ten questions to ask any certifying body](https://credentialfinder.org/questions-to-ask-a-certifying-body), and any body that treats them as hostile has answered the important one.

**Does a big syllabus mean a serious body?**
No. It means a lot has been written down. Seriousness shows in the boundary statements, the pass-standard policy and the appeal route, which are short documents and much harder to write honestly than a long topic list.
