# Ten questions, and what a good answer actually sounds like

*LinkedIn document post — 13 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

A £3.7m move in a delivery forecast moved the reported number by £1.93m, and nobody in the chain had been examined on the crossing.
Thirteen slides on the ten questions to ask a certifying body, and what a weak answer sounds like.

Disclosure before you read: credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials, so we are one of the bodies you would be asking.

---

**Slide 1 — The ten questions**

1. Can I read the whole syllabus before I pay anything?
2. Who writes the examination, and are they separate from whoever sells the preparation?
3. How was the pass mark set, and by whom?
4. What will the certificate say I was examined on?
5. Can a third party verify it without going through me?
6. Who hears an appeal, and are they independent of the people who marked me?
7. What does recertification actually check?
8. Are you accredited, by whom, to what standard, and if not, do you say so plainly?
9. What will you not claim?
10. If you closed tomorrow, where would the record of my examination live?

**Slide 2 — Why question 4 decides the most**

Scope is what you are buying. Every category examines its own subject thoroughly and is built to, and the boundaries between them are design decisions rather than shortcomings.

The risk is not a bad credential. It is a good credential answering a question your job does not ask.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic behind question 4**

Fixed-price contract **£60.0m**. Budget at completion **£48.0m**. Cost incurred to date **£21.6m**. Control transfers over time, progress measured cost-to-cost.

**Forecast held at £48.0m:**
Progress = 21.6 ÷ 48.0 = **45.00%**
Revenue = 0.4500 × 60.0 = **£27.00m**

**Forecast moved to £51.7m:**
Progress = 21.6 ÷ 51.7 = **41.78%**
Revenue = 0.4178 × 60.0 = **£25.07m**

A **£3.7m** move in a delivery forecast moves the reported number by **£1.93m**. A planner made the move. A financial controller explains it. Ask which side your credential examines.

**Slide 4 — Questions 1 and 2: what you can see before you pay**

**Good:** a link to the document the examination is written from, and a stated separation between item writers and whoever sells preparation.

**Weak:** the syllabus arrives on enrolment, and the same team teaches, writes and decides. Alignment is the problem, not the reassurance.

**Slide 5 — Question 3: how the pass mark was set**

**Good:** a method and a panel. A criterion-referenced cut, set by subject experts judging what a just-competent candidate should manage, fixed before the sitting.

**Weak:** a percentage with nothing attached. Seventy per cent of what? A cut score means nothing without the difficulty of the paper beside it.

**Slide 6 — Question 5: can somebody else check it**

**Good:** a register a hiring manager can query with an ID, returning a state — active, expired, suspended, revoked — with expiry computed at the moment of the query.

**Weak:** a downloadable certificate. A document is a picture of a claim.

**Slide 7 — Question 6: who hears an appeal**

**Good:** a published procedure with named stages, a time limit, and a decision-maker who took no part in the original decision.

**Weak:** appeals handled case by case. Case by case means there is no rule, and a process invented after your result is shaped by your result.

**Slide 8 — Question 7: what recertification checks**

**Good:** a stated purpose — that the holder still practises and has kept up with what changed — plus what evidence counts and what happens if you cannot produce it.

**Weak:** a renewal fee with a form attached. That is a subscription, and the name does not change it.

**Slide 9 — Question 8: accreditation, answered here**

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

**Weak** is any sentence engineered to sound like accreditation without being it. If you cannot tell from the wording whether somebody else assessed the body, that is the answer.

**Slide 10 — Question 9: what a body will not claim**

No promised salary. No promised role. No employer-preference claim. No published pass rates. No holder counts.

Stated as a position rather than left as a gap. A body that volunteers what it cannot yet prove is telling you something about how it will treat your result.

**Slide 11 — Scope, at category level**

| Category | What it sets out to examine |
|---|---|
| Cost and scheduling credentials | Estimating, cost control, planning, schedule analysis |
| Accountancy and finance qualifications | Recognition, measurement, disclosure, financial control |
| Audit credentials | Governance, risk, control and the assurance process |
| Project management certifications | Managing projects across people, process and the business environment |

Read your job description next to this table before you read anybody's brochure.

**Slide 12 — When the answer points elsewhere**

If your employer mandates a specific certification, question 8 settles it before the other nine. Take the mandated one.

If a client's procurement rule requires an accredited credential, an unaccredited scheme can answer nine questions well and still not meet a requirement written into a contract.

If your work sits entirely on one side of the finance and delivery line, a credential built for that side is the better buy.

**Slide 13 — Ask us the ten**

PCI publishes the Body of Knowledge in full before anyone pays, publishes its certification decision and appeals policies, and publishes no pass rates, salary outcomes or holder numbers.

Behind the syllabus sit 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements, and 92 sector case studies across the three volumes. The PCI Standards are certification requirements established by the Institute. They are not law.
