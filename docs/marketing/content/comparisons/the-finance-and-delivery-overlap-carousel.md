# The join between a forecast and the reported accounts

*LinkedIn document post — 12 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

Reported margin fell £0.57m in a month when nothing changed on site.
Twelve slides on the join between a project forecast and the reported accounts, with the arithmetic on slide 3 so you can check it yourself.

Disclosure before you read: credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials. PCI is new and not accredited. Read the syllabus and judge it.

---

**Slide 1 — Where the money goes**

A forecast is built on the delivery side. A reported number is produced on the finance side. Between them sits a handshake: the cut-off, the earning rule, the accrual, the change.

Both sides are examined thoroughly by their own credentials. The handshake is examined by neither, and that is where the surprises come from.

**Slide 2 — The two sides, and the middle**

| | Delivery side | The join | Finance side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examined on | Estimating, planning, measurement | — | Recognition, measurement, disclosure |
| Owns | Progress and the outturn estimate | Cut-off, earning rule, accrual | Revenue, margin, provision |
| Assumes | Somebody books it correctly | — | The delivery data is sound |
| Fails quietly when | The estimate is stale | Nobody owns it | Inputs were never questioned |

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic, checkable in your head**

Contract price **£26.0m**. Costs incurred **£9.0m**. Estimated total cost **£24.0m**.

Progress = 9.0 ÷ 24.0 = **37.5%**
Revenue = 0.375 × 26.0 = **£9.75m**
Margin = **£0.75m**

The estimate moves to **£25.5m**:

Progress = 9.0 ÷ 25.5 = **35.3%**
Revenue = 0.353 × 26.0 = **£9.18m**
Margin = **£0.18m**

Same site. Same month. **£0.57m** less margin.

**Slide 4 — And if the estimate keeps moving**

If estimated total cost reaches **£26.6m** against a price of **£26.0m**, the contract is expected to lose **£0.6m**.

That loss is recognised as soon as it is known, not spread over the remaining months. Which is why a forecast revision can land as a single large movement in a reported result.

**Slide 5 — A second artefact, same mechanism**

Earned value **£4.20m**. Cost report shows **£4.30m**. CPI = 4.20 ÷ 4.30 = **0.977**.

Subcontractor work of **£0.28m** was done and not invoiced. Accrued properly: cost **£4.58m**, CPI = 4.20 ÷ 4.58 = **0.917**.

On a **£12.0m** budget at completion: **£12.28m** forecast against **£13.09m**.

**£0.81m**, produced by an invoice that had not arrived.

**Slide 6 — Artefact one: the cut-off calendar**

Progress measured to one date. Costs booked to another.

The index that comes out moves for reasons that are not performance, and the forecast built on it inherits the movement. Aligning the two dates is administrative work that nobody's syllabus makes anybody's responsibility.

**Slide 7 — Artefact two: the accrual list**

Work done and not yet invoiced.

Left out, the cost report flatters itself, the index looks better than the job is, and every forecast built on it carries the flattery forward. Chasing accruals is unglamorous and it is the single cheapest control in this list.

**Slide 8 — Artefact three: the earning rule**

Take an activity budgeted at **£600k**, started and not finished.

A **50/50** rule earns **£300k** this month. A **0/100** rule earns **£0**.

Against actual cost of **£4.30m**, that is earned value of **£4.20m** or **£3.90m**: CPI of **0.977** or **0.907**, and a forecast at completion of **£12.28m** or **£13.23m** on a **£12.0m** budget.

Same work. Different convention.

**Slide 9 — Artefact four: the change register**

Instructed and not priced. Priced and not approved. Approved and not in the budget.

Three states, three different treatments, and the distance between them is where a claim is won or lost. A register that does not record the state records nothing useful.

**Slide 10 — What each category is examined on**

Cost and scheduling credentials: estimating, cost control, planning, schedule analysis.

Accountancy and finance qualifications: recognition, measurement, disclosure, financial control.

Audit credentials: governance, risk, control and the assurance process.

Project management certifications: managing projects across people, process and the business environment.

Four subjects, each examined thoroughly and built to be. The join belongs to all four and sits at the centre of none.

**Slide 12 — When the overlap is not your problem**

If your work stops at the programme — build it, status it, analyse it, hand it on — the join is somebody else's week. A scheduling-focused credential examines what you actually do, and paying for a large finance component is buying reading rather than evidence.

The same in reverse for a finance professional who never sees a progress claim.

The overlap matters when you personally sign something that crosses it.
