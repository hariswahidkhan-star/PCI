# Pass marks: how the number gets chosen, and by whom

*LinkedIn document post — 12 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

Two candidates score 68. One passes, one fails, on the same paper with the same knowledge.
Twelve slides on how a pass mark is actually set, and why the percentage on its own tells you nothing.

Disclosure before you read: credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials. PCI is new and not accredited. Read the syllabus and judge it.

---

**Slide 1 — Seventy per cent of what?**

A cut score is a property of the paper it was derived from. Without the paper and the method beside it, the percentage carries no information at all.

Candidates compare pass marks between schemes because the numbers look comparable. They are not.

**Slide 2 — Two families, two different jobs**

| | Norm-referenced | Criterion-referenced |
|---|---|---|
| What fixes the cut | A share of the cohort | A judgement about competence |
| Your result depends on | Everyone who sat with you | You and the standard |
| Known when | After the sitting | Before the sitting |
| Built for | Ranking a field for limited places | Deciding whether somebody meets a standard |

Both are legitimate. A certification decision is the second job.

**Slide 3 — The norm-referenced trap, in numbers**

A scheme passes the **top 30%**.

**Sitting A:** the 70th percentile score is **66**. You score **68** → **pass**.
**Sitting B:** a stronger cohort puts the 70th percentile at **71**. You score **68** → **fail**.

Same paper. Same knowledge. Different month.

That number measured the room you sat in, not what you can do.

**Slide 4 — The panel method, in numbers**

Start with a written description of the just-competent candidate: what they can do unsupervised, what they must not get wrong.

A panel of subject experts then judges, item by item, the share of just-competent candidates who would answer correctly. A hard item might draw **0.35**, a straightforward one **0.85**.

Average across the paper. A mean of **0.62** gives a cut of **62% of the marks available**.

Nothing in that process asks how many candidates will pass. That is the point of it.

**Slide 5 — Why 62 can be a higher standard than 70**

Put the same panel in front of a harder paper and the judgements fall, so the cut falls with them.

A cut of 62 on a hard paper can therefore be a stiffer test than 70 on an easy one. Ranking two credentials by their published percentages compares nothing, with an air of precision.

**Slide 6 — Equating, or the version you happened to draw**

Where a scheme runs several forms of a paper, they will not be identically difficult however carefully they are built.

Equating puts scores from different forms on the same scale, so nobody is failed for drawing the harder one. On one annual paper it is a non-issue. On continuous testing from a large item bank it is central.

**Slide 7 — The border always exists**

Measurement carries error. A score is an estimate of standing, not a reading off a ruler.

Suppose the standard error is **3 marks**. A **61** against a cut of **62** sits inside it, and so does a **63**.

That is a property of every examination ever set, including the ones you already hold.

**Slide 8 — The rule that should already exist**

What separates a well-run scheme from a badly run one is not whether the border is fuzzy.

It is whether the rule for the border was published **before** you sat. A fixed rule applied to everybody is defensible. A judgement taken about your paper after somebody saw your name is not.

**Slide 9 — Four questions to ask any certifying body**

1. Was the cut set before or after the results were seen?
2. Is it re-derived when the paper changes materially?
3. Where several forms exist, are they equated?
4. Who sat on the panel, and were they independent of whoever sells the preparation?

A body that moves the cut after seeing results, with no published rule, is managing its pass rate rather than its standard.

**Slide 10 — What a pass rate would tell you**

Less than you would hope. A pass rate describes the candidates who happened to sit, not the standard they were held to.

A high one can mean a well-prepared cohort or a soft cut. A low one can mean a hard paper or a poorly targeted entry route. Neither number can be read without the other information, which is why this Institute publishes no pass rates at all.

**Slide 11 — What this body publishes, and what it does not**

Published: the Body of Knowledge in full, before anybody pays. How examinations are developed. How the certification decision is made.

Not published: pass rates, examination weightings, salary outcomes, holder counts. The 40/40/20 proportions describe the Body of Knowledge, not any paper.

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles.

**Slide 12 — When this is not the deciding question**

If your employer mandates a specific certification, how its cut was derived is interesting and not decisive. Take the mandated one.

If a client's procurement rule requires an accredited credential, an excellent standard-setting process at an unaccredited body still will not meet it. Settle that first.

Then come back to this list, because the mechanics matter for the rest of your career.
