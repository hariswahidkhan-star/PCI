# What a syllabus should tell you, and where depth hides

What a syllabus should tell you is four things: what is examined, at what depth, in what proportion, and what it assumes you already know. A document that gives you the first and none of the other three is a topic list, and a topic list cannot tell you whether the examination fits the decisions you actually sign.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. Run every check below against PCI's own documents as readily as anyone else's.

## Six checks, and what a missing one costs

| What to look for | What it lets you decide | What its absence costs you |
|---|---|---|
| Scope, written as boundaries | Whether the decisions you sign sit inside it | You discover the exclusions on the day |
| Depth, written with verbs | Whether you recall it or defend it | Preparation time cannot be estimated |
| Proportion across the subject | Where your study hours should go | You revise evenly and misspend half of them |
| Assumed prior knowledge | Whether to sit now or get two more years first | The gate stays hidden until you are in the room |
| Sources it draws on | What the assessment treats as correct | House method and discipline become indistinguishable |
| Explicit exclusions | What somebody else is expected to cover | Every boundary turns into an argument |

An evening is enough to run all six against two documents. It is the cheapest hour of due diligence available to a candidate, and almost nobody spends it.

## Depth is the check people skip

Scope tells you a subject appears. Depth tells you what you will be asked to do with it, and depth is where preparation time actually goes.

A line reading "cost performance index" could mean anything. A line reading "reconcile committed, accrued and posted cost at a reporting cut-off and justify the treatment" means something specific, and you can price your own study against it.

Here is why the difference is not academic.

At the cut-off, a package has **£2.35m** of supplier invoices posted in the ledger and **£0.64m** of goods and services received but not yet invoiced. Earned value for the same period is **£2.80m**.

- On posted cost alone: 2.80 ÷ 2.35 = **1.191**
- With the accrual included: 2.80 ÷ 2.99 = **0.937**

One report says the package is running about 19 per cent better than plan on cost. The other says it is about 6 per cent worse. Nothing on site changed between them. What changed is a cut-off convention that lives on the finance side of the house and lands on the delivery side's index.

A recall-depth syllabus is satisfied when you can define the index. A judgement-depth syllabus is satisfied when you can say which figure you would put in the board pack, what you would attach to it, and what you would do about the £0.64m next month. Those are different examinations and different study plans, and the syllabus is the only place the difference is written down.

## Assumed prior knowledge is a gate in disguise

Every syllabus assumes something. The useful ones say what.

If a document assumes you have run a month-end, sat opposite a commercial manager on a variation, or defended a forecast to somebody who did not want to hear it, that assumption is doing real work in the examination. Candidates without it rarely fail on topics. They fail on situations.

Where the assumption is written down you can act on it: sit later, or get the reps first. Where it is not, you are guessing, and a wrong guess costs the whole fee.

## Proportion tells you where the subject's centre is

A list of topics implies they all carry the same weight. They almost never do.

Proportion is also the quickest way to see what a credential is really about. Two syllabuses can name the same twenty topics and be entirely different examinations, because one spends most of itself on measurement and the other on reporting.

One caution that applies to every body, this one included. The proportions of a Body of Knowledge and the weighting of an examination are two different statements. A body may publish the first while the second is still an open decision. Ask which one you are reading rather than assuming they are the same number.

## What the categories tell you about themselves

Read enough syllabuses and the categories separate cleanly, on their own terms and in their own words.

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. They are built to examine those thoroughly and they say so plainly.

Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. Their syllabuses assume the delivery data arrives from somewhere else, and they are explicit about that assumption.

Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment. Breadth is the design, and the documents are honest about what breadth costs in depth.

None of that is a shortcoming in any of them. Each category examines its own subject to a standard it publishes. The reason to read the syllabus is to find out which subject you are being examined in, so that the boundary is a decision you made rather than a surprise you received. The £0.64m above sits exactly on one of those boundaries, which is the argument for reading two syllabuses rather than one.

## When the syllabus is not the deciding document

If your employer or a client's pre-qualification names a specific certification, read the syllabus anyway — you should know what you are about to be examined on — but it is not the decision. Take the one that is named and read the rest later.

The same holds where a role is regulated, or where a framework agreement lists an accredited credential as a condition. A scope argument does not beat a procurement requirement, and finding that out late wastes a year of preparation.

## FAQ

**How long should reading a syllabus take?**
An hour for the structure and the exclusions, an evening if you are comparing two. Read the boundary statements first, then the depth verbs, then the assumed prior knowledge. Topic lists are the least informative part of the document and the part most people read first.

**What if the syllabus is behind a paywall?**
Ask why. A body that publishes its standard openly is inviting you to check it. A body that will not show you what it examines until you have paid is asking for trust it has not given you a way to test. Some publish a detailed outline free and the full text on registration, which is a fair middle.

**Does a long syllabus mean a hard examination?**
No. Length measures how much has been written down, not how deeply any of it is assessed. A short syllabus with judgement-level verbs can be a harder paper than a long one built on recall. Read the verbs before the page count.

**How do I know a syllabus is current?**
Look for a version number, a date and a change note, then check whether the sources it draws on have moved since. A document that cannot tell you when it last changed is telling you something about how it is maintained.

**Should I bother if my employer is paying?**
Yes, and for the same reason. The fee is not the cost; your preparation hours are, and the syllabus is what makes those hours estimable. It is also the only place you will find out what the examination assumes you already know.
