# A topic list is not a syllabus, and depth is why

*LinkedIn text post — 1,877 characters. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post body:**

A syllabus that lists topics and nothing else cannot be judged. It can only be admired.

What a syllabus should tell you is four things: what is examined, at what depth, in what proportion, and what it assumes you already know.

Depth is the one people skip, and it is where the preparation hours actually go.

"Cost performance index" on a syllabus line could mean anything. Here is why that matters.

At the cut-off, a package has £2.35m of supplier invoices posted and £0.64m of goods received but not yet invoiced. Earned value for the period is £2.80m.

On posted cost alone: 2.80 ÷ 2.35 = 1.191
With the accrual included: 2.80 ÷ 2.99 = 0.937

One report says the package is running about 19% better than plan on cost. The other says about 6% worse. Nothing on site changed. A cut-off convention that lives on the finance side landed on the delivery side's index.

A recall-depth syllabus is satisfied when you can define the index. A judgement-depth syllabus is satisfied when you can say which figure goes in the board pack and what you do about the £0.64m next month.

Different examinations. Different study plans. The syllabus is the only place that difference is written down.

Two more checks while you have the document open. What does it assume you already know — because candidates rarely fail on topics, they fail on situations. And what does it explicitly exclude, because every unstated boundary becomes an argument later.

If your employer or a client's pre-qualification names a specific certification, take the named one and read the rest for interest. A scope argument does not beat a procurement condition.
