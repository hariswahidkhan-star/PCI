# What a certification appeal process should look like

A real appeal process names the grounds you may appeal on, a deadline, a panel with no involvement in the original decision, a published timetable and a stated range of outcomes. Read it before you book, because after a result it reads very differently. A body with no appeal policy has told you it does not expect to be wrong.

> **Who publishes this page.** credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. Apply the checklist to PCI's own policy as strictly as to anyone else's.

## Four different things that get called an appeal

| Route | What it is for | Who should decide | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Appeal | A decision you believe was reached wrongly | A panel independent of the original decision | Decision upheld, amended, or the assessment repeated at no charge |
| Complaint | Conduct, service or administration | A function separate from the one complained about | Explanation, apology, process change |
| Re-mark or result review | A clerical or marking error is suspected | Assessment staff not involved originally | Result confirmed or corrected |
| Reasonable adjustment request | Access needs before an assessment | Assessment operations, in advance | Adjustment agreed and applied |

Mixing these is the commonest failure. A body that routes everything through one inbox called "feedback" has no process; it has a customer service function.

## What you can appeal, and what you cannot

You can appeal a decision reached through a procedural error: a condition of the assessment not delivered as published, an adjustment agreed and not applied, a scoring or administrative mistake, or a decision inconsistent with the body's own policy.

You cannot usually appeal because you disagree with the standard, because you needed the credential for a job, or because you feel the paper was harder than expected. Those are real experiences and they are not grounds.

A well-drafted policy says both halves out loud. The absence of the second half is a signal: a policy that lists no exclusions has not been written by anyone who has handled an appeal.

## The shape of a timetable worth trusting

Here is the shape to look for. The exact numbers vary between bodies; what matters is that numbers exist and are published before you sit.

A result is issued on **3 May**. The policy allows **20 working days** to lodge an appeal, so the window closes at the end of **31 May**.

Acknowledgement within **5 working days**. Panel convened and decided within **30 working days** of receipt. Written outcome with reasons.

Three things make that timetable meaningful rather than decorative. It is published in advance. It commits the body as well as the candidate. And it says what happens when the body misses its own dates.

## Independence, which is the whole point

The panel must include people with no involvement in the original decision. If the person who made the decision also reviews it, the process is a re-reading rather than an appeal.

Ask two questions of any policy. Who sits on the panel, and what is their relationship to the assessment function? And does the panel have authority to change the decision, or only to recommend?

A panel that can recommend but not decide is an advisory step, and the policy should say so plainly rather than let a candidate assume otherwise. The same discipline that separates preparation from the certification decision applies here: the reviewer must not be the party whose work is under review, a point covered further in [ten questions to ask before you pay](https://credentialfinder.org/questions-to-ask-a-certifying-body).

## Fees, and what happens to them

Some bodies charge to lodge an appeal, which is defensible as a filter against volume. What matters is what happens next.

A fair policy refunds the fee where an appeal is upheld. A policy that keeps the fee whichever way the decision goes is charging you for the right to be right, and it will deter exactly the appeals that would have been justified.

Ask, in writing, whether the fee is refunded on a successful appeal. It is a short question with a short answer, and the answer sorts the policies quickly.

## What the outcome should contain

**Reasons.** Not "your appeal was considered and rejected", but which ground was argued, what evidence was examined and why it did or did not support the ground.

**The decision's effect.** Upheld, dismissed, or partially upheld, and precisely what changes as a result.

**What happens next.** Whether the decision is final, whether an external stage exists, and what records are retained.

**A route out.** Where a body is accredited, an accreditation body has a complaints route about the body's process, which is separate from the assessment decision. Where a body is not accredited, that external step does not exist, and a candidate should know that before sitting rather than after. That gap is part of what accreditation covers, as set out in [what accreditation means, and what it does not prove](https://credentialfinder.org/what-accreditation-means).

## What PCI does not claim

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

Because PCI is not accredited, there is no accreditation body to escalate a process complaint to. That is a genuine limitation of an unaccredited scheme, it applies to PCI as much as to any other unaccredited body, and it is stated here rather than left for a candidate to discover.

What does exist is a published appeal route with grounds and a timetable, decided by people not involved in the original decision, and a criterion-referenced standard that a decision can be tested against. Whether a standard can be tested at all depends on how it was set, which is worked through in [how a pass mark is set](https://credentialfinder.org/how-a-pass-mark-is-set).

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I appeal a result I simply disagree with?**
Only if you can name a ground. Read the policy, identify what went wrong procedurally, and set it out with dates and evidence. An appeal that argues the standard was unfair will be dismissed, and a resit usually gets you where you want to go faster.

**How much detail should I expect about my failed attempt?**
Domain-level feedback is reasonable to expect and is a good sign about the paper's design. Item-level detail generally is not, because published items would have to be retired. If a body offers no feedback at all, ask how a candidate is meant to prepare differently for a resit.

**Can an appeal make my result worse?**
Under most policies, no: the outcome is upheld, amended in your favour, or a repeat assessment. Check the wording rather than assume, because a policy that reserves the right to revise a result downwards is unusual enough that you should know about it first.

**What if the body ignores its own timetable?**
Write once, citing the policy and the dates, and ask for a revised date. Keep the correspondence. Where a body is accredited, its accreditation body handles process complaints; where it is not, your remedy is the body's own escalation route, which is one more reason to read the policy before you book.
