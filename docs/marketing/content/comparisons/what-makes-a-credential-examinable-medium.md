# What makes a credential examinable: four tests

A credential is examinable when every line of its syllabus names something a candidate must be able to do, on an object specific enough that the marker knows what data came with the question, at a stated depth. Two competent markers should reach the same score on the same answer. If they cannot, the line is a reading list entry, not a syllabus entry.

That is the whole test, and it is checkable by anybody, on any document, in an afternoon.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. Apply the four tests below to PCI's published Body of Knowledge as readily as to anybody else's syllabus. A test that only works on other people is not a test.

## The difference, in one table

| | A topic line | An examinable line |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | understands, is familiar with, appreciates | computes, reconciles, classifies, selects and justifies |
| Object | a subject heading | a defined task with defined inputs |
| Depth | unstated | recall, application or analysis, said out loud |
| Marking | depends on the marker | a key the candidate could have read in advance |
| What it produces | a shared vocabulary | an answer that is right or wrong for a stated reason |

Both kinds of line have a use. A topic list orients a reader and tells them what a body considers part of the discipline. It simply cannot carry an examination, and a scheme that builds an examination on top of one is deciding the standard after the fact.

## Test one: an observable verb

Calculate. Reconcile. Classify. Select and justify. Reject and state the reason. Each of these describes something a candidate does that leaves evidence on the page.

Understand, appreciate and be aware of describe an internal state. Nobody can mark an internal state, so in practice the marker substitutes a proxy — usually whether the answer sounds like the textbook — and the standard drifts with whoever is marking that week.

The quick version of this test: highlight every verb in the document. If most of them are internal states, you are holding a curriculum, and the examination that follows it has been designed somewhere you cannot see.

## Test two: a bounded object

"Progress measurement" is a heading. "Selects an earning rule for a partially complete work package and defends the choice against the evidence available" is a task, and you can already picture the question.

The boundary matters because it tells the candidate what arrives with the question. A well-drawn line implies its own data: a budget, a set of quantities, a cut-off date, a contract clause. A line with no boundary means the examiner supplies whatever they like, and the candidate revises everything.

Reading a whole document for boundaries rather than a single line is a different job, and there is a checklist for it in [what a syllabus should tell you](https://credentialfinder.org/what-a-syllabus-should-tell-you).

## Test three: a stated depth

Recall, application and analysis of one topic are three different examinations. Being able to state what a to-complete performance index is, being able to compute one, and being able to say what a value above 1.20 implies about the remaining work are separated by years of practice.

A syllabus that names the topic but not the depth has not made the decision that matters most to a candidate deciding whether they are ready. Where a scheme has levels, the same topic should appear at more than one, deepening each time. If it appears twice in identical words, the second level is padding.

## Test four: a defensible key

Arithmetic marks itself. Judgement does not, and most of the useful questions in this field require judgement.

An examinable scheme says, in advance and in public, what makes a judgement answer right: which factors must appear, which conclusions are defensible on the stated facts, and what an answer must not do. A candidate should be able to read that before paying anything.

## One line, rewritten, with the arithmetic

Here is a line that fails all four tests:

> Understands estimate at completion.

And here is the same subject, examinable:

> Given a budget at completion of £48.0m, earned value of £14.4m and actual cost of £16.0m, computes the estimate at completion on the assumption that current cost performance continues, and states the result.

Work it. The cost performance index is 14.4 ÷ 16.0 = **0.90**. On the assumption stated, the estimate at completion is 48.0 ÷ 0.90 = **£53.33m**.

Now change one clause — remaining work is delivered at the budgeted rate — and the same three numbers give 16.0 + (48.0 − 14.4) = 16.0 + 33.6 = **£49.60m**.

**£3.73m of difference, one dataset, and the only thing that moved was the assumption.** That is why the assumption has to be in the question. The examinable version has exactly one right answer and a candidate can see why. The unexaminable version has as many right answers as it has markers.

This is also the honest reason the four tests matter to a practitioner rather than to a standards committee. The month you have to defend a forecast, nobody asks whether you understand estimate at completion. They ask which assumption you used and why that one.

## What the four tests do not tell you

They do not tell you difficulty. A syllabus can be immaculately examinable and cover material you already know.

They do not tell you standing, market value or whether a particular employer will accept the credential. Those are separate questions and this test says nothing about them.

They do not rank categories. Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process. Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment. Product certifications for scheduling software examine operating a specific tool. Each publishes outcomes in its own subject and each examines that subject thoroughly, because that is what it was built to do. A scope boundary is a design decision, not a shortcoming.

What the tests do tell you is what a credential is undertaking to make you able to do, in words precise enough that you can hold the body to them afterwards.

## Where PCI stands on its own test

PCI publishes [the Body of Knowledge](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/body-of-knowledge) in full before anyone pays: 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas for PCL-AI, 16 and 61 for PFL-AI, 16 and 63 for PML-AI, with 92 sector case studies across the three volumes. The scheme holds itself to 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements. The Body of Knowledge is proportioned 40/40/20 across finance and reporting, project management and governed AI — those are the book's proportions, and they are not an examination weighting, because the examination blueprint is not settled and PCI will not publish one it has not decided.

That last sentence is the sort of thing this piece is asking you to look for in anybody's documentation, including PCI's.

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

## How to decide

Print the syllabus. Highlight the verbs. Pick the three knowledge areas closest to the work you actually do and see whether you could write an examination question from each line. If you can, the body has told you what it will hold you to.

If you cannot, that is not proof of a bad credential. It is a question worth asking the body directly, and how they answer tells you more than the document did.

And if your employer mandates a specific certification, or a tender scores an accredited one, none of this decides anything. Take the credential you are required to hold. Examinability is the right question only when the choice is genuinely yours.

## FAQ

**Is an examinable syllabus the same as a hard examination?**
No. Examinability is about whether an outcome can be marked consistently; difficulty is about how much a candidate has to know to meet it. A precisely written syllabus at an introductory depth is fully examinable and undemanding. The two properties are independent, and confusing them is the commonest error in this whole subject.

**Can I test a syllabus without being an expert in it?**
Largely, yes. The verb test and the depth test need no subject knowledge at all — you are reading grammar, not content. Judging whether the object is bounded well does need some familiarity, so apply that test only to the areas you already work in, and take the rest on the pattern.

**Does a body have to publish its marking key?**
Not in full, and no examination could survive publishing model answers to live questions. What a candidate should be able to see in advance is the standard: which factors a good answer addresses and what would make an answer wrong. Publishing that costs a body nothing and tells a candidate a great deal.

**What if the syllabus is examinable but the subject is the wrong one?**
Then it is the wrong credential, and no amount of drafting quality fixes that. Scope comes first: decide which side of the finance and delivery line your next five years sit on, then test the syllabus of whatever serves that. A beautifully written examination in a subject you will not use is still a year you do not get back.

**How does this apply to a body with no track record?**
It applies harder. A new body cannot lean on a reputation, so the published syllabus is most of the evidence available, and it should be judged strictly — the rest of that method is in [judging a certifying body that is new](https://credentialfinder.org/judging-a-certifying-body-that-is-new). That cuts both ways: a new scheme that publishes examinable outcomes and its own process requirements has put more on the table than a mature one that publishes a topic list.
