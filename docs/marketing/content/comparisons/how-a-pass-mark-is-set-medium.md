# How a pass mark is set, and why that matters to you

A pass mark should be derived before anybody sits, from a panel's judgement about what a just-competent candidate can do, and re-derived whenever the paper changes materially. The percentage that comes out belongs to that paper. It cannot be compared with another scheme's percentage, and quoted alone it tells a candidate almost nothing.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. A body writing about how cut scores should be set is describing a standard it will be held to, so the disclosure belongs at the top rather than in the footer.

## Two ways to fix a cut, and what each is for

| | Norm-referenced | Criterion-referenced |
|---|---|---|
| What fixes the cut | A share of the cohort | A judgement about competence |
| Your result depends on | Everyone who sat with you | You and the standard |
| Can be known | Only after the sitting | Before the sitting |
| Suited to | Ranking a field for limited places | Deciding whether somebody meets a standard |
| Fails badly when | Used to certify competence | The competence description is vague |

Neither is a bad instrument. They answer different questions, and certification is the second question.

## The norm-referenced route, worked

Take a scheme that passes the top 30 per cent of each sitting.

In sitting A the 70th percentile score is 66, so a candidate scoring **68 passes**.

In sitting B a stronger cohort lifts the 70th percentile to 71, so the same candidate with the same **68 fails**.

The number described the room. If a scheme is allocating a fixed number of places that is exactly what you want; if it is certifying that somebody can be trusted with a forecast, it is not.

## The criterion-referenced route, worked

First the scheme writes down the just-competent candidate — the person who should barely pass — in terms of what they can do unsupervised and what they must never get wrong.

Then a panel of subject experts goes through the paper item by item. Each expert estimates the share of just-competent candidates who would answer that item correctly: perhaps 0.35 on a demanding item, 0.85 on a routine one.

Average the estimates across the paper. A mean of **0.62** gives a cut of **62 per cent of the marks available**. Panels normally run a second round after seeing each other's judgements, and the figure shifts a little.

At no point does the method ask how many candidates will pass. That is what makes it a standard rather than a quota.

## Why comparing published percentages is useless

The cut belongs to the paper it came from. Give the same panel a harder paper and the estimates drop, so the cut drops with them.

A cut of **62 on a hard paper can be a stiffer test than 70 on an easy one**. Ranking credentials by their pass marks compares nothing at all, while looking precise enough that people do it anyway.

This is the practical reason to distrust a body that quotes a percentage without a method attached. Seventy per cent of what, judged by whom, against what description of competence?

## The aggregation rule nobody asks about

Two schemes can share a cut score of 62 and behave completely differently, because the cut can be applied in two ways.

**Compensatory.** The total decides. Strength in one area offsets weakness in another, and a candidate can be thin in a domain and still pass.

**Conjunctive.** Each section must be passed in its own right. No compensation, so a single weak domain fails the paper however strong the rest is.

Ask which one applies before you plan your revision, because it changes what "prepared" means. A compensatory scheme rewards breadth with a strong core; a conjunctive one punishes the domain you were going to skim.

## Equating, when there is more than one paper

Where a scheme runs several forms, they will not be identically difficult however carefully they are assembled.

Equating adjusts scores onto a common scale so that a candidate is not failed for drawing the harder form. On a single annual paper it does not arise. On continuous testing from a large item bank it is central, and a scheme that cannot describe how it does it is telling you something.

## The border, and the rule that should predate you

Measurement carries error. A score estimates a candidate's standing rather than reading it off a ruler.

Suppose the standard error of measurement is 3 marks. A **61** against a cut of **62** sits inside it, and so does a **63**. Every examination ever set has this property, including the ones you already hold and the ones you respect most.

What distinguishes a well-run scheme is not the absence of a fuzzy border. It is whether the rule for the border existed, in writing, before you sat. A fixed rule applied to everybody is defensible. A decision taken about your script after somebody saw your name is not, however statistically it is dressed.

## A pass mark is not a pass rate

They get conflated constantly and they answer different questions.

The **pass mark** is the standard: the score at which the scheme says a candidate is competent. The **pass rate** is a fact about the people who happened to sit: what proportion cleared it.

A high pass rate can mean a well-prepared cohort or a soft cut. A low one can mean a hard paper or a poorly targeted entry route. Neither figure is interpretable without the other information, which is why this Institute publishes no pass rates and treats the absence as a position rather than an omission.

## How to use all this when you are choosing

Ask four things of any body, in any field. Was the cut set before or after the results were seen? Is it re-derived when the paper changes materially? Where several forms exist, are they equated? Who sat on the panel, and were they independent of whoever sells the preparation?

Then weigh the answers against the questions that outrank them.

If your employer mandates a specific certification, take the mandated one. How its cut was derived is worth knowing and is not your decision.

If a client's procurement rule or a regulator requires an accredited credential, settle that before anything else. A scrupulous standard-setting process at an unaccredited body will not satisfy a contract clause, and that is a fair reason to choose elsewhere.

And if your work sits wholly on one side of the finance and delivery line, the credential built for that side is the better buy, whatever anybody's cut score is.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I choose the credential with the higher pass mark?**
No. The cut is derived from the difficulty of the paper it belongs to, so a higher percentage often just means an easier paper. Compare what each credential examines and how each derived its cut. The percentage is the least informative number a body publishes about itself.

**Do I have to pass every domain separately?**
It depends on the aggregation rule. A compensatory scheme decides on the total, so strength in one area offsets weakness elsewhere. A conjunctive scheme requires each section to be passed in its own right. Ask before you plan revision, because the answer changes which weak spot actually matters.

**Who should sit on a standard-setting panel?**
Practitioners who do the work being examined, spread across the settings the credential covers, and independent of whoever sells preparation for it. A panel drawn entirely from one employer or one training provider produces a standard shaped by that experience rather than by the discipline.

**Is a computer-marked examination more objective?**
Marking is more consistent, which is not the same thing. The judgement moved earlier in the process, into item writing and the standard-setting panel, and those are the stages worth asking about. Automatic marking removes marker variation; it does not decide what competent means.

**Why will you not tell me your pass mark?**
Because a percentage without its paper and its method invites exactly the false comparison this article is about. A cut score is meaningful published alongside the standard-setting method and the syllabus it applies to. Published as a number in a brochure, it is marketing wearing a decimal point.
