# Eleven slides on reading a syllabus properly

*LinkedIn document post — 11 slides, 1080 × 1350. One idea per slide. Slide 3 carries the arithmetic and is the one people screenshot. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

A syllabus that lists topics and nothing else cannot be judged. It can only be admired.
Same month, same package: 19% ahead on cost or 6% behind, depending on one cut-off convention — slide 3.

Eleven slides on what a syllabus should tell you, why depth is the check people skip, and where the boundary between two categories actually sits.

---

**Slide 1 — Four things, not one**
What is examined. At what depth. In what proportion. And what it assumes you already know. A document that gives you the first and none of the rest is a topic list.

**Slide 2 — Depth is where the hours go**
"Cost performance index" on a syllabus line could mean recall or judgement. Those are different examinations, different study plans, and the syllabus is the only place the difference is written down.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic**
At the cut-off: supplier invoices posted **£2.35m**, goods and services received but not yet invoiced **£0.64m**, earned value **£2.80m**.
On posted cost alone: 2.80 ÷ 2.35 = **1.191**
With the accrual included: 2.80 ÷ 2.99 = **0.937**
About **19% ahead** of plan on cost, or about **6% behind**. Same month, same work.

**Slide 4 — Where that number came from**
Nothing on site changed between the two figures. A cut-off convention that lives on the finance side of the house landed on the delivery side's index. That join is where the money goes.

**Slide 5 — Recall depth against judgement depth**
Recall: you can define the index. Judgement: you can say which figure goes in the board pack, what evidence you attach, and what happens to the £0.64m next month. Read the verbs.

**Slide 6 — Assumed prior knowledge**
Every syllabus assumes something; the useful ones say what. Candidates rarely fail on topics. They fail on situations they had never been in. Where the assumption is written down, you can sit later instead of failing now.

**Slide 7 — Proportion**
A topic list implies equal weight. Two syllabuses can name the same twenty topics and be different examinations, because one spends itself on measurement and the other on reporting.

**Slide 8 — One caution on proportion**
The proportions of a Body of Knowledge and the weighting of an examination are two different statements. A body may publish the first while the second is still an open decision. Ask which one you are reading.

**Slide 9 — What the categories say about themselves**
Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and control. Project management certifications examine people, process and the business environment. Each is built to examine its own subject thoroughly.

**Slide 10 — When the syllabus is not the decision**
If your employer or a client's pre-qualification names a certification, take the named one. Read the syllabus anyway, so you know what you are sitting, but a scope argument does not beat a procurement condition.

**Slide 11 — Where PCI stands**
The Bodies of Knowledge are published in full and free before anybody pays: 13, 16 and 16 domains and 61, 61 and 63 knowledge areas across PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI. credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards them. PCI is new and not accredited — read the syllabus and judge it.
