# Choosing the credential for a cost engineer, on scope

The credential for a cost engineer is a scope decision and almost nothing else. Estimating, cost
control and the economics of an asset's life are examined thoroughly by one category, and if that
is your work, that is the purchase. The reason to look further is narrow: your estimate becomes a
budget, the budget becomes a forecast, and the forecast becomes a reported number.

Disclosure before anything else. credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute
Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials mentioned later. I write for the
body that awards them. Discount this accordingly, then go and read a syllabus.

## Three numbers that were never put on one page

Every estimator I know has watched this happen and very few have been asked about it afterwards.

A sanction estimate of **£20.0m**, issued with a stated accuracy of **±15%**.

The band = 20.0 × 0.15 = **±£3.0m**. So the honest statement is a range of **£17.0m to £23.0m**.

The contingency approved alongside it: **£0.6m**, or **3%**.

| Number on the page | What it says | Who set it |
|---|---|---|
| Estimate £20.0m | The expected out-turn on the stated basis | The estimator |
| Accuracy ±15% (±£3.0m) | The honest range around that expectation | The estimator |
| Contingency £0.6m (3%) | What is held against the range | The sanction meeting |
| Escalation on £8.0m at 4% | £0.32m of indexation exposure | The commercial assumption sheet |

Both the estimate and the contingency are defensible on their own. Put them side by side and they
say something nobody said out loud: the organisation is holding £0.6m against a stated upside
exposure of £3.0m.

That is a risk appetite decision made by arithmetic rather than by anybody in the room deciding
it.

## Then escalation eats half of it

Take the escalation row seriously for a moment. If **£8.0m** of spend remains and indexation runs
at **4%**, the exposure is 8.0 × 0.04 = **£0.32m**.

That is more than half the contingency, before a single risk event has occurred. Draw it down and
**£0.28m** is left standing against everything else on the register.

None of this is an estimating error. It is what happens when an accuracy range, a contingency and
an escalation allowance are set in three separate conversations and never reconciled on one page.

Being able to put those three numbers in front of a sponsor, together, is the competence worth
being examined on. In my experience it is also the single fastest way for a cost engineer to be
taken seriously by a finance function.

## What each category sets out to examine

**Cost and scheduling credentials** examine estimating, cost control, planning, schedule analysis
and the economics of an asset's life. That is your subject, examined in depth, by people who take
it seriously.

**The established project controls certifications** examine measurement, progress, forecasting
and the control of delivery. That is the frame around your estimate once delivery starts and it
begins to be updated every period.

**Accountancy and finance qualifications** examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and
financial control. That is where an out-turn estimate finally lands, at a breadth far wider than
the part that touches you.

Each publishes its boundary and examines its own subject properly. A scope boundary is a design
decision, not a shortcoming. The reconciliation between an accuracy range and a contingency
belongs to none of them in particular, which is exactly why it is so rarely challenged.

## What PCL-AI examines, and the honest reading of it

PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) covers the controls frame at 13 domains and 61 knowledge
areas. Each PCI Body of Knowledge is proportioned 40% finance and reporting, 40% project
management and 20% governed AI, which describes the syllabus rather than an examination weighting.

Now the honest reading, which matters more than the sales version. A large share of that syllabus
is not estimating. If your week is estimate build, benchmarking and basis-of-estimate work, much
of it will be new material rather than a deepening of your own subject.

## When a cost-focused credential is the right answer

**If you estimate for a living** — front-end work, sanction estimates, benchmarking, basis of
estimate — a credential examining estimating and cost control is the fit. It will be recognised
quickly in a cost engineering market, and it asks you to study your own subject rather than an
adjacent one.

**If your employer names a certification in its grading matrix, take that one.** If a client's
prequalification lists one, the same applies. Those are conditions, not comparisons.

**If you need an accredited credential today**, go where accreditation already exists. PCI cannot
meet that requirement now.

**If you are early in estimating**, spend the time on reps and benchmark data first. Examinations
in this category ask for judgement about a real basis of estimate, and judgement comes from having
been wrong a few times and understood why.

## The test to run on your own estimates

Take your last three estimates that went to sanction. For each, write down the accuracy range you
stated, the contingency that was approved, and what actually happened to the out-turn.

If the ranges were respected and the contingency was set against them deliberately, your
organisation understands your work, and a deeper estimating credential builds on solid ground.

If the contingency was set by convention and your range was never mentioned again, the gap is not
estimating skill. It is the conversation between your estimate and everybody who uses it, and that
is a scope question rather than a technique one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is estimating a separate discipline from cost control?**
They are examined together in one category and they are different jobs in most organisations.
Estimating is answered before sanction; cost control is answered every month afterwards. Read the
depth verbs on each side of a syllabus rather than assuming the balance.

**Does benchmarking data matter more than a credential?**
For the work, often yes. A good rate library and an honest benchmark history beat any certificate.
For a move, a credential is what makes your judgement checkable by somebody who cannot see your
data. They answer different questions.

**Should a cost engineer learn revenue recognition?**
Not the whole subject. The useful part is what an out-turn estimate does to a percentage complete,
and therefore to reported margin. That is one mechanism rather than a syllabus, and it can be
learned in an afternoon with a real project in front of you.

**How do I evidence estimating competence without a credential?**
A basis of estimate somebody else could pick up and defend, a benchmark set with sources, and
three examples where you were challenged and held your position with data. That portfolio
interviews well; a credential makes it checkable before the interview.

**Does AI change what a cost engineer is examined on?**
It changes what a cost engineer has to govern: what a model may draft, what it may never decide,
and what evidence has to survive so a reviewer can trace a rate back to its source. That is a
current addition to the job and it is examined in very few places yet.
