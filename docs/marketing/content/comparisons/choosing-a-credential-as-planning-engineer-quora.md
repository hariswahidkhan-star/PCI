# Which certification suits a planning engineer?

It depends on how far past the programme your signature travels, which is a question about your job rather than about the credentials. Two planners with the same title sign very different things, which is why the honest answer is a sorting rule rather than a name.

**Write your signature block first.** Every decision you put your name to in a normal month. Programme development, logic and baseline. Monthly progress and status. Delay analysis and an extension-of-time position. Earned value derived from the programme. Forecast dates feeding a cost forecast. Five to ten lines is usual, and this list is the input.

**Then price the thing your programme is quietly carrying.** A package has 20 days of total float on the path to a sectional completion date, and time-related preliminaries run at £8,500 a day. Priced, the float is 20 × 8,500 = £170,000. Suppose 12 of those days are consumed by instructed change and 8 by a subcontractor's own performance. The entitlement worth pursuing is 12 × 8,500 = £102,000, recoverable only if the records were made in the month each event happened. The other £68,000 is outturn cost with no payer attached: on a package worth £24.0m against a cost forecast of £21.0m, forecast margin falls from £3.0m to £2.932m.

Neither of those consequences is a scheduling question, and that is the whole point of the exercise.

**What the categories examine.** Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. The established project controls certifications examine measurement, progress, forecasting and the control of delivery. Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment. Product certifications for scheduling software examine operating a specific tool competently, which is a real and separately marketable skill. Each publishes a boundary and examines its own subject in depth. None is deficient.

**So here is the sorting rule.** If your work stops at the programme and the cost report is somebody else's document, a credential examining schedule development, maintenance and control examines your actual job. Take it. It will not ask you to study a boundary you never cross. If your dates and progress drive a cost report, an entitlement position or a forecast somebody books, half the consequences of your work land outside a scheduling examination, and that is the case for reading across the boundary before you choose.

**Three things that end the exercise early.** A client's prequalification names a certification: take the named one. An employer's grading matrix names one: take that one. A procurement rule in your market requires an accredited credential: settle that first, because scope reasoning cannot get round it.

One more thing. If you are less than two years into planning, get the reps before the paper: these examinations ask what you would do rather than what a term means.
