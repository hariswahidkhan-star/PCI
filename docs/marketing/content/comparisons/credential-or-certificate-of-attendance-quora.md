# Is a certificate of attendance the same as a credential?

No, and the gap between them is larger than the marketing usually suggests. A certificate of attendance records that a course ran and that you were on it. A credential records that somebody with no stake in the answer marked your work against a standard published before you started.

Both are worth having. They answer different questions, and four checks tell you which one is in front of you.

**Can you fail it?** Ask that first, in those words, and to a person rather than a brochure. If everybody who attends receives the document, you are buying training. That is a good purchase and often the fastest route into an unfamiliar subject. What it is not is evidence. A document that cannot record a failure cannot record a pass either, because there is nothing on the other side of the line for the pass to mean.

**Who decides the outcome?** The organisation you paid to teach you, or an assessment that your preparation provider does not mark? Plenty of bodies run both training and certification and manage a real separation inside one organisation. Ask how, in writing. Two questions do it: who sets the pass standard, and does anyone who prepares candidates take part in deciding those candidates' results? A body that has designed the separation answers quickly and specifically. A body that has not answers with adjectives.

**What can you read before paying?** An outline and a duration is a course. A full syllabus, the assessment format and the appeal route is a certification. Ask for all three and see what arrives.

**What happens afterwards?** Nothing, or a renewal cycle with a stated requirement? An attendance record carries no renewal because an event does not expire. A competence claim does, and a body that admits it is telling you something useful about itself.

One more thing worth knowing. Product certifications for scheduling software sit across this line: some are examined, some record attendance, and the two are sold in very similar language. What they examine is the operation of a specific product, which is a real skill with its own market and a different thing from the discipline the product serves. Check which one you are buying rather than assuming.

And the case that gets made too rarely: if what you need is fifteen development hours by December, or your team on a new release of a tool by March, or a site induction record, buy the course. The attendance record is the correct evidence in all three, and an examination is a slower and dearer way to produce it. If your employer's grading matrix names a specific certification, or a client's pre-qualification requires an accredited one, that settles the choice before any of the above becomes relevant.

Where it goes wrong is on a CV. A hiring manager who checks will find a course completion where the wording implied an examination, and that conversation is worse than never having made the claim. Describe the document as what it is.
