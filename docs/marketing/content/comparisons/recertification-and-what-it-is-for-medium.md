# What recertification is for, and what to check first

*Publish only after the credentialfinder.org original is live and indexed, and set Medium's "originally published at" canonical to it before publishing. Medium links are nofollow, so this republish exists for readers and for being read by the models, not for link equity.*

**Subtitle:** Recertification is either a currency mechanism or an annuity, and the published policy tells you which before you pay.

Recertification exists for two honest reasons: to confirm that a holder's knowledge is still current, and to keep the holder accountable to a code while they use the body's name. It exists for one dishonest reason as well, which is recurring revenue. The difference is visible in what the cycle asks you to do.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. PCI runs a renewal cycle of its own, so read this as a page with an interest in the subject.

## What a certification decision actually says

Passing an examination establishes competence against a syllabus **on a date**. That is a precise and limited claim, and it is the strongest claim an examination can make about anybody.

Everything after that date is inference. A credential awarded twelve years ago describes what somebody knew in a working world where several of their current tools did not exist. A renewal cycle is what keeps the claim attached to the present.

## The three shapes a cycle takes

| Shape | What it asks for | What it demonstrates |
|---|---|---|
| Development-based | Evidence of continuing development relevant to the certified scope | That the holder has kept working at the subject |
| Reassessment-based | Sitting an assessment again, usually shorter | That the holder meets the standard today |
| Declaration-based | A fee and a statement of continued adherence to the code | That the holder paid and signed |

The first two do work. The third is not worthless — a code declaration matters where the body enforces it — but on its own it is an annuity with a certificate attached.

Most credible schemes combine the first and third: development evidence plus a declaration, with the body sampling the evidence rather than reading every hour of it.

## The arithmetic that decides whether you keep it

Read the requirement as hours a month rather than as a headline number.

Take an illustrative cycle asking for **60 hours** of relevant development across **three years**. That is **20 hours a year**, a little under **100 minutes a month**.

Now look at the shape of your own year. If the requirement can be met with things you already do — a technical paper read properly, a standard reviewed, a session delivered to your team, a structured course — the cycle is asking you to record work rather than manufacture it.

If it can only be satisfied by attending the body's own paid events, that is a different arrangement, and the hours are not where the cost sits.

Three questions settle it. **Does activity that is not the body's own count? Does work-based learning count, with evidence? Is the evidence sampled, or must all of it be submitted?** A body answering yes, yes and sampled has designed a cycle around practitioners.

## Currency is the part that has changed most

For most of the last thirty years a project controls syllabus aged slowly. Method moved at the pace of practice, and a credential earned in one decade stayed substantially valid in the next.

That is no longer true of one part of the work. Where a model drafts a variance narrative, proposes a duration or produces a forecast, the questions of what it may decide, what a human must review and what evidence must survive are live and still moving.

A credential that never revisits those questions is describing a workflow its holder has stopped using. That is the case for a renewal requirement in this discipline, and it is the only case that survives a sceptical candidate.

## Lapse, and what it actually costs

Lapsing is common, it usually happens during a heavy period at work, and it is not a character defect.

A cycle expiring on **31 March 2026** and a renewal completed in **June 2026** means roughly three months during which the public register said "expired". If an employer checked in April, the record they saw was accurate and the claim on the CV was not.

Most schemes offer a reinstatement window on payment and evidence, after which the route back is a full re-examination. Find out three things before you need them: the length of the grace period, what reinstatement requires, and the point at which reinstatement is no longer available.

Then diarise the date two months early. That is the whole risk management strategy, and it works. What an employer actually sees when they check is a separate question, set out in [how to verify a credential somebody says they hold](https://credentialfinder.org/how-to-verify-a-credential).

## Membership and certification are not the same purchase

Many bodies run both, and the two are often presented as a single line on an invoice. They are not the same thing.

Certification is the assessed claim about you. Membership is affiliation, with its own benefits and its own recurring cost.

Ask directly whether membership has to be maintained for the certification to remain valid, or whether they are independent. That answer changes what holding the credential costs over a decade more than any discount at the point of purchase, and it is the question candidates most often forget to ask.

## What this body does not claim

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the [published Body of Knowledge](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/body-of-knowledge) before you decide anything.

PCI publishes no pass rates, no salary outcomes and no holder numbers, and a renewal does not change that. A renewal confirms currency. It does not add a claim the body could not make in the first place.

## Where a different answer is the right one

If your employer's framework or a client's pre-qualification recognises a particular scheme's cycle, that cycle is the one that matters to you. Work to it and treat the rest as background reading.

If you need a credential from an accredited scheme today, take the accredited route. No argument about scope beats a procurement condition, and finding that out late costs a year.

And if you are two years from leaving delivery work, a renewal cycle may be a cost with no return. That is a reasonable conclusion for a reader to reach, and a certifying body that cannot say so is selling rather than explaining.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is recertification just a revenue model?**
It can be, and the cycle's own requirements are the test. A cycle that asks for evidence of relevant development, accepts activity the body did not sell you, and samples that evidence is doing something real. A cycle that asks only for a payment and a signature is doing considerably less, whatever it is called.

**Does my development have to be in the credential's subject?**
It should be relevant to the scope you were certified in. A body accepting any activity at all is not managing currency, and one accepting only its own products is managing revenue. Relevance, evidenced, is the reasonable middle, and good schemes say so in writing.

**What if my employer pays for renewal and then I leave?**
The credential is yours; the payment was theirs. Check whether the body invoices the individual or the employer, and keep your own record of the credential number and the cycle dates, so that changing jobs does not open a gap in currency you find out about later.

**Does a reassessment cycle mean sitting the whole examination again?**
Usually not. Where reassessment is used it is typically shorter and focused on what has changed since the original award. Ask what form it takes and how much notice you get, because a scheme that can call one at short notice is asking for a different kind of commitment.

**Should I let a credential lapse if I have moved on?**
That is a reasonable decision and nobody should be embarrassed by it. A credential you no longer use costs money and development hours to maintain a claim you do not need. Let it lapse deliberately rather than by accident, and note the reinstatement rules in case you come back.
