# The finance and delivery overlap: who actually examines it

The finance and delivery overlap is the place where a forecast becomes a reported number. Delivery credentials examine how the forecast is built. Accountancy qualifications examine how the reported number must be arrived at. Neither sets out to examine the join, because it belongs to both subjects and sits at the centre of neither.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. The argument below can be checked against any syllabus you care to open, and an interested party is exactly the sort of author whose argument you should check.

## What sits on each side of the join

| | Delivery side | The join | Finance side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examined on | Estimating, planning, measurement, forecasting | — | Recognition, measurement, disclosure, control |
| Owns | Physical progress and the outturn estimate | The cut-off, earning rule, accrual, change | Reported revenue, margin and provision |
| Assumes | Somebody downstream books it correctly | — | The delivery data arriving is sound |
| Typical artefact | Cost report and programme | The month-end handshake | Contract asset, provision, recognised revenue |
| Fails quietly when | The estimate is stale | Nobody owns it | The inputs were never questioned |

Both outer columns are worked to a published standard by people who were examined on them. The middle column has no examination behind it in either category, and that is not a failing of either. A syllabus needs boundaries, and these are drawn where the professions are drawn.

## The arithmetic, so you can check it in your head

Percentage complete on a contract is commonly measured by costs incurred against total expected costs. The denominator is a forecast the delivery team owns. The output is revenue, which finance reports.

Contract price **£26.0m**. Costs incurred to date **£9.0m**. Estimated total cost at the last approved review **£24.0m**.

Progress = 9.0 ÷ 24.0 = **37.5%**. Revenue = 0.375 × 26.0 = **£9.75m**. Margin recognised = 9.75 − 9.0 = **£0.75m**.

Now the delivery team revises the estimate to **£25.5m** for good reasons that have nothing to do with accounting: a subcontractor replaced, a rate agreed, three weeks of weather.

Progress = 9.0 ÷ 25.5 = **35.3%**. Revenue = 0.353 × 26.0 = **£9.18m**. Margin recognised = 9.18 − 9.0 = **£0.18m**.

The same month, the same site, the same work in the ground, and **£0.57m** less reported margin. Nobody in that sequence did anything wrong. A delivery judgement became a reported result through a mechanism neither professional was examined on.

## What happens when the estimate keeps going

If estimated total cost reaches **£26.6m** against a price of **£26.0m**, the contract is expected to lose **£0.6m**.

An expected loss is recognised as soon as it is known rather than spread across the remaining periods. That is why a single forecast revision can land in a reported result as one large movement rather than a gentle slope, and why the timing of the revision matters as much as its size.

This is also the point at which a delivery conversation becomes a board conversation, usually without anybody planning it.

## The four artefacts that live in the join

Each belongs to nobody in particular, which is why each is where the failures are found.

**The cut-off calendar.** Progress measured to one date, costs booked to another, and a performance index that moves for reasons that are not performance.

**The accrual list.** Work done and not yet invoiced. Left out, the cost report flatters itself, and the forecast built on it inherits the flattery.

**The earning rule.** A 50/50 rule and a 0/100 rule on the same activity produce different earned value in the same month, and therefore different indices, forecasts and reported margin.

**The change register.** Instructed work not yet priced, priced work not yet approved, approved work not yet in the budget. Three states, three treatments, and the difference between them is where a claim is won or lost.

None of the four is technically difficult. All four require somebody who understands what the other side does with them, which is a scope question rather than a process one. What the index in these examples is actually measuring, and what it is not, is set out in [earned value, defined and worked](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/earned-value-management).

## What each category examines, in its own terms

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. A holder can build the forecast above and defend the estimate underneath it.

Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. A holder can take the progress percentage and produce a defensible set of accounts from it.

Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process. A holder can test whether the controls around the handshake operated, which is a different and complementary question — one taken from the finance side in [where PFL-AI sits against audit credentials](https://credentialfinder.org/pfl-ai-and-audit-credentials), on the site this Institute operates.

Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment, which is the breadth the other three assume around them.

Four categories, four subjects, each examined thoroughly. The estimate that moved and the margin that followed sit between them.

## Who should own the join

Somebody named, with authority over both the earning rules and the cut-off calendar, normally in project controls with a direct line into finance.

The specific answer matters less than the fact that it has been answered. Unowned, the four artefacts drift, and each drift is defensible on its own terms. That is what makes this failure quiet rather than loud.

A practical test: ask who signs off the earning rules for the year. If three people think it is somebody else, you have found the gap before it finds you.

## When the overlap is not your problem

If your work stops at the programme — you build it, status it, analyse it and hand it on — the join is somebody else's week, and a scheduling-focused credential examines what you actually do. Paying for a syllabus with a large finance component to cover a boundary you never cross is buying reading, not evidence.

The same is true in reverse. A finance professional in a functional role who never sees a progress claim is squarely served by an accountancy qualification, and the overlap is an interesting subject rather than a needed one.

And if an employer or a client requires a specific credential, that settles the question before scope enters it.

The overlap matters when you personally sign something that crosses it: a forecast a finance team will book, a valuation a client will pay against, a percentage complete that becomes revenue.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the overlap not just month-end process?**
Process is how it is administered; the overlap is what the process administers. A spotless close can still report margin that moves £0.57m because an estimate changed for delivery reasons. The calendar cannot tell you whether the estimate was right, and the estimate is what decides the number.

**Does this mean delivery people need an accounting qualification?**
No. It means being examined on the handshake: what a percentage complete becomes, what an accrual is for, when an expected loss must be recognised in full. That is a far smaller subject than accountancy, and it is the part that touches their own signature.

**How do I tell whether a credential covers the join?**
Look for the artefacts by name in the syllabus: cut-off, accrual, earning rules, change control, contract asset, expected loss. If they appear only as definitions, the coverage is recall depth. If they appear with decisions attached, that is working depth.

**Why does one estimate revision move so much?**
Because the estimate is the denominator in the progress fraction and the trigger for expected-loss recognition. It affects both the percentage and, once expected costs exceed the price, the timing of the whole loss. Two mechanisms, one number, which is why it moves the result twice.

**Who audits any of this?**
Audit credentials examine governance, risk and control, so a holder can test whether the controls around the handshake operated as designed. That is a genuinely different question from whether the estimate was any good, and both are worth asking.
