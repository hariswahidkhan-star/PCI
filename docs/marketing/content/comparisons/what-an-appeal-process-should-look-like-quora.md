# Yes, if you can name the rule that was not followed

Usually yes, and the outcome depends almost entirely on whether you can point at a rule the body did not follow. An appeal is a request to reconsider a decision. It is not a second opinion on whether you knew the material, and reviewers cannot give you one.

Start by getting the route right. An appeal challenges a decision made about you — the result, an eligibility refusal, an integrity finding, an invalidated credential, a refused accommodation. A complaint is about conduct or service, such as a centre that opened late. They are usually different routes with different people and deadlines, and an appeal sent to the complaints inbox comes back as an apology instead of a decision.

Then work out whether you have a ground.

Checkable grounds look like this: the session was disrupted and the disruption is in the record; the paper asked for one thing and the marking scheme rewarded another; an eligibility rule was applied that is not the published rule; an accommodation was agreed and not provided.

Here is the second one worked, because it is the one candidates most often have and least often spot. An item gives earned value of £26.6m and actual cost of £30.0m and asks for the cost performance index to two decimal places. 26.6 ÷ 30.0 = 0.886666…, which is 0.89 to two places and 0.887 to three. A candidate who wrote 0.89 answered the question that was asked. If only 0.887 scored, the marking scheme contradicted the paper's own instruction — and that defect hit everybody who sat it, which is why it is worth raising even if your own result would not change.

What is not a ground on its own: disagreeing with the syllabus, disagreeing with the pass standard, or finding the paper harder than expected. Those are arguments about how the scheme is designed, and the route for them is a complaint or a consultation.

Write it short. One sentence naming the decision, one naming the rule or instruction you say was not followed, and the evidence attached. Three lines beat three pages, because the reviewer is looking for the rule.

Two more things to check in the policy. Whether there is a deadline that binds the body as well as you — most policies give the candidate a window and give themselves none. And what your record shows while the appeal is open: a result under review should show no score and no pass or fail, because a provisional number that is later withdrawn cannot be recalled from the people who already saw it.

Disclosure, since it belongs in the answer: I write for Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials and also operates credentialfinder.org, the comparison site linked at the end. PCI publishes its appeals policy and a separate complaints route, and its review sits with people who did not make the original decision — but the route ends inside PCI. An accredited scheme adds a body above the scheme that can be told it is not following its own rules. If you need that layer, take the accredited route.
