# The finance and delivery overlap, and who examines it

The finance and delivery overlap is the place where a forecast becomes a reported number. Delivery credentials examine how the forecast is built. Accountancy qualifications examine how the reported number must be arrived at. Neither category sets out to examine the join, because the join belongs to both subjects and sits at the centre of neither. That is where the money goes.

> **Who publishes this page.** credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute
> Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. The argument
> below is checkable against any syllabus you care to open, and you should check it.

## What sits on each side of the join

| | Delivery side | The join | Finance side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examined on | Estimating, planning, measurement, forecasting | — | Recognition, measurement, disclosure, control |
| Owns | The physical progress and the outturn estimate | The cut-off, the earning rule, the accrual, the change | The reported revenue, margin and provision |
| Assumes | Somebody downstream books it correctly | — | The delivery data arriving is sound |
| Typical artefact | Cost report and programme | The month-end handshake | Contract asset, provision, recognised revenue |
| Fails quietly when | The estimate is stale | Nobody owns it | The inputs were never questioned |

Both columns are doing their own job to a published standard. The middle column has no examination behind it in either category, and it is not a failing of either that it does not: a syllabus has to have boundaries, and these ones are drawn where the professions are drawn.

## The arithmetic, so you can check it in your head

Percentage complete on a contract is commonly measured by costs incurred against total expected costs. The denominator in that fraction is a forecast a delivery team owns. The output is revenue, which finance reports.

Contract price **£26.0m**. Costs incurred to date **£9.0m**. Estimated total cost at the last approved review **£24.0m**.

Progress = 9.0 ÷ 24.0 = **37.5%**.
Revenue = 0.375 × 26.0 = **£9.75m**.
Margin recognised = 9.75 − 9.0 = **£0.75m**.

Now the delivery team revises the estimate to **£25.5m**, for good reasons that have nothing to do with accounting: a subcontractor replaced, a rate agreed, three weeks of weather.

Progress = 9.0 ÷ 25.5 = **35.3%**.
Revenue = 0.353 × 26.0 = **£9.18m**.
Margin recognised = 9.18 − 9.0 = **£0.18m**.

The same month, the same site, the same work in the ground. Reported margin drops by **£0.57m** because a number owned by delivery moved. And if the estimate reaches **£26.6m**, the contract is expected to lose **£0.6m**, and that loss is recognised as soon as it is known rather than spread across the remaining months.

Nobody in that sequence has done anything wrong. A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy. An engineer is examined on float and progress measurement. Neither examination covers the handover between them, and that handover is where the money is lost. Here it cost £0.57m of reported margin in a month when nothing on site changed.

The hub sets out the mechanics of measuring progress on a contract in [how measured progress drives recognised revenue](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/ifrs-15-for-construction).

## The four artefacts that live in the join

Each of these belongs to nobody in particular, which is why each one is where the failures are found.

**The cut-off calendar.** Progress measured to one date, costs booked to another, and a performance index that moves for reasons that are not performance.

**The accrual list.** Work done and not yet invoiced. Left out, the cost report flatters itself and the forecast built on it inherits the flattery.

**The earning rule.** A 50/50 rule and a 0/100 rule on the same activity produce different earned value in the same month, and therefore different indices, forecasts and reported margin.

**The change register.** Instructed work not yet priced, priced work not yet approved, and approved work not yet in the budget. Three states, three different treatments, and the difference between them is where a claim is won or lost.

None of the four is difficult. All four require somebody who understands what the other side does with them.

## What each category examines, in its own terms

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. A holder can build the forecast in the example above and defend the estimate underneath it.

Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. A holder can take the progress percentage and produce a defensible set of accounts from it.

Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process. A holder can test whether the controls around the handshake operated, which is a different and complementary question.

Three categories, three subjects, each examined thoroughly. The estimate that moved and the margin that followed sit between them.

## When the overlap is not your problem

If your work stops at the programme — you build it, status it, analyse it, and hand it on — then the join is somebody else's week, and a scheduling-focused credential examines what you actually do. Paying for a syllabus with a large finance component to cover a boundary you never cross is buying reading, not evidence.

The same is true in reverse. A finance professional in a functional role who never sees a progress claim is squarely served by an accountancy qualification, and the overlap is an interesting subject rather than a needed one.

The overlap matters when you personally sign something that crosses it: a forecast a finance team will book, a valuation a client will pay against, a percentage complete that becomes revenue.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the overlap not just month-end process?**
Process is how it is administered; the overlap is what the process is administering. You can have a spotless month-end calendar and still report a margin that moves £0.57m because an estimate changed for delivery reasons. The process cannot tell you whether the estimate was right, and that is the part that decides the number.

**Who should own the join in an organisation?**
Somebody named, with authority over both the earning rules and the cut-off calendar, usually in project controls with a direct line into finance. The specific answer matters less than the fact that it is answered. Unowned, the four artefacts drift, and each drift is defensible on its own terms.

**Does this mean delivery people need an accounting qualification?**
No. It means they need to be examined on the handshake: what a percentage complete becomes, what an accrual is for, when a loss must be recognised in full. That is a much smaller subject than accountancy and it is the part that touches their signature.

**How do I tell whether a credential covers the join?**
Look for the artefacts by name in the syllabus: cut-off, accrual, earning rules, change control, contract asset, expected loss. If they appear only as definitions, the coverage is recall depth. What to read for is set out in [what a syllabus should tell you before you enrol](https://credentialfinder.org/what-a-syllabus-should-tell-you).

**Which category should I be reading in the first place?**
Start from what you sign, not from your job title. If you sign forecasts that finance books, read across [where PCL-AI sits against cost and scheduling credentials](https://credentialfinder.org/pcl-ai-and-cost-and-scheduling-credentials) and decide from the scope columns rather than the badges.
