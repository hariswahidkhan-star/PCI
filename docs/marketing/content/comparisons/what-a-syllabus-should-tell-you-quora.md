# What should I look for in a certification syllabus?

Six things, and most candidates read only the first. A certification syllabus should tell you the scope as boundaries, the depth in verbs, the proportion across the subject, what it assumes you already know, the sources it draws on, and what it explicitly excludes.

**Scope, written as boundaries.** Not a topic list. A boundary statement tells you whether the decisions you actually sign sit inside the examination. Without it you find out what was excluded on the day.

**Depth, written in verbs, is the check people skip.** A line reading "cost performance index" could mean recall or judgement, and the difference decides how many hours you need.

At a reporting cut-off, a package has £2.35m of supplier invoices posted and £0.64m of goods and services received but not yet invoiced. Earned value for the period is £2.80m.

On posted cost alone, the index is 2.80 ÷ 2.35 = 1.191. With the accrual included, it is 2.80 ÷ 2.99 = 0.937. About 19 per cent better than plan on cost, or about 6 per cent worse. Nothing on site changed: a cut-off convention that lives on the finance side of the house landed on the delivery side's index.

A recall-depth syllabus is satisfied when you can define the index. A judgement-depth syllabus is satisfied when you can say which figure goes in the board pack and what you do about the £0.64m next month.

**Proportion.** A topic list implies equal weight and that is almost never true. Two documents can name the same twenty topics and be different examinations, because one spends itself on measurement and the other on reporting. One caution that applies to every body: the proportions of a body of knowledge and the weighting of an examination are two different statements, and a body may publish the first while the second is still an open decision.

**Assumed prior knowledge.** Every syllabus assumes something; the useful ones say what. Candidates rarely fail on topics. They fail on situations they have never been in — a month-end, a variation argued across a table, a forecast defended to somebody who did not want to hear it. Where the assumption is written down you can sit later instead of failing now.

**Sources, and explicit exclusions.** The sources tell you what the assessment treats as correct, which is how you tell the discipline apart from one organisation's house method. The exclusions tell you what somebody else is expected to cover, and every unstated boundary eventually becomes an argument.

The categories are honest about themselves, which makes reading two of them useful. Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. Project management certifications examine people, process and the business environment. Each is built to examine its own subject thoroughly, and the point of the reading is to find out which subject you are being examined in.

One case where the syllabus is not the deciding document: if your employer or a client's pre-qualification names a specific certification, take the named one. Read the syllabus anyway so you know what you are sitting, but a scope argument does not beat a procurement condition.
