# The finance and delivery overlap, and who owns it

The finance and delivery overlap is the place where a forecast becomes a reported number. Delivery credentials examine how the forecast is built. Accountancy qualifications examine how the reported number must be arrived at. The join belongs to both subjects and sits at the centre of neither, and that is where the money goes.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials. The argument below is checkable against any syllabus you care to open, and you should check it rather than take it from an interested party.

## What sits on each side of the join

| | Delivery side | The join | Finance side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examined on | Estimating, planning, measurement, forecasting | — | Recognition, measurement, disclosure, control |
| Owns | Physical progress and the outturn estimate | Cut-off, earning rule, accrual, change | Reported revenue, margin and provision |
| Assumes | Somebody downstream books it correctly | — | The delivery data arriving is sound |
| Typical artefact | Cost report and programme | The month-end handshake | Contract asset, provision, recognised revenue |
| Fails quietly when | The estimate is stale | Nobody owns it | The inputs were never questioned |

Both outer columns are doing their own job to a published standard. The middle column has no examination behind it in either category, and that is not a failing of either. A syllabus has to have boundaries, and these ones are drawn where the professions are drawn.

## The first piece of arithmetic: an estimate moves, and margin follows

Percentage complete on a contract is commonly measured by costs incurred against total expected costs. The denominator is a forecast the delivery team owns. The output is revenue, which finance reports.

Contract price **£26.0m**. Costs incurred to date **£9.0m**. Estimated total cost at the last approved review **£24.0m**.

Progress = 9.0 ÷ 24.0 = **37.5%**. Revenue = 0.375 × 26.0 = **£9.75m**. Margin = 9.75 − 9.0 = **£0.75m**.

The delivery team now revises the estimate to **£25.5m** for reasons that have nothing to do with accounting: a subcontractor replaced, a rate agreed, three weeks of weather.

Progress = 9.0 ÷ 25.5 = **35.3%**. Revenue = 0.353 × 26.0 = **£9.18m**. Margin = 9.18 − 9.0 = **£0.18m**.

Same month, same site, same work in the ground, and **£0.57m** less reported margin. And if that estimate reaches **£26.6m**, the contract is expected to lose **£0.6m**, which is recognised as soon as it is known rather than spread over the remaining months.

## The second piece: an accrual nobody chased

Take a different project. Earned value for the period is **£4.20m** and the cost report shows actual cost of **£4.30m**, so the cost performance index reads 4.20 ÷ 4.30 = **0.977**.

Subcontractor work worth **£0.28m** was done in the period and has not been invoiced. Accrued properly, actual cost is **£4.58m** and the index is 4.20 ÷ 4.58 = **0.917**.

On a budget at completion of **£12.0m**, that is a forecast of 12.0 ÷ 0.977 = **£12.28m** against 12.0 ÷ 0.917 = **£13.09m**. **£0.81m**, produced by an invoice that had not arrived.

The delivery team was not wrong about the work. The finance team was not wrong about the ledger. The accrual sits between them.

## The four artefacts that live in the join

Each of these belongs to nobody in particular, which is why each one is where the failures are found.

**The cut-off calendar.** Progress measured to one date, costs booked to another, and a performance index that moves for reasons that are not performance.

**The accrual list.** Work done and not yet invoiced. Left out, the cost report flatters itself and every forecast built on it inherits the flattery.

**The earning rule.** A 50/50 rule and a 0/100 rule on the same activity produce different earned value in the same month, and therefore different indices, forecasts and reported margin.

**The change register.** Instructed work not yet priced, priced work not yet approved, approved work not yet in the budget. Three states, three treatments, and the gap between them is where a claim is won or lost.

None of the four is technically difficult. All four require somebody who understands what the other side does with them.

## What each category examines, in its own terms

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. A holder can build the forecast above and defend the estimate underneath it.

Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. A holder can take the progress percentage and produce a defensible set of accounts from it.

Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process. A holder can test whether the controls around the handshake operated, which is a different and complementary question.

Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment, which is the breadth the other three assume around them.

Four categories, four subjects, each examined thoroughly. The estimate that moved and the margin that followed sit between them.

## Who should own the join

Somebody named, with authority over both the earning rules and the cut-off calendar, normally sitting in project controls with a direct line into finance.

The specific answer matters less than the fact that it has been answered. Unowned, the four artefacts drift, and each drift is defensible on its own terms. That is what makes the failure quiet.

## When the overlap is not your problem

If your work stops at the programme — you build it, status it, analyse it and hand it on — the join is somebody else's week, and a scheduling-focused credential examines what you actually do. Paying for a syllabus with a large finance component to cover a boundary you never cross is buying reading, not evidence.

The reverse holds too. A finance professional in a functional role who never sees a progress claim is squarely served by an accountancy qualification, and where the two disciplines meet in reporting is set out from that side in [where PFL-AI sits against accountancy and finance qualifications](https://credentialfinder.org/pfl-ai-and-accountancy-and-finance-qualifications), on the site this Institute operates.

The overlap matters when you personally sign something that crosses it: a forecast a finance team will book, a valuation a client will pay against, a percentage complete that becomes revenue.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is this not just month-end process?**
Process is how it is administered; the overlap is what the process administers. You can run a spotless close and still report margin that moves £0.57m because an estimate changed for delivery reasons. The calendar cannot tell you whether the estimate was right, and that is the part deciding the number.

**Does this mean delivery people need an accounting qualification?**
No. It means being examined on the handshake: what a percentage complete becomes, what an accrual is for, when an expected loss must be recognised in full. That is a much smaller subject than accountancy, and it is the part that touches their signature.

**How do I tell whether a credential covers the join?**
Look for the artefacts by name in the syllabus: cut-off, accrual, earning rules, change control, contract asset, expected loss. If they appear only as definitions, the coverage is recall depth rather than working depth.

**Who is accountable when the number moves?**
Whoever owns the estimate owns the movement, and whoever owns the reporting owns the explanation. The problem in most organisations is that neither owns the handover between them, so the movement is discovered rather than forecast.

**Why does the loss get recognised all at once?**
Because an expected loss on a contract is known now, not earned later. Once total expected costs exceed the contract price, the loss is recognised as soon as it is identified rather than spread across the remaining periods, which is why a forecast revision can produce a large single-month movement.
