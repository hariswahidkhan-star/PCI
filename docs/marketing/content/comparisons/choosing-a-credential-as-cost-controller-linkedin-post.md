# One missing invoice, one million pounds of forecast

**Post body (1,895 characters):**

An invoice that has not arrived is worth about a million pounds on this job.

Earned value for the period: £6.60m. Actual cost as booked: £6.30m. Two subcontractors have done work they have not yet invoiced, valued at £0.45m.

As booked: 6.60 ÷ 6.30 = 1.048. The job is outperforming.
With the accrual: 6.60 ÷ 6.75 = 0.978. The job is behind.

Forecast on a budget at completion of £15.0m:
at 1.048, outturn = £14.32m
at 0.978, outturn = £15.34m

Just over £1.0m of difference, from a missing invoice rather than anything that happened on site. Report the first number and a board has been told the job will come in under budget when it will not. The correction lands a quarter later.

Now the credential question. That outturn figure is the denominator when progress is measured by costs incurred against total expected costs, so the £1.0m does not stop at the cost report — it moves recognised revenue and reported margin.

Does the examination you are weighing cover that step, or hand over at the cost report?

Both designs are legitimate. Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis, in depth. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control, and assume delivery data arrives from a source they do not examine. Each publishes a boundary.

So sort it by what you sign. If you own the cost report and a commercial manager takes it from there, a credential examining estimating and cost control examines your job. Take that one.

If your accrual list and cut-off calendar are what you argue about at month-end, the join is where your errors land, and the join is what to look for in a syllabus.
