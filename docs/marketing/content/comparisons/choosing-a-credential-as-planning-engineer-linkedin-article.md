# What a planner should look for in a credential

If your signature stops at the programme, a credential examining schedule development, maintenance and analysis covers your job and is the shortest route to evidence. If your dates and progress drive a cost report, an entitlement position or a forecast somebody books, the examination you want has to cross that boundary. Sort it by what you sign.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials mentioned below. Treat the reasoning as checkable and the recommendation as interested, which is the only sensible way to read anybody's comparison, including this one.

## Start with your signature block, not your job title

Two people with the same title on the same site can sign very different things. Write your own list before you read anybody's syllabus.

| What you put your name to | Which category examines it | What that category leaves to a different examination |
|---|---|---|
| Programme development, logic and baseline | Cost and scheduling credentials | What the programme does to the cost report |
| Monthly progress update and status | Cost and scheduling credentials | Whether the progress becomes recognised revenue |
| Delay analysis and an extension-of-time position | Cost and scheduling credentials | The commercial valuation of the entitlement |
| Earned value derived from the programme | The established project controls certifications | The accrual and cut-off sitting under the actuals |
| Forecast dates feeding a cost forecast | Split across two categories | The reported result the forecast produces |
| Resource and cost loading of the programme | The established project controls certifications | The accounting treatment of what is loaded |
| Building the programme in a specific tool | Product certifications for scheduling software | The planning discipline the tool exists to serve |

The first three rows sit squarely inside one category, examined thoroughly and by design. The rest straddle a boundary that professions draw and projects do not.

## The categories, described by what they examine

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. For the top half of that table, that is precisely the subject and the examinations go at it in depth.

The established project controls certifications examine measurement, progress, forecasting and the control of delivery. That is the wider frame around the programme, and it is where a planner moving towards a controls role is heading.

Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment. Breadth is the design, so depth on schedule analysis is not what they set out to prove, and they say so.

Product certifications for scheduling software examine operating a specific tool competently. That is a real and separately marketable skill. It is evidence about the tool rather than about the discipline, and the two are often sold in the same sentence.

None of these categories is deficient. Each publishes a boundary, examines its own subject properly and leaves the rest to somebody with a different examination. The question is only which boundary sits inside your signature block.

## Float looks free until somebody prices it

Here is the arithmetic that decides the credential question for most planners, and it takes thirty seconds.

A package carries **20 days** of total float on the path to a sectional completion date. Time-related preliminaries on that package run at **£8,500 a day**.

Total float, priced: 20 × 8,500 = **£170,000**.

Now split the consumption. Suppose **12 days** go to instructed change and **8 days** to a subcontractor's own performance.

The entitlement worth pursuing is 12 × 8,500 = **£102,000**. Whether any of it is recoverable depends almost entirely on the records made in the month each event happened, not on the analysis anybody runs at the end.

The other 8 days are **£68,000** of outturn cost that nobody is paying you for.

Follow that £68,000 one step further, because this is the step scheduling examinations are not built to cover. Say the package carries a contract value of **£24.0m** against a cost forecast of **£21.0m** — a forecast margin of **£3.0m**, or 12.5%. Add the £68,000 and the forecast margin becomes **£2.932m**, or 12.2%.

Three tenths of a percentage point, from eight days of float that looked free on a programme. And the £102,000 on the other side sits in a claim position somebody in finance has to decide how much of to recognise, if any, because unagreed amounts are constrained until it is highly probable they will not reverse.

## Two planners, one job title

A planner who hands over a programme and a planner who can carry that chain from float to reported margin are doing different jobs. Both are legitimate and both are needed.

The first is examined thoroughly by a scheduling-focused credential. The second is only half-examined by one, because half the consequences of the work land in a different subject.

Which of those two you are — or intend to be in two years — is the real question behind the credential choice. Everything else is detail.

## When a scheduling-focused credential is the better answer

Plainly, and often.

If you plan, status and analyse programmes, and the cost report is somebody else's document, a credential examining schedule development, maintenance and control examines your actual job. It will not ask you to study a boundary you never cross, and the evenings you save are real.

If a client's prequalification or an employer's grading matrix names a specific certification, take the named one. That is a requirement, not a comparison, and no amount of scope reasoning changes it.

If a procurement rule in your market requires an accredited credential, settle that before anything else. It settles the question against this Institute today.

And if you are two years into planning, get the reps before the paper. Credible examinations in this category ask what you would do rather than what a term means, and the situations only read like your own month once you have had a few. The route into the role, and what the first three years actually contain, is set out in [how to become a planning engineer](https://pciworld.org/how-to-become-a-planning-engineer).

## How to decide this week

Write the list of what you sign. Mark each line examined, assumed or excluded against two syllabuses, and count.

Then read ten current advertisements for the job you want next, in the market you want it in, and note what they ask for. That is your own recognition data, gathered in twenty minutes, and it is worth more than any general claim about what employers prefer.

If the count and the advertisements point the same way, you are finished. If they conflict, work out which problem you actually have: not getting the interview, or struggling once you are in the job.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a cost credential as well as a scheduling one?**
Only if you sign cost outcomes. Holding two credentials that examine adjacent halves of one discipline is common and often unnecessary. Look instead for a single examination that covers the join you cross, and put the saved evenings into whichever part of the month-end currently costs you sleep.

**Is a tool certificate enough on its own?**
It proves you can drive a specific scheduling product, which is a genuine and marketable skill with its own market. It is not evidence about the discipline the product serves. Decide which of the two you are buying before you pay, because they are frequently sold together.

**How much experience should I have before sitting anything?**
Enough that the scenarios read like your own month. Two to three years of live programme work is a common expectation, and it exists because these papers ask for judgement rather than definitions. What a syllabus assumes about you is usually stated somewhere in it, and it is worth finding before you enrol.

**My programme feeds an earned value report. Does that change the answer?**
Yes, materially. Once your dates and progress drive an index somebody forecasts from, half the consequences of your work land outside a scheduling examination. That is the case for reading across the categories rather than picking the one nearest your job title, and the category-level comparison is in [where PCL-AI sits against cost and scheduling credentials](https://credentialfinder.org/pcl-ai-and-cost-and-scheduling-credentials).

**Will a credential get me a planning role in a new market?**
It gets your CV read by somebody with thirty seconds and no context for your last employer, which is worth a great deal and is not the same as being hired. Pair it with three programmes you can talk through in detail, because that is what the second interview turns out to be.
