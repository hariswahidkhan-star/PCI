# How to judge a new certifying body before you pay

You judge a new certifying body on what it can show you today, because a track record is the one thing it does not have. Three artefacts do most of the work: the syllabus it publishes before payment, the separation between preparing candidates and deciding results, and the list of claims it declines to make.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here — and which is itself a new body. Read this as an argument you are entitled to turn back on the author, because that is exactly what the last section does.

## What age is actually standing in for

Longevity is a useful signal because it correlates with things that are hard to fake. It is not the thing itself, and each of the things it stands in for can be inspected directly.

| What age suggests | What you can inspect instead | What the inspection proves |
|---|---|---|
| A settled standard | The syllabus, published in full before payment | What is examined, assumed and excluded |
| A defensible decision | The pass-standard policy, in writing | Whether the line is about you or the cohort |
| Independence | Who writes, marks and sells the preparation | Whether an incentive runs the wrong way |
| A working process | The appeal policy, with grounds and a decision-maker | How the worst day was designed |
| A durable record | A public verification check you run yourself | Whether a stranger can confirm your result |
| Restraint | The claims that are absent | Whether the numbers were manufactured |

Five of those six take under an hour. The sixth, restraint, takes only a careful read.

## The syllabus: read the boundaries first

Every published syllabus lists topics, and a long list proves that somebody wrote a long list. The informative part is the boundary.

Look for what the examination assumes a candidate already knows and what it deliberately leaves to a neighbouring discipline. A body that has drawn its boundaries in writing has done the hard thinking. A body that implies it covers everything has not started.

The other half of this check is access. A syllabus published free, in full, before payment, gives you the means to disagree with the body that wrote it. That is an unusual thing for an organisation to hand a prospective customer, and it is worth noticing who does.

## The pass standard: about you, or about the room

Ask one question and listen to how quickly a considered answer arrives. Is the pass standard set against a written description of a competent candidate, or against the distribution of everybody who sat?

If a fixed share of each cohort passes, your result depends partly on who happened to sit that month. That is the right instrument for allocating a limited number of places and the wrong one for certifying that a person meets a standard.

Neither method is disreputable. Not knowing which one you use is.

## Separation: who sells the preparation

Find out who writes the questions, who sets the standard, who marks the paper, and whether any of them earns money from the course.

Where the organisation that prepares candidates also decides results, the incentive points the wrong way. Nothing about that is an accusation. It is a design flaw, and design flaws are fixed by design: separation, written down, with names against roles.

This is one of the few checks where a new body can be as strong as an old one on day one, because separation is a structural choice rather than an accumulated reputation.

## The appeal route, read before you need it

Four elements belong in an appeal policy: the grounds on which you may appeal, the timescale, the person who decides, and confirmation that the person deciding had no part in the original decision.

Read it before you enrol rather than after a result you did not expect. It is a short document, and it is much harder to write honestly than a topic list.

A body that has designed carefully for the day a candidate disputes a result has thought about the thing that will eventually test it. That is the closest a new organisation can get to showing you how it will behave under pressure.

## Verification: the thirty-second test

A credential is worth what a stranger can confirm in half a minute, years later, without your involvement.

Run it now, on somebody else's credential, before you enrol in anything. See what the public record returns: name, credential, status, date awarded, date expiring. If confirming a result needs an email to an inbox, that is what your future employer's recruiter will find at five o'clock on a Friday.

## Read for absence, and treat it as evidence

A body in its first years has no pass rate worth quoting, no holder population, no employer survey and no salary data. There is nothing to compute any of them from.

So a new organisation arriving with a full set of impressive figures has told you one of two things: it is older than it looks, or the numbers are decoration. The absence of those numbers is the more reassuring result.

The same test applies to language. "Globally recognised" is a claim about other people's behaviour that nobody can evidence in year one. "Endorsed by industry", with no named endorsement you can check by tonight, is the same claim wearing a coat.

## What a new body cannot borrow

One difference cannot be closed by publishing anything. An established organisation has heard appeals, revised a syllabus under pressure, and survived a cohort that went badly. It was still there when the questions arrived.

A new one has done none of that and cannot claim to have. That belongs in your decision, and the only cure is elapsed time. Anybody telling you otherwise is selling.

## When a new body is the wrong choice

If a client's prequalification names an accredited credential, or a framework agreement lists one, or your employer's policy is written that way, take the accredited route. A procurement condition beats every scope argument ever written, and finding that out a year late is expensive.

If your next grade depends on a certification named in an internal matrix, take the named one and read the rest of this for interest.

If what you want is assessed chartered standing, that is years of supervised practice assessed by a professional route. It proves something an examination cannot, and no examination substitutes for it.

A new body is worth considering when its published scope matches decisions you are already accountable for, and when you can read enough of it in advance to judge that yourself. That case is real, and it is narrower than any founder would like it to be.

## Frequently asked questions

**How new is too new?**
There is no threshold, because age is a proxy for things you can inspect directly. Inspect the syllabus, the pass-standard policy, the separation and the appeal route. Then treat elapsed time as one input among several rather than the whole judgement, and weigh it honestly rather than dismissing it.

**Is an unaccredited credential a red flag?**
Not on its own. Accreditation is third-party assurance that a certification process meets a standard, and obtaining it takes years. Its absence means that assurance has not happened yet, so you do the reading yourself. A body that implies it holds accreditation it does not hold is the red flag.

**What happens to my credential if the body fails?**
Ask what survives it: the published syllabus, an examination record held so a third party can confirm it, and any successor or escrow arrangement. Put the question in writing before you enrol. The answer, or the silence, tells you how far ahead the organisation has thought.

**Does a large syllabus indicate a serious body?**
No. It indicates that a lot has been written down. Seriousness shows in the boundary statements, the pass-standard policy and the appeal route, all of which are short documents and far harder to write honestly than a long list of topics.

**Can I put these questions to a body directly?**
Yes, and how it answers is part of the evidence. Ask in writing, keep the replies, and note how long a considered answer takes to arrive. A body that treats the questions as hostile has answered the most important one.
