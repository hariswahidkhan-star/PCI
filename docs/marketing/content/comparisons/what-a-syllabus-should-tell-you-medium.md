# What a syllabus should tell you before you sign up

*Publish only after the credentialfinder.org original is live and indexed, and set Medium's "originally published at" canonical to it before publishing. Medium links are nofollow; this republish earns qualified readers and model citations rather than link equity.*

**Subtitle:** What a syllabus should tell you is four things, and a topic list is only the first of them.

A syllabus should tell you what will be examined, at what depth, in what proportion, and what it assumes you already know. If it gives you a list of topics and none of the other three, you cannot judge whether the examination fits your work, and no amount of marketing copy will close that gap.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. Weigh what follows accordingly, and check it against whatever syllabus you are actually holding.

## The six checks, and what a missing one costs you

| What to look for | What it lets you decide | What its absence means |
|---|---|---|
| Scope, stated as boundaries | Whether the decisions you sign are inside it | You will find out what was excluded on the day |
| Depth, stated with verbs | Whether you must recall it or defend it | Preparation time is unknowable |
| Proportion across the subject | Where to spend your study hours | You revise evenly and mis-spend half of them |
| Assumed prior knowledge | Whether you can start now or need two more years | The gate is hidden until you sit |
| Sources it draws on | Whether it teaches the discipline or one house method | You cannot tell what the examination treats as correct |
| Explicit exclusions | What somebody else is expected to cover | Every boundary becomes an argument |

Read those six for any credential you are weighing, including the ones on the site this article came from. The exercise takes an evening and it is the cheapest hour of due diligence available.

## Depth is the check people skip

Scope tells you the subject appears. Depth tells you what you will be asked to do with it, and depth is where preparation time actually goes.

A syllabus line reading "estimate at completion" could mean anything. A line reading "select among accepted forecasting methods and justify the assumption" means something specific, and you can price your own study against it.

Take a package with a budget at completion of **£10.0m**. Actual cost is **£4.20m** and earned value is **£3.80m**.

- Cost performance index = 3.80 ÷ 4.20 = **0.905**
- Forecast if that performance persists = 10.0 ÷ 0.905 = **£11.05m**
- Forecast if the overrun is behind you = 4.20 + (10.0 − 3.80) = **£10.40m**

The gap is **£0.65m** on a £10m job, and it comes entirely from an assumption a person chooses.

A recall-depth syllabus is satisfied when you can name both methods. A judgement-depth syllabus is satisfied when you can say which one you would defend to a board and what evidence you would bring. Those are different examinations and different study plans. Working through [a full month of earned value worked through end to end](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/earned-value-worked-example) is a fair way to test which depth you are currently at.

## Assumed prior knowledge is a gate in disguise

Every syllabus assumes something. The good ones say so.

If a document assumes you have run a month-end, sat opposite a commercial manager on a variation, or defended a forecast to somebody who did not want to hear it, that assumption is doing real work in the examination. Candidates without it do not fail on the topics; they fail on the situations.

Where the assumption is stated, you can act on it: sit later, or get the reps first. Where it is not stated, you are guessing, and the guess costs you the whole fee if it is wrong.

## Proportion tells you where the subject's centre is

A list of topics implies they carry equal weight. They almost never do.

Proportion is also the fastest way to see what a credential is really about. Two syllabuses can name the same twenty topics and be entirely different examinations, because one spends most of itself on measurement and the other on reporting.

One caution, and it applies to every body including this one: the proportions of a Body of Knowledge and the weighting of an examination are two different statements. A body may publish the first while the second is still an open decision, and you should ask which one you are reading rather than assume they are the same number.

## What the categories tell you about themselves

Read enough syllabuses and the categories separate cleanly, on their own terms.

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. They are built to examine those thoroughly, and they say so.

Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. Their syllabuses assume the delivery data arrives from somewhere else, and they are explicit about it.

Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment. Breadth is the design, and the syllabus is honest about the trade that breadth requires.

None of that is a shortcoming. Each of those categories is examining its own subject to a standard it publishes. The point of reading the syllabus is to find out which subject you are being examined in, so that the boundary is a decision you made rather than a surprise you received.

## What PCI's syllabus states

The PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI), the PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI) and the PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI) each publish a Body of Knowledge in full, free, before anybody pays anything.

The three run to 13, 16 and 16 domains and 61, 61 and 63 knowledge areas. Across the three volumes there are 92 sector case studies, and 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements sit underneath them, so the requirement being examined is written down rather than implied.

Each Body of Knowledge is proportioned 40% finance and reporting, 40% project management and 20% governed AI — the proportion of the syllabus, not an examination weighting, and PCI publishes no examination weighting because the blueprint is a separate and open decision. A suite of 15,613 machine calculation checks covers the PFL-AI and PML-AI material; PCL-AI has no equivalent suite.

Those are the numbers to check the document against. The method for doing it is the same one you would apply anywhere else, set out in [how to read a Body of Knowledge before you pay](https://credentialfinder.org/how-to-read-a-body-of-knowledge).

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

## When the syllabus is not the deciding document

If your employer or a client's prequalification names a specific certification, the syllabus reading is still worth an evening — you should know what you are about to be examined on — but it is not the decision. Take the one that is named, and read the rest later.

The same applies where a role is regulated, or where a framework agreement lists an accredited credential as a condition. Scope arguments do not win against a procurement requirement, and pretending otherwise wastes your year.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should reading a syllabus take?**
An hour for the structure and the exclusions, an evening if you are comparing two. Read the boundary statements first, then the depth verbs, then the assumed prior knowledge. Topic lists are the least informative part of the document and the part most people read first.

**What if the syllabus is behind a paywall?**
Ask why. A body that publishes its standard openly is inviting you to check it, and a body that will not show you what it examines until you have paid is asking for trust it has not yet given you a way to test. Some publish a detailed outline free and the full text on registration, which is a reasonable middle.

**Does a long syllabus mean a hard examination?**
No. Length measures how much has been written down, not how deeply any of it is assessed. A short syllabus with judgement-level verbs can be a harder paper than a long one built on recall. Read the verbs before the page count.

**How do I know a syllabus is current?**
Look for a version number, a date and a change note. Then check whether the sources it draws on have moved since. A syllabus that cannot tell you when it last changed is telling you something about how it is maintained.

**Should I read the syllabus if my employer is paying?**
Yes, and for the same reason: the fee is not the cost. Your preparation hours are, and the syllabus is what makes those hours estimable. Comparing two credentials properly means running the same six questions past both of them before either invoice is raised.
