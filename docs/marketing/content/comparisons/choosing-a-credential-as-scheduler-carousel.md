# Scheduling to controls: eleven slides on what changes

*LinkedIn document carousel. 11 slides at 1080 × 1350. One idea per slide. Slide 3 carries the
arithmetic, because slide 3 is the one people screenshot.*

---

### Slide 1 — cover
**Moving from scheduling into controls?**
Three things arrive with your signature.
*Alt text: title slide reading "Moving from scheduling into controls? Three things arrive with
your signature".*

### Slide 2 — the three things
An **earning rule**. A **forecast**. A **cut-off date**.

Each produces a number somebody outside delivery relies on.
*Alt text: slide listing the earning rule, the forecast and the cut-off date as the three
additions when a scheduler moves into controls.*

### Slide 3 — the arithmetic
A £1.2m package straddles period end.

50/50 rule → period EV **£3.0m**
0/100 rule → period EV **£2.4m**
Actual cost either way → **£2.8m**

CPI **1.07** or **0.86**. Same steel in the ground.
*Alt text: calculation slide showing a cost performance index of 1.07 under a 50/50 earning rule
and 0.86 under a 0/100 rule for the same month.*

### Slide 4 — why it matters beyond the pack
The index feeds the forecast.

The forecast is the denominator in a percentage complete.

The percentage complete drives what gets recognised.
*Alt text: slide tracing the chain from a cost performance index through a forecast to recognised
revenue.*

### Slide 5 — the cut-off row nobody owns
Progress cut on the 25th. Ledger cut on the 30th.

Five days of cost with no matching earned value, every single month.
*Alt text: slide describing a five-day gap between the progress cut-off and the ledger cut-off.*

### Slide 6 — what the scheduling category examines
Schedule development, maintenance and analysis. Estimating, cost control, planning and the
economics of the asset life cycle.

Examined thoroughly, and built to be.
*Alt text: slide listing what cost and scheduling credentials set out to examine.*

### Slide 7 — where the boundary sits
The earning rule, the forecast handover and the cut-off calendar sit past that boundary.

A scope boundary is a design decision, not a failing.
*Alt text: slide stating that the three additions sit outside the scope of the scheduling
category by design.*

### Slide 8 — the tool question
Product certifications for scheduling software examine operating a tool.

Certification of the discipline is a different claim.

Both are worth holding. Know which one your CV line makes.
*Alt text: slide distinguishing a software product certification from certification of the
discipline the tool serves.*

### Slide 9 — what PCL-AI examines
**13 domains. 61 knowledge areas.**

Measurement, progress and forecasting — and how those numbers become reported numbers.
*Alt text: slide summarising PCL-AI as 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas covering the control
cycle and the reporting handover.*

### Slide 10 — when this is not for you
You like the programme work → stay, and go deep.

A prequalification names a certification → take that one.

You need an accredited credential today → go where accreditation exists.

PCI is a new, independent certifying body, not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other
ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and not claiming to be.
*Alt text: slide listing three situations in which a different route is the right answer, with
PCI's accreditation position stated plainly.*

### Slide 11 — what to do, and the disclosure
Write down your earning rule per work type, and who chose it. Write down both cut-off dates.
Rebuild your CPI as if they matched.

Then read the syllabus — ours is free, in full, before anyone pays.

*credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards PCL-AI.*
*Alt text: closing slide with the ownership disclosure and an instruction to rebuild the index on
matching cut-off dates.*
