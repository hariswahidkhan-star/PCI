# Why do the cost report and the accounts disagree?

Usually because the cost report and the accounts disagree by design rather than by error: they are measuring different things, to different dates, under different rules, and the join between them belongs to nobody in particular. Four artefacts cause almost all of it, and each one is checkable in an afternoon.

**Different dates.** Progress is commonly measured to a delivery cut-off and costs are booked to a finance cut-off, and they are rarely the same day. Earned value to the 25th against actual cost to the 30th produces a performance index that has moved for calendar reasons. Align the dates and the index changes without anybody's productivity changing.

**Accruals.** Work done and not yet invoiced belongs in the cost report. Say earned value for the period is £4.20m and the report shows £4.30m of cost, giving an index of 0.977. Add £0.28m of subcontractor work done and not invoiced, and cost becomes £4.58m, so the index is 0.917. On a £12.0m budget at completion, that is a forecast of £12.28m against £13.09m. The gap is £0.81m and its cause is an invoice that had not arrived.

**The earning rule.** An activity budgeted at £600k and started but not finished earns £300k under a 50/50 rule and nothing under a 0/100 rule. Same work, different convention, different earned value, different forecast.

**The forecast itself.** Percentage complete on a contract is often measured as costs incurred over total expected costs, and the denominator is a delivery forecast. Contract price £26.0m, costs to date £9.0m, estimated total cost £24.0m gives 37.5% complete, revenue of £9.75m and margin of £0.75m. Revise the estimate to £25.5m for ordinary delivery reasons and progress falls to 35.3%, revenue to £9.18m and margin to £0.18m. Reported margin dropped £0.57m because a number owned by delivery moved.

That last one surprises people the most, because nobody made a mistake. A delivery judgement became a reported result through a mechanism neither profession is examined on. And if expected costs pass the contract price, the whole expected loss is recognised as soon as it is known rather than spread over the remaining months, which is why a single revision can land as a large movement.

Who should own it? Somebody named, with authority over both the earning rules and the cut-off calendar, usually sitting in project controls with a direct line into finance. The specific answer matters less than the fact that it has been answered.

It is worth being clear about the professions here. Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis, and go at that subject thoroughly. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control, and assume the delivery data arriving is sound. Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process. None of them is deficient. The join simply belongs to all of them and sits at the centre of none.

If your own work stops at the programme, this is genuinely somebody else's problem and a scheduling-focused credential covers what you actually do.
