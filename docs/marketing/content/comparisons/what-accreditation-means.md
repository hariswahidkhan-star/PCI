# What accreditation means, and what it does not prove

Accreditation is a third party assessing how a certifying body runs its process: impartiality, competence, standard setting, appeals, records. It is assurance about the machinery. It is not a judgement that the syllabus is good, that employers want the credential, or that it suits you. Both halves of that matter when you are deciding.

> **Who publishes this page.** credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. PCI is not accredited, which is a reason to read this page sceptically and a reason it is written carefully.

## What the word actually describes

Personnel certification has an international standard covering how a body should operate: how it separates training from the certification decision, how it manages conflicts of interest, how it sets and reviews a pass mark, how it handles appeals, and what records it keeps.

An accreditation body assesses a certifying body against that standard, on a defined scope, and re-assesses periodically. That assessment is what the word "accredited" points at.

Note what is being examined. The assessors look at the process. They do not sit the paper, and they do not rule on whether the syllabus covers the right subject for your job.

| Accreditation assures | Accreditation does not assure |
|---|---|
| That impartiality risks are identified and managed | That the syllabus is well chosen for your role |
| That the people who prepare candidates do not decide the outcome | That the examination is intellectually demanding |
| That a pass mark is set by a defined method and reviewed | That the credential is recognised by any employer |
| That appeals and complaints have a route and a timescale | That holding it changes your pay or your prospects |
| That records, security and confidentiality are managed | That the body will still be here in ten years |

Both columns are worth having. The mistake is reading the first as though it were the second.

## The claims you will see, and what each one means

The word appears in several forms, and they are not equivalent.

| The claim | What it usually means | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| "Accredited certification body" | An accreditation body has assessed the certification process against a standard, on a stated scope | Which accreditation body, and does the scope schedule name this specific scheme? |
| "Accredited training provider" | Somebody has approved the organisation to deliver a course, often another body's course | Approved by whom, and does it cover the certificate or only the teaching? |
| "Recognised by industry" | Usually a marketing statement with no assessment behind it | Recognised by whom, evidenced how? |
| "Endorsed" or "in partnership with" | A commercial or content arrangement, which may be real | What does the arrangement actually cover? |
| "Certified to a quality management standard" | The organisation's management system was audited | That is about the company, not about your credential |
| "Internationally recognised" | Nothing, in itself | Ask for one named organisation that requires it |

None of these is inherently dishonest. They are simply different claims, and they are frequently displayed together in a way that invites a reader to treat them as one.

## How to check an accreditation claim in five minutes

**Find the accreditation body's own register.** Search their site for the certifying body's name. If the body is accredited, it appears there; if it does not appear, the logo on the website is decoration.

**Read the scope schedule, not the certificate.** Accreditation is granted for a defined scope. A body can be accredited for one scheme and not another, and the certificate looks identical either way. The scope schedule names the schemes.

**Check the dates.** Accreditation lapses, is suspended and is withdrawn. A current certificate is the only one that means anything.

**Separate the entity from the scheme.** A parent organisation's accreditation does not transfer to a subsidiary or to a new product line unless the scope says so.

Doing this on a credential you are considering takes less time than reading its brochure, and it is the only version of the check that is worth anything. Applying the same discipline to a certification's actual claims is set out in [what a project controls certification proves](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/project-controls-certification).

## What PCI does not claim

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

There is no partial version of that statement worth making. A body is accredited on a scope or it is not, and language such as "built to the standard", used without the plain sentence above beside it, is designed to be misheard.

What PCI does instead is publish the material an accreditation assessment would look at: the Body of Knowledge in full and free, the separation between preparation and the certification decision, and the appeal route. That is not accreditation and it is not offered as a substitute. It is the evidence a reader can check without waiting for anybody.

## If you need accreditation, take an accredited credential

Some readers have no decision to make. If a tender requires an accredited credential, if an internal policy names one, or if a regulator does, then the accredited route is the answer and no scope argument changes that.

Say the same thing to any body that tries to talk you out of a hard requirement. A certifying body that argues its way around your employer's policy is telling you how it will behave when something else is inconvenient.

For the reader whose requirement is softer, the next question is how to assess a body that has not yet been assessed by anyone else. [How to judge a certifying body that is new](https://credentialfinder.org/judging-a-certifying-body-that-is-new) sets out the tests that do not depend on age, and [how to verify a credential](https://credentialfinder.org/how-to-verify-a-credential) covers checking a claim that somebody actually holds one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is an unaccredited credential worthless?**
No, and it is not automatically worth much either. It is worth what its examination and published syllabus can demonstrate, and you have to check that yourself rather than relying on somebody else's assessment. That is more work. Where a requirement names accreditation, the unaccredited credential does not satisfy it, whatever its content.

**Can a body accredit itself?**
No. Accreditation is by definition an assessment by an independent third party. A body that has written its own standard and declared itself compliant has published a policy, not an accreditation, and the wording usually gives it away: look for the name of the assessing organisation and the date.

**Does accreditation mean the examination is hard?**
It means the process for setting the standard is documented and reviewed. Difficulty follows from the syllabus, the item writing and the cut score, which are content decisions. An accredited examination can be easy and an unaccredited one demanding; the assessment does not rank them.

**How long does accreditation take to obtain?**
Long enough that a new body cannot have it, which is a structural fact rather than an excuse. Any body claiming accreditation shortly after launch should be asked for the register entry and the scope schedule, and the register is public.
