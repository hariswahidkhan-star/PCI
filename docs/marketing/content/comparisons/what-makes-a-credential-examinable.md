# What makes a credential examinable, and what does not

A credential is examinable when every statement in its syllabus describes something a candidate can be observed doing, within a boundary, with an answer that can be defended against challenge. Statements about awareness, appreciation or importance cannot be examined, only asserted. That single test sorts most of the market in ten minutes.

> **Who publishes this page.** credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. Apply the tests below to PCI's own syllabus first; if they do not survive contact with it, they are not worth applying to anyone else.

## The test, in one line per syllabus statement

Read any line of a syllabus and ask: *could two competent examiners agree on whether a candidate got this right?* If not, it is a topic heading rather than an examinable objective.

| Syllabus statement | Examinable? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Understand the importance of stakeholder engagement" | No | No observable behaviour, no boundary, no defensible right answer |
| "Be aware of current developments in artificial intelligence" | No | Unbounded, and it dates the moment it is written |
| "Calculate an estimate at completion by each of the four accepted methods and state the assumption each one makes" | Yes | Bounded, observable, one defensible answer set |
| "Given a contract and a set of progress data, select the defensible earning rule and justify the choice" | Yes | Has a right answer under stated conditions, and a wrong one |
| "Appreciate the role of governance" | No | Cannot be marked, only agreed with |
| "Identify what a model may not decide in a given reporting scenario, and what evidence must be retained" | Yes | Scenario-bounded, with a defensible answer |

A syllabus of the first kind can still support a good course. It cannot support an examination, because there is nothing to mark.

## The four properties that make a statement examinable

**Observable.** It names something the candidate does: calculates, selects, identifies, reconciles, justifies. Verbs like "understand" and "appreciate" describe a state nobody can see.

**Bounded.** The conditions are stated, so the candidate knows what is in play. "Given a contract, a data date and a cost ledger" is bounded; "in the modern project environment" is not.

**Defensible.** There is a correct answer, and the body can explain why the other options are wrong. An item that cannot survive a candidate asking "why is B wrong?" should not be on the paper.

**Levelled.** The syllabus says whether you must recall the rule, apply it, or judge between competing applications. Those are three different examinations of the same content, and a syllabus that never distinguishes them is telling you it has not been blueprinted.

## What a blueprint is, and why its absence matters

A blueprint maps every examination item back to a syllabus statement and a cognitive level, so the paper covers the scope in known proportions rather than in whatever proportions the item writers happened to enjoy.

Without one, coverage drifts towards whatever is easiest to write items about, which is usually definitions. Candidates then prepare for definitions, and the credential slowly stops meaning what its syllabus says.

Note the distinction that matters when you read any published proportions. A Body of Knowledge proportion describes how much of the syllabus a subject occupies. An examination weighting describes how much of the paper it occupies. They are different numbers and a body should say which one it is publishing. PCI publishes the first — 40% finance and reporting, 40% project management, 20% governed AI — and does not publish the second, because the exam blueprint remains an open decision.

## How a body knows its items are working

Once a paper has been sat, each item can be checked against the candidates who answered it. Here is the arithmetic, on an illustrative cohort — these are made-up figures for a hypothetical item, not any credential's data.

Take **200 candidates**. Rank them by total score and compare the top 27% with the bottom 27%, which is **54 candidates** in each group.

On item 14, **48 of the top group** answer correctly (88.9%) and **47 of the bottom group** answer correctly (87.0%).

Discrimination = 0.889 − 0.870 = **0.019**.

An item that strong candidates and weak candidates answer at almost the same rate is not measuring anything. It might be ambiguous, it might have two defensible answers, or it might test a fact everybody memorises. A body with a functioning review process pulls it, rewrites it, and says so.

Now compare item 22: **44 of the top group** correct (81.5%) and **19 of the bottom group** (35.2%). Discrimination = **0.463**. That item is separating candidates who know the material from those who do not, which is the entire job.

You will not usually be shown these numbers. You can ask whether the body produces them, what it does when an item fails review, and whether any candidate's result has ever been amended as a result. The answer tells you whether an examination is being maintained or merely reprinted. ## What to do with this before you pay

Open the syllabus. Take ten statements at random. Score each one against observable, bounded, defensible and levelled.

If eight of ten pass, you are looking at something that can be examined. If two of ten pass, you are looking at a course outline, however impressive the branding around it. [How to read a Body of Knowledge before you pay](https://credentialfinder.org/how-to-read-a-body-of-knowledge) works through a full reading, and [how a pass mark is set](https://credentialfinder.org/how-a-pass-mark-is-set) covers the other half of the question, which is what your score is compared against.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a multiple-choice examination test real competence?**
A badly written one tests recall. A well-written scenario item gives you a situation, four defensible-looking options and one best answer, which is much closer to the decisions you are paid to make. The format matters less than whether the items were blueprinted, reviewed and analysed after use.

**Is a portfolio better than an examination?**
It is different. A portfolio shows what a candidate has actually done and is strong evidence of practice; it is harder to standardise, because no two candidates submit the same work. An examination standardises the content and the conditions. Some routes use both, for exactly that reason.

**Why does the cognitive level matter to me as a candidate?**
Because it tells you how to prepare. If the syllabus asks you to apply and judge, memorising definitions will not carry you, and a course built on definitions has mis-sold you the preparation. The level is also what makes the credential mean something to an employer.

**Can I tell any of this from the marketing page?**
Rarely. The marketing page will list benefits. The syllabus lists objectives, and only the objectives can be tested against the four properties. If the syllabus is not published in full before payment, that is itself an answer to the question you were asking.
