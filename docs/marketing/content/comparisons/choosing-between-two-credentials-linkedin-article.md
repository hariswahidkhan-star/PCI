# How to choose between two credentials, done properly

Choosing between two credentials means putting both through the same six questions: what each examines, what each assumes you already know, who decides the result, what it costs in hours rather than fees, what changes on the day you hold it, and what happens if you fail. Score the answers against the decisions you personally sign. If they still tie, choose for the job you will hold in two years.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials mentioned below. A ranking of credentials published by a body that awards three of them would be worth nothing, so what follows is a method, with the weights left where they belong.

## Why no honest league table exists

A ranking has to assume one buyer. Certification has millions of them, each signing a different set of decisions, and the ordering flips depending on which set you hold.

A ranking also has to assume the credentials stay still. Syllabuses are revised on their owners' schedules, so any published order is a photograph of a year rather than a fact.

And a ranking has to come from somewhere. Whoever publishes one is usually selling an entry, or selling the preparation for it. That includes this Institute, which is why you will not find an ordering here.

What survives all three problems is a method you run yourself in an evening.

## The six rows

| Row | What a good answer looks like | Why the row is there |
|---|---|---|
| What is examined, and at what depth? | A published syllabus you can read before paying, specific enough to argue with | This is the only row that describes what you will actually know |
| What does it assume you already know? | A stated entry expectation, in years and in subject matter | An examination pitched above you costs evenings; one pitched below you costs the fee |
| Who decides the result? | The certification decision held apart from whoever sells the preparation | Where one organisation takes the fee and awards the result, the evidence is weaker whatever the syllabus says |
| What does it cost in hours? | Your own honest estimate of study weeks | The fee is the visible number and rarely the deciding one |
| What changes when you hold it? | A pay band, an approval limit, a client-facing role — or nothing | "Nothing" is a legitimate answer, and it is better found now than in week nine |
| What happens if you fail? | A published resit route and a written appeal process | A scheme with no published appeal has not thought about being wrong |

## Row one, done properly: count the decisions you sign

Write out every decision you put your name to in a normal month. A forecast. A valuation. A progress percentage. A programme reissue. A change-order recommendation. An accrual. Eight to twelve lines is typical.

Now read both syllabuses against your list and mark each line examined, assumed or excluded. Then count the examined lines.

That turns a preference into a number, and the number usually decides it before you reach row four. It also catches the expensive error nobody warns you about: holding two credentials that examine nearly the same subject, and none of the subject that has moved into your week since you last chose.

## Compare like with like, at category level

Half the confusion in these decisions comes from comparing across a boundary, as though two credentials in different categories were competing versions of one thing.

| Category | What it examines | What it leaves to a different examination | Who it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost and scheduling credentials | Estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis | The accounting treatment of what has been measured | Somebody who owns the cost report or the programme |
| The established project controls certifications | Measurement, progress, forecasting and the control of delivery | The reported result those forecasts eventually produce | Somebody running an integrated controls function |
| Accountancy and finance qualifications | Recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control | How the delivery data was produced before it arrived | Somebody moving into a finance function |
| Audit credentials | Governance, risk, control and the assurance process | The day-to-day production of the numbers being assured | Somebody whose job is assurance rather than delivery |
| Project management certifications | Managing projects across people, process and the business environment | Depth in any single control discipline | Somebody accountable for a whole project |
| Product certifications for scheduling software | Operating a specific tool competently | The discipline the tool exists to serve | Somebody who needs tool fluency on the record |
| Chartered routes in surveying and engineering | Assessed professional competence over years of supervised practice | The single-sitting examination model entirely | Somebody who wants chartered standing |

None of those boundaries is a shortcoming. Each category publishes what it examines, goes at that subject in depth, and leaves the rest to somebody with a different examination. Where your two candidates sit in different rows, the count of signed decisions is nearly always decisive on its own.

## Row four, worked: the hours are the real cost

Fees are the visible number. Preparation is the one that lands on your evenings, and you have to estimate it yourself.

Suppose credential A extends work you already do daily and you judge it at **60 hours**. Credential B covers a subject half of which is new to you, so you judge it at **140 hours**.

At **four hours a week** — one weekday evening and part of a Sunday, which is what a full-time role actually leaves — A takes 60 ÷ 4 = **15 weeks** and B takes 140 ÷ 4 = **35 weeks**.

If your real availability is two hours a week, the same estimates give **30 weeks** and **70 weeks**. Seventy weeks is not a study plan. It is a decision to reorganise a year, and it should be taken as one.

At 35 weeks, ask whether the subject you are preparing for will still be the subject you need. If your role is moving, the long route can be right anyway.

## The tie-breaks, in order

**The two-year test.** Credentials are slow to earn and slower to be recognised. Aim at the work you will be doing, not the work you did last year.

**Independence.** Where the organisation selling the preparation also awards the result, the evidence is weaker. Ask how the two are separated and who sits on the decision.

**Outside verification.** Try to confirm a stranger's credential in that scheme today, without an account. If you cannot, neither can a hiring manager in four years.

**Read-before-you-pay.** A syllabus published in full is a body inviting you to check its work before taking your money.

## When the grid is not the decision

If one of your two is named in a client's prequalification, an employer's grading matrix or a framework agreement, take the named one and stop. Requirements outrank fit, and no grid was ever going to change the requirement.

If a procurement rule in your market requires an accredited credential, that settles it too, and it settles it against this Institute today. Say so out loud before spending an evening on rows two to six.

And if one of the two is a chartered route and what you want is chartered standing, the grid does not apply. That is an assessment of professional competence built over years of supervised practice, and no examination substitutes for it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I just take both?**
Sometimes, in sequence, and only where they genuinely examine different subjects. Two credentials covering the same ground is a common and expensive duplication. Take the one that unblocks you now, and let the second follow the direction your role is moving in.

**What if I cannot tell what a syllabus examines?**
That is an answer in itself. Write to the body with three specific questions about depth on the topics you sign for, and judge how fast and how concretely they reply. A body that cannot describe its own coverage in an email has told you something the brochure did not.

**Does the harder examination make the better credential?**
No. Difficulty is not relevance, and a hard paper on material you never touch is a hard paper you did not need. Match the depth to the decisions you sign, then let difficulty land where it lands.

**My employer will fund one. Does that change the method?**
Only the fee row. The hours are still yours, and so is the row about what changes when you hold it. Funded candidates give up at the same point as everybody else, usually because that last row was always going to say nothing.

**How do I judge recognition without a ranking?**
Read ten current advertisements for the job you want next, in the market you want it in, and note what they ask for. That is your own recognition data, gathered in twenty minutes, and it beats any general claim about what employers prefer, including mine.
