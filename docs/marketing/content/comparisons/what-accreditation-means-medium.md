# What accreditation means, and what it does not mean

Accreditation means a third party has assessed how a certifying body works — its impartiality, how it writes and reviews examination questions, how it sets a defensible pass standard, who makes the certification decision, how appeals are heard — against a published standard, on a repeating cycle. It says the machinery has been inspected by somebody with no stake in the outcome. It says nothing whatever about whether the syllabus covers your job.

Those are two different questions, and most candidates collapse them into one. Keeping them apart is the whole of this piece.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. PCI is not accredited, which makes this a subject on which the Institute has an obvious interest — so the piece is written to give an accredited credential its full due, and it does.

## The two axes

| | Scope fits the work you do | Scope does not fit your work |
|---|---|---|
| **Accredited** | The straightforward case. Independent assurance on the process, and the subject you need. | A sound process examining somebody else's job. Common, and a real waste of a year. |
| **Not accredited** | You have to do the process assessment yourself, from the published documents, and you should. | No. |

Candidates who ask only "is it accredited?" answer one axis and guess at the other. The second axis is the one that decides whether the credential changes your working week, and the reason it gets skipped is set out in [scope versus prestige](https://credentialfinder.org/scope-versus-prestige).

## What accreditation actually assesses

The subject of an accreditation assessment is the certifying body, not the discipline. In broad terms an assessor examines:

**Impartiality.** Whether the body has a structural interest in candidates passing, and what it does about it. The sharpest case is training: a body that both prepares candidates and certifies them has an incentive problem that has to be managed and shown to be managed.

**Examination development.** How questions come into existence, who reviews them, how they are trialled, how a defective question is found and retired.

**The standard.** Whether the pass standard is arrived at by a defensible method rather than a round number chosen because it looked reasonable.

**The decision.** Who certifies, on what evidence, and whether the person who taught the candidate has any part in it.

**Appeals.** Whether an unhappy candidate reaches people who were not involved in the original decision.

**Records and data.** How results, identities and evidence are kept, for how long, and who can see them.

**Review.** Whether the scheme is revisited on a stated cycle rather than when somebody remembers.

That is a serious list. A body that has passed an assessment against it has been through something demanding, and the credential is better for it.

## What accreditation does not assess

**Whether the syllabus is the right one for you.** An accreditation body is not a subject-matter authority in cost engineering, financial reporting or scheduling. It assesses process integrity, and it is scrupulous about not straying into content.

**Difficulty.** Nothing in the assessment says the examination is hard, and nothing says it is easy.

**Market acceptance.** Whether an employer, a client or a framework recognises the credential is a commercial fact about a market, decided by that market.

**Currency.** Accreditation checks that a review cycle exists and is followed. It does not check that the content is the best available account of a fast-moving subject.

**Scope.** The single most important property of a credential, and the one accreditation is silent on by design.

## A worked month that no accreditation would catch

Scope matters because work does not arrive labelled by discipline. Here is one month, in figures you can check.

A fixed-price contract at £30.0m, with revenue measured by progress on a cost-to-cost basis.

At last close, expected total cost was £25.0m and cost incurred was £10.0m. Progress = 10.0 ÷ 25.0 = 40%. Cumulative revenue = 0.40 × 30.0 = **£12.0m**. Expected margin = 30.0 − 25.0 = **£5.0m**.

This month, £1.0m of cost is incurred, and the delivery team reforecasts the remaining work: expected total cost becomes £27.5m. Progress = 11.0 ÷ 27.5 = **40%**. Cumulative revenue = 0.40 × 30.0 = **£12.0m**, unchanged.

So the month books **£1.0m of cost and no revenue at all**, and the expected margin has halved from £5.0m to £2.5m. Nobody made an error. The denominator grew in step with the numerator, and a perfectly ordinary reforecast produced a month that looks like a disaster on the profit and loss and like nothing at all on the progress curve.

Which credential examines that month? Cost and scheduling credentials examine the estimate that moved the denominator, and examine it thoroughly. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine the measure that turns progress into revenue, and examine that thoroughly. Each is built for its own side and does that job properly. The month sits in the join, and the join belongs to neither by design.

No accreditation assessment would find that gap, because finding it is not what accreditation is for. Where the join sits, and why it is where the money goes, is set out in [the finance and delivery overlap](https://credentialfinder.org/the-finance-and-delivery-overlap).

## Where PCI stands, stated plainly

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

Building with reference to a standard is not the same as being assessed against it by an independent party, and this Institute will not blur the two. What PCI does instead is put the machinery where it can be inspected: [the certification scheme](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/certification-scheme) is published, along with 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements, and the Body of Knowledge is readable in full before anyone pays — 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas for PCL-AI, 16 and 61 for PFL-AI, 16 and 63 for PML-AI.

That is evidence you can weigh yourself. It is not third-party assurance, and it does not pretend to be.

## When you should take the accredited credential instead

Say a regulator, a client framework or a public tender requires an accredited certification. Take the accredited one. That is a requirement, not a preference, and PCI cannot meet it today.

Same answer if your employer mandates a specific certification, if a professional route you are already committed to expects a particular qualification, or if an immigration or licensing process names a category of credential. In all those cases the decision has been made for you and reading syllabuses is a hobby, not a decision.

Where the choice is genuinely yours, the two axes both apply, and the second one — scope — is the one that changes what you can do in March.

## FAQ

**Is an unaccredited credential worthless?**
No, and neither is it equivalent to an accredited one. Accreditation is independent assurance on process, and without it a candidate has to do that assessment personally from the published documents. That is real work and some people will reasonably decline to do it. What an unaccredited credential cannot do is satisfy a rule that requires accreditation, and no amount of syllabus quality changes that.

**Does accreditation mean the examination is hard?**
It means the standard was set by a defensible method and applied consistently, which is a different property. A well-run examination can sit at any depth. Difficulty is a function of the syllabus and the level, not of who assessed the body's processes, and no accreditation mark carries information about it either way.

**Can a body claim to follow a standard without being accredited to it?**
It can say it built the scheme with reference to the standard's principles, and that is a meaningful statement about design intent. It must not present that as accreditation, or use wording that suggests approval already granted. The distinction is the plainest test of whether a certifying body is telling you the truth about itself.

**How do I assess process myself if there is no accreditation?**
Read what the body publishes about its own machinery rather than its syllabus: how examination questions are developed and retired, who makes the certification decision, who hears an appeal, how long records are kept, when the scheme is reviewed. A body that publishes all of that has handed you the material. A body that describes it in a paragraph has not.

**Does accreditation ever say anything about scope?**
Only that the scheme has defined its scope and works within it. It does not judge whether that scope is the right one for a role, an industry or a decade. Scope fit is the candidate's judgement, always, and it is the axis with the larger effect on whether the credential is worth the year.
