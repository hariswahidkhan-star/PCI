# Accreditation certifies the process, not the syllabus

"Is it accredited?" is a good question with a narrow answer. Accreditation means a third party has assessed how a certifying body operates — impartiality, examination development, the pass standard, the certification decision, appeals, records — against a published standard, on a repeating cycle. It is an inspection of the machinery. It is silent, deliberately, on whether the syllabus covers the work you do.

The question most candidates meant to ask is the second one. This piece separates them.

A disclosure, since this Institute has an obvious interest in the subject: credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials. PCI is not accredited. That is exactly why the accredited route gets its full due below, and it deserves it.

## Seven things an accreditation assessment looks at

**Impartiality.** Whether the body has a structural interest in candidates passing, and what it does about it. The sharpest version is training: a body that both prepares and certifies has an incentive problem, and it has to be managed and shown to be managed.

**Examination development.** How a question comes into existence, who reviews it, how it is trialled, how a defective one is identified and retired.

**The pass standard.** Whether it was reached by a defensible method rather than by choosing a round number that sounded reasonable.

**The certification decision.** Who makes it, on what evidence, and whether anybody who trained the candidate takes part.

**Appeals.** Whether an unhappy candidate reaches people with no involvement in the original decision.

**Records and candidate data.** What is kept, for how long, and who can see it.

**Scheme review.** Whether the scheme is revisited on a stated cycle rather than when somebody remembers.

That is a demanding list. A body that has been assessed against it and passed has done something real, and the credential is better for it. Nothing in this piece argues otherwise.

## Five things it does not look at

**Whether the syllabus suits you.** An accreditation body is not a subject-matter authority in cost engineering, financial reporting or scheduling, and is careful not to behave as one.

**Difficulty.** No accreditation mark carries information about how hard an examination is, in either direction.

**Market acceptance.** Whether a client, an employer or a framework recognises the credential is a fact about a market.

**Currency of content.** The assessment checks that a review cycle exists and is followed, not that the material is the best available account of a subject that is moving quickly.

**Scope.** The property with the largest effect on whether a credential changes your working week, and the one accreditation says nothing about by design.

## One month an accreditation assessment would never find

Scope matters because the work does not arrive labelled by discipline. Here is a month, with figures you can check.

A £30.0m fixed-price contract. Original budget at completion £26.0m. At cut-off, earned value on the base scope is £10.4m, which is 40% complete, and actual cost is £11.6m.

Of that £11.6m, £1.2m relates to an instructed change with no agreed price yet.

Carry the unpriced change inside the reported figures and the cost performance index is 10.4 ÷ 11.6 = **0.897**. Hold it outside, against the base scope only, and the index is 10.4 ÷ 10.4 = **1.000**.

**The entire apparent overrun is one instruction nobody has priced.** Both presentations are defensible. Which is right depends on a rule that lives half in the cost procedure and half in the accounting policy.

Meanwhile the same £1.2m has a second life. Revenue on an unpriced variation is recognised only to the extent it is highly probable there will be no significant reversal. Until that test is met, the month carries £1.2m of cost against no matching revenue, and the margin the board sees moves for a reason that has nothing to do with productivity.

Cost and scheduling credentials examine the estimating and the control of that spend, thoroughly, because that is their subject. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine the recognition test, thoroughly, for the same reason. Each does its own side properly and by design. The reconciliation between them belongs to neither, and it is a monthly event on most large contracts.

No accreditation assessment would find that gap, because finding it is not what accreditation is for. Why the join is where the money goes is set out in [the finance and delivery overlap](https://credentialfinder.org/the-finance-and-delivery-overlap).

## Where PCI stands, and what it will not say

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

Building with reference to a standard is not the same as being assessed against it by an independent party. Those are different things and this Institute will not blur them into a phrase that sounds like approval already granted.

What PCI does instead is publish the machinery so it can be inspected directly: [the certification scheme](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/certification-scheme), 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements, and the full Body of Knowledge before anybody pays anything — 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas for PCL-AI, 16 and 61 for PFL-AI, 16 and 63 for PML-AI. That is evidence a reader can weigh. It is not third-party assurance and it is not offered as a substitute for it.

## When to take the accredited credential instead

If a regulator, a client framework or a public tender requires an accredited certification, take the accredited one. That is a requirement rather than a preference, and PCI cannot meet it today.

The same applies where an employer mandates a specific certification, where a professional route you have already committed to expects a particular qualification, or where a licensing or visa process names a category of credential. In each of those cases the decision has already been made for you, and comparing syllabuses is a pastime rather than a choice.

Where the choice is genuinely yours, both axes apply — has the process been independently assessed, and does the scope match the work — and the second one is the one you feel every month.

## FAQ

**Is an unaccredited credential worthless?**
No, and it is not equivalent to an accredited one either. Without accreditation, the process assessment falls to the candidate, using whatever the body publishes about its own machinery. That is real work, and some people will sensibly decline to do it. What an unaccredited credential cannot do is satisfy a rule that requires accreditation.

**Does accreditation tell me the examination is rigorous?**
It tells you the standard was set by a defensible method and applied consistently, which is not the same as demanding. Rigour of process and depth of content are independent properties. A carefully run examination can sit at an introductory level, and an unaccredited one can be searching. Neither fact is carried by the mark.

**How do I assess the process myself when there is no accreditation?**
Read what the body publishes about itself rather than about its subject: how questions are developed and retired, who makes the certification decision, who hears an appeal, how long records are kept, when the scheme is next reviewed. A body that publishes those documents has given you the material to judge it. One that summarises them in a paragraph has not. The full method is in [judging a certifying body that is new](https://credentialfinder.org/judging-a-certifying-body-that-is-new).

**Is "built with reference to a standard" a meaningful claim?**
It is meaningful about design intent and nothing more. It says the scheme was drafted against the principles of a recognised standard. It does not say anyone independent has checked that. A body that keeps those two statements apart in its own copy is telling you something useful about how it will behave on harder questions.

**If accreditation is silent on scope, what do I use instead?**
The published syllabus, read properly, and the scheme documents. Trace one topic you already know from end to end, count the verbs, look for the exclusions, and see whether the join between disciplines appears anywhere. That takes an evening and answers the axis accreditation was never designed to address.
