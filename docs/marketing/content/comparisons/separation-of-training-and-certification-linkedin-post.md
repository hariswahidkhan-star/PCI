# Nobody should mark the candidate they sold a course to

*LinkedIn text post — 1,780 characters. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post body:**

The organisation that sold you the course should not be the organisation that decides whether you passed.

That is the whole argument for keeping preparation and the certification decision apart. It is not an argument against training. Good preparation is worth paying for, and most people need some.

It is an argument about who signs the decision.

Five questions you can answer in ten minutes on any scheme's own website:

1. Can you read the whole syllabus before you pay anything?
2. Is a named course required before you are allowed to sit?
3. Does the body suggest, in any wording, that its own preparation improves your odds?
4. Who looks again at a decision you disagree with, and did that person make it?
5. Is there a published impartiality policy that says what happens when those interests pull against each other?

Separation only works if the questions have defensible answers. Take one.

EV £4.8m. AC £5.6m. BAC £24.0m.
CPI = 4.8 ÷ 5.6 = 0.857
EAC = BAC ÷ CPI = 24.0 ÷ 0.857 = £28.0m

£28.0m, or the answer is wrong. Nobody is talked round on that by who taught the candidate. An item with one right answer is what makes independence practical rather than a slogan.

The honest cost of the separation: a body that publishes its syllabus in full hands its material to everybody, earns nothing from preparation, and cannot promise you a route through. Candidates who want a taught path have to assemble one.

And if your employer mandates a particular certification, the mandate decides and none of this changes it.
