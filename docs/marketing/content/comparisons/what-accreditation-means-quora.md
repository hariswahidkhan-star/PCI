# Is an unaccredited certification worth anything at all?

It depends on which question you are actually asking, because there are two and they get collapsed into one.

Accreditation means a third party has assessed **how a certifying body operates**, against a published standard, on a repeating cycle. Scope means **what the credential examines**. Accreditation is silent on scope by design, and scope is usually the thing that decides whether the credential changes your working week.

A disclosure before the argument: credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials mentioned at the end. PCI is not accredited, so treat everything below as an interested party trying to give the accredited route its full due — which it deserves.

**What an accreditation assessment covers**

Impartiality, including how a body manages the incentive problem when it both trains and certifies. How examination questions are developed, reviewed, trialled and retired. Whether the pass standard was set by a defensible method. Who makes the certification decision and on what evidence. Whether appeals reach people not involved the first time. How records and candidate data are held. Whether the scheme is reviewed on a stated cycle.

That is a demanding list, and a body that has passed against it has done something substantial.

**What it does not cover**

Whether the syllabus suits your job. How hard the examination is. Whether an employer or framework will accept it. Whether the content is current in a fast-moving subject. And scope, on which an accreditation body says nothing, because it is not a subject-matter authority in cost engineering, financial reporting or scheduling, and is careful not to act as one.

**So the answer, in four cases**

Accredited and matching your work: the straightforward case.

Accredited and not matching your work: a sound process examining somebody else's job. It happens often and it costs a year.

Not accredited and matching your work: worth something, but you have to do the process assessment personally, from the body's published scheme documents rather than its marketing. Some people will reasonably decline that work.

Not accredited and not matching your work: no.

**The one case where none of this applies**

If a regulator, a client framework or a public tender requires an accredited credential, take the accredited one. Same if an employer mandates a specific certification. That is a requirement rather than a preference, and no syllabus quality substitutes for it.

**How to assess a process yourself**

Read what a body publishes about its own machinery, not about its subject: how questions are developed and retired, who decides, who hears appeals, how long records are kept, when the scheme is next reviewed. A body that publishes those documents has handed you the material. One that summarises them in a paragraph has not.

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything. Building with reference to a standard is not the same as being assessed against it by an independent party, and the difference is worth insisting on whoever is claiming it.
