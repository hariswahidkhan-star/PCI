# What accreditation means and what it does not: 7 posts

*X / Threads thread — 7 posts, each under 280 characters and each able to stand alone. No link in the thread itself; the link goes in the first reply. X counts any URL as 23 characters.*

**Post 1/7 — the hook** (183 characters)
Two cost performance indices for the same month. 0.897 and 1.000. Both defensible.

The difference is £1.2m of instructed change nobody has priced. This is a post about accreditation.

**Post 2/7 — the figures** (175 characters)
£30.0m fixed price. Original budget at completion £26.0m.

At cut-off: earned value on base scope £10.4m (40%), actual cost £11.6m, of which £1.2m is the unpriced instruction.

**Post 3/7 — the arithmetic** (155 characters)
Change carried inside the figures: 10.4 ÷ 11.6 = 0.897.
Held outside, base scope only: 10.4 ÷ 10.4 = 1.000.

The whole apparent overrun is one instruction.

**Post 4/7 — what accreditation is** (222 characters)
A third party assessing how a certifying body works: impartiality, how questions are developed and retired, how the pass standard is set, who decides, who hears appeals, records, review cycle.

Demanding, and worth having.

**Post 5/7 — what it is not** (213 characters)
It says nothing about scope. An accreditation body is not a subject-matter authority in cost engineering or financial reporting and does not act like one.

None of it assesses whether a syllabus covers that £1.2m.

**Post 6/7 — two axes** (213 characters)
Has the process been independently assessed? Does the scope match the work you do?

Ask only the first and you have answered half. If a regulator or a tender requires accreditation, take the accredited credential.
