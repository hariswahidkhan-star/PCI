# Two margins, one month, and the rule nobody re-reads

*LinkedIn text post — 1,891 characters including hashtags. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

Same month, same job, same ledger. £1.5m of margin or £0.4m.

The difference is which measure of progress the contract set up, probably years before you arrived.

The figures. Contract price £20.0m. Cost incurred to date £6.0m. Expected total cost £16.0m.

Measure progress by cost incurred against expected total cost: 6.0 ÷ 16.0 = **37.5%**. Revenue 0.375 × 20.0 = £7.5m. Margin = 7.5 − 6.0 = **£1.5m**.

Measure it by output — units installed, milestones certified — and the delivery team's figure is **32%**. Revenue 0.32 × 20.0 = £6.4m. Margin = 6.4 − 6.0 = **£0.4m**.

Both are permitted. Neither is an accounting error.

**£1.1m of reported margin, decided by a choice made once, by somebody who has usually left.**

This is why the gap for a project accountant is rarely accounting. If you are qualified, recognition and disclosure are examined already. The exposure is upstream, in numbers you did not build: is the estimate current, was the progress earned, is the accrual complete.

Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control — properly, and at depth. They assume competent delivery data arrives from a process they do not set out to examine.

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis, which is how that 37.5% and that £16.0m get built in the first place.

Neither category is deficient. They were designed to examine different things, and a project accountant lives exactly where the two meet.

If you are progressing inside a finance function, finish that qualification. A project-side credential does not substitute for a stage of it.
