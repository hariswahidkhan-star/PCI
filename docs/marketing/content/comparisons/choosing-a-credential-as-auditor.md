# Choosing a credential as an auditor moving into projects

Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process, and that transfers to capital projects intact. What does not come with it is the technical subject the controls are meant to govern: how progress is measured, how a forecast is built, and which numbers can be tested against evidence rather than against a process. That is the gap worth closing, and it is smaller than a second profession.

> **Who publishes this page.** credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute
> Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. The page argues
> for a narrow addition to what you hold, and it has an interest in doing so.

## What transfers, and what does not

| What the engagement needs | Covered by audit credentials | The technical part underneath it |
|---|---|---|
| Scoping and risk assessment | Yes, directly | Which project numbers are actually volatile |
| Control design and operating effectiveness | Yes, directly | What a control over progress measurement looks like |
| Sampling and evidence standards | Yes, directly | What counts as evidence for earned progress |
| Reporting findings and follow-up | Yes, directly | Whether the recommendation is technically workable |
| Testing a forecast | Partly | How a forecast is built and where judgement sits |
| Testing a percentage complete | Partly | Earning rules, cut-off and measurement method |

The left column is why you are on the engagement. The right column is what stops the last two rows from becoming a conversation about process where the number was the risk.

## Sampling works. What you test it against is the question.

Take a period in which **£6.0m** of progress has been claimed across a package.

You sample **30** progress claims and find **4** with no supporting measurement record. That is 4 ÷ 30 = **13.3%**.

Extrapolated across the population, 6.0 × 0.133 = roughly **£0.80m** of progress claimed without evidence behind it.

That is a defensible audit finding on its own. Now take it one step further, which is where the project knowledge earns its place.

Unsupported progress inflates earned value. Inflated earned value raises the cost performance index. A raised index lowers the forecast out-turn. And the forecast out-turn is the denominator when progress is measured by costs incurred against total expected costs, so a lower forecast raises the percentage complete and therefore the revenue recognised in the period.

The finding is not "records were missing". The finding is that up to £0.80m of unevidenced progress has flowed through an index into a reported number, and the finding lands very differently when it is put that way. Making that chain visible is a technical competence, and the reporting layer it runs through is set out in [how project performance metrics turn into decisions](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/project-performance-management).

## What each category examines, in its own terms

Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process. That is a full profession with its own standards, and nothing on this page suggests it needs supplementing for the work it was built for.

Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. On a project engagement, that is the reporting end of the chain above.

The established project controls certifications examine measurement, progress, forecasting and the control of delivery. That is the technical subject in the right-hand column of the table.

Three distinct subjects, each examined properly by its own category. A project assurance engagement crosses all three, which is why the engagements are hard and why staffing them is harder.

## The four artefacts worth learning to test

You do not need the whole discipline. You need to be able to test these.

**The earning rule.** Whether one exists per work type, whether it was applied consistently, and what a different rule would have produced.

**The cut-off calendar.** Whether progress and cost are measured to the same date, and what the gap does to the index if not.

**The accrual list.** Whether work done and uninvoiced is captured, and whether the estimate behind each accrual is supportable.

**The estimate at completion.** Whether it was rebuilt or escalated, when it was last challenged, and whether the trend log supports the movement.

Four artefacts, each testable with evidence, each producing findings that a board understands immediately.

## When your audit credential is the whole answer

If you audit information systems, an audit credential built for that subject is exactly the right qualification and no project credential improves it. The same holds for internal audit functions whose portfolio is compliance, financial controls and operational process: the technical project subject is a small part of a wide brief, and a full second syllabus is a poor trade for it.

If your firm's methodology and progression are built around a specific credential, take the one your progression requires. That is not a scope decision.

The case for adding project material is narrow: you are on capital project engagements regularly, the findings that matter are about numbers rather than process, and you would rather test the number than the control around it.

## How to decide

Take your last three project engagements. For each, write down the highest-value finding, and mark whether it came from control testing or from understanding how the number was built.

If they all came from control testing, your existing credential is doing the work. If the ones that mattered came from the number, the addition is worth costing, and the category comparison at the reporting boundary is in [where PFL-AI sits against audit credentials](https://credentialfinder.org/pfl-ai-and-audit-credentials).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I audit a project without technical project knowledge?**
Yes, and the engagement will find control weaknesses rather than number weaknesses. That is a legitimate scope and often the one commissioned. It is worth being explicit about it in the terms of reference, so nobody reads the report as assurance over the forecast when it was assurance over the process.

**What is the single most useful thing to learn first?**
Earning rules. They decide what earned value means in a given month, they are rarely documented, and inconsistency in them is both easy to evidence and expensive. Almost every other project finding gets easier once you can test them.

**Does this apply to external audit as well as internal?**
The technical chain is the same; the standards you work to are not. On an external engagement the questions land on estimates and their supportability, which is exactly where the four artefacts sit. The mechanism connecting them is in [the finance and delivery overlap](https://credentialfinder.org/the-finance-and-delivery-overlap).

**How much project experience do I need before sitting a project credential?**
Enough that the scenarios read like engagements you have run. Two or three capital project audits is usually the point where the material stops being abstract, and before that the study time is much larger for the same result.

**Will a project credential change how findings are received?**
Sometimes, at the margin. What changes reception more is a finding expressed in the client's own terms — a number, a mechanism, a consequence — which the technical material makes possible. The credential is the evidence that you can do it; the finding is the demonstration.
