# A credential for a project manager who owns numbers

A credential for a project manager who owns numbers is chosen on scope, not on ranking. Decide which side of the finance-and-delivery line your bad months sit on, then read the syllabus that examines that side. Every category below examines its own subject thoroughly. None of them examines all three sides, and no reader should expect one to.

This is a LinkedIn original. It is not a copy of anything on the PCI estate.

A disclosure first, since this Institute has an obvious interest in the answer: credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials. PCI is not accredited. That is why the other categories get their full due below, and they deserve it.

## Three questions that settle it faster than a comparison table

**One. Which argument do you lose most often?** Not which subject interests you. The one where you cannot produce the evidence in the room. Scope, change and stakeholders is one answer. Method — the estimate, the network, the earning rule — is another. The join between the site figure and the reported figure is a third.

**Two. Has somebody already decided for you?** An employer's approved list, a client framework, a tender rule or a visa route settles the question before scope enters it. Take the mandated route.

**Three. What can you read before paying?** A body that will show you the whole syllabus first has given you the means to judge it. One that will not has told you something too.

## One month, two calendars, £750k

Scope sounds abstract until a month like this one arrives.

A package with a budget at completion of **£10.0m**. Spend is running at about **£60k a working day**, roughly £1.2m across a 20-day month.

The planner's data date is the **25th**. Progress is measured to that date, and earned value to date is **£4.0m**.

The ledger closes on the last working day of the month, five days later. Cost to the 25th was **£4.0m**. Cost at the ledger cut-off is 4.0 + (5 × 0.06) = **£4.3m**.

Take the two numbers from the same report:

- CPI on matched dates = 4.0 ÷ 4.0 = **1.00**, and the estimate at completion stays at **£10.0m**.
- CPI as the report actually shows it = 4.0 ÷ 4.3 = **0.930**, and the estimate at completion becomes 10.0 × 4.3 ÷ 4.0 = **£10.75m**.

**£750k of forecast overrun, created by two calendars.** No productivity changed. Five days of cost sit against no earned value, because the two systems were cut on different days.

Fixing it is not difficult. It needs somebody who can see both cuts, knows which one the finance system will not move, and can restate the progress or the cost to match. That person needs the vocabulary of both sides in the same head, which is a scope property of what they were taught, not a personality trait.

## Where each category is the right answer

**Project management certifications** are the right answer when the difficulty is running the project: a sponsor who keeps moving the scope, three functions each certain they own the same decision, a business case nobody has tracked since sanction. That is a wide brief and this category covers it properly.

**Cost and scheduling credentials** are the right answer when you are the person defending a number in a project review. They go deep on method — how an estimate is built and classed, how a network behaves, how progress is measured, how a forecast is produced. A project manager who has been through that argues from method rather than from confidence.

**The established project controls certifications** are the right answer when your seat is the control cycle itself: measurement, progress, forecasting and the control of delivery, across a portfolio rather than one package.

**Accountancy and finance qualifications** are the right answer when your next move is into the finance function. They examine recognition, measurement, disclosure, financial analysis and control. They are not built to examine a critical path, and there is no reason they should be. That boundary is a design decision, not a shortfall.

The four EAC methods, and which assumption each one carries, are worked through in [four ways to build an estimate at completion](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/four-eac-formulas) — useful before you sit any of these examinations.

## FAQ

**Do I need a project management certification and a project controls one?**
Most people do not. Two credentials examining the same side of the line duplicate each other; two examining different sides do not. Decide by looking at which argument you lose most often, then read the syllabus for that side before committing money to either.

**My employer mandates a specific certification. Does scope still matter?**
The mandate settles this decision. Take the certification your employer requires — it is a business requirement, not a scope question. Scope matters for the second credential, if you ever take one, and for what you read in between.

**I have never worked on the finance side. Is the reporting half too far a jump?**
No accountancy background is assumed by the PCL-AI syllabus. It builds the reporting side from the delivery side you already know: how progress becomes revenue, how a commitment becomes an accrual, how a forecast becomes a reported margin. If you have run a month-end handover, you have met the problem.

**How do I compare a project management certification against a controls one honestly?**
On what each sets out to examine, and on what it assumes you have already done. Fees, question counts and difficulty rankings tell you less than one hour with two published syllabuses. [What a project management certification sets out to examine](https://credentialfinder.org/pml-ai-and-project-management-certifications) sets out that comparison at category level.

**What should I read before paying anybody?**
The full syllabus, whoever the body is. Read the domain list, pick the three areas closest to work you have actually done, and check whether the treatment matches what you know. That test tells you more than any marketing page, this one included.
