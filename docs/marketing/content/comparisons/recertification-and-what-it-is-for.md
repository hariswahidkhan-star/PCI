# What recertification is for, and what to check first

What recertification is for comes down to two honest things: confirming that a holder's knowledge is still current, and keeping the holder accountable to the body's code while they use its name. It exists for one dishonest reason too, which is recurring revenue. The difference shows in what the cycle asks you to do.

> **Who publishes this page.** credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. PCI operates a recertification cycle, so read this as a page with an interest in the subject.

## What recertification is for, given what a pass date says

Passing an examination establishes competence against a syllabus **on a date**. That is a precise and limited claim, and it is the strongest one an examination can make.

Everything after that date is inference. A credential from twelve years ago says what you knew in a world where several of your current tools did not exist. Recertification is the mechanism that keeps the claim attached to the present.

## The three shapes a cycle takes

| Shape | What it asks | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| Development-based | Evidence of continuing professional development relevant to the scope | That you have kept working at the subject |
| Reassessment-based | Sitting an examination again, usually shorter | That you still meet the standard today |
| Declaration-based | A fee and a statement of continued adherence to the code | That you paid and signed |

The first two do work. The third is not worthless — a code declaration matters if the body enforces it — but on its own it is an annuity with a certificate attached.

Most credible schemes combine the first and third: development evidence plus a declaration, with the body sampling evidence rather than reading everything.

## The arithmetic that decides whether you will actually keep it

Read the requirement as hours per month rather than as a headline.

Take an illustrative cycle requiring **60 hours** of relevant development across **three years**. That is **20 hours a year**, which is a little under **100 minutes a month**.

Now check the shape of your year. If the requirement can be met by things you already do — a technical paper read properly, a standard reviewed, a session delivered to your team, a structured course — then the cycle is asking you to record work rather than invent it.

If it can only be satisfied by attending the body's own paid events, that is a different arrangement and the hours are not the real cost.

Three questions settle it. **Does non-body activity count?** **Does work-based learning count, with evidence?** **Is the evidence sampled or must everything be submitted?** A body whose answers are yes, yes and sampled has designed a cycle for practitioners.

## Currency is the part that has changed most

For most of the last thirty years, a project controls syllabus aged slowly. Method changed at the pace of practice, and a credential earned in one decade was substantially valid in the next.

That is no longer true of one part of the work. Where a model drafts a variance narrative, proposes a duration or produces a forecast, the questions of what it may decide, what a human must review and what evidence must survive are live and moving. A credential that never revisits them is describing a workflow you have stopped using. What has actually changed in day-to-day practice, as against what has been announced, is set out on [the AI domain's practitioner assessment](https://pciai.org/ai-in-project-controls).

This is why PCI's recertification carries an AI-currency component alongside ordinary continuing development. It is also why the governed AI portion is 20% of each Body of Knowledge rather than an appendix.

## Lapse, and what it actually costs

Lapsing is common, it usually happens during a heavy period at work, and it is not a moral failure.

A cycle expiring on **31 March 2026** and a renewal completed in **June 2026** typically means a period during which the register said "expired". If an employer checked in April, the record they saw was accurate and your claim to a current credential was not.

Most schemes offer a reinstatement window on payment and evidence, after which the route back is a full re-examination. Find out three things before you need them: the length of the grace period, what reinstatement requires, and the point at which reinstatement is no longer available.

Then diarise the date two months early. That is the entire risk management strategy, and it works.

The employer's side of this — reading a register, and what an expired record does and does not mean — is set out in [how to verify a credential somebody says they hold](https://credentialfinder.org/how-to-verify-a-credential).

## What PCI does not claim

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

PCI publishes no pass rates, no salary outcomes and no holder numbers, and recertification does not change that. A renewal confirms currency; it does not add a claim the body could not make in the first place.

## Membership and certification are not the same purchase

Many bodies run both, and the two are frequently presented as one line. They are not.

Certification is the assessed claim about you. Membership is affiliation, and it usually carries its own benefits and its own recurring cost.

Ask directly whether membership must be maintained for the certification to remain valid, or whether they are independent. The answer changes what holding the credential costs over a decade more than any discount at the point of purchase, and it is the question candidates most often forget to ask. Whether the underlying syllabus is the kind of thing that can be examined at all — before or after renewal — is covered in [what makes a credential examinable](https://credentialfinder.org/what-makes-a-credential-examinable).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is recertification just a revenue model?**
It can be, and the test is what the cycle asks for. A cycle requiring evidence of relevant development, accepting activity that is not the body's own, and sampling that evidence, is doing something. A cycle requiring only a payment and a signature is doing considerably less, whatever it is called.

**Does my continuing development have to be in the credential's subject?**
It should be relevant to the scope you were certified in. A body that accepts any activity at all is not managing currency; one that accepts only its own products is managing revenue. Relevance, evidenced, is the reasonable middle and most good schemes say so explicitly.

**What if my employer pays for renewal and then I leave?**
The credential is yours; the payment was theirs. Check whether your body invoices the individual or the employer, and keep your own record of the credential number and cycle dates so that a change of job does not create a gap in currency.

**Does a reassessment-based cycle mean sitting the whole examination again?**
Usually not. Where reassessment is used it is typically shorter and focused on what has changed since the original award. Ask what form it takes and how much notice you get, because a scheme that can call a reassessment at short notice is asking for a different kind of commitment.

**Should I let a credential lapse if I have moved on from the work?**
That is a reasonable decision and nobody should be embarrassed by it. A credential you no longer use, in a field you have left, costs money and development hours to maintain for a claim you do not need. Let it lapse deliberately rather than by accident, and note the reinstatement rules in case you return.
