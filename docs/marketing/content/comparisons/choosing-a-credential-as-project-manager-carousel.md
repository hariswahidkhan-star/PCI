# Three numbers for one month, and none of them agree

*LinkedIn document post — 12 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

Earned £6.0m. Certified £5.4m. Spent £5.7m. One package, one month, three numbers, and every one of them is correct.
A project manager who owns the numbers has to explain all three in the same meeting — which is why the credential question is about scope, not about ranking.

Twelve slides.

---

**Slide 1 — Three numbers, one month**
A £12.0m package at the half-way point. The progress report says £6.0m earned. The valuation says £5.4m certified. The ledger says £5.7m spent. Nobody has miscounted. The three systems measure three different things, on three different dates.

**Slide 2 — The figures**
Budget at completion £12.0m. Earned value at cut-off £6.0m, which is 50% of the work. Certified value to date £5.4m. Cost in the ledger, accruals included, £5.7m.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic**
CPI = 6.0 ÷ 5.7 = **1.053**.
EAC = 12.0 × 5.7 ÷ 6.0 = **£11.4m**, which is £600k under budget.
Work done and not yet certified = 6.0 − 5.4 = **£600k**, funded from your own balance sheet until the next valuation.

**The forecast is good news and the cash position is bad news, in the same month, from the same data.**

**Slide 4 — Why the three never match**
Earned value is measured to the planner's data date. Certified value is measured at a valuation meeting, and reduced by anything under dispute. Cost is cut when the ledger closes. Three calendars, three rules, one report.

**Slide 5 — Who has to explain it**
Not the planner, who owns one of the three. Not the accountant, who owns another. The project manager, who signs the report that carries all three and has to say why the forecast improved while the cash got worse.

**Slide 6 — What project management certifications examine**
Managing a project across people, process and the business environment: initiating and planning, scope and change, leading a team, stakeholders, the business case. A wide brief, covered properly, and the right category if your bad months are about scope and sponsors.

**Slide 7 — What cost and scheduling credentials examine**
Estimating, cost control, planning, schedule analysis and the economics of the asset life cycle. Deep on method: how an estimate is built and classed, how a network behaves, how progress is measured. The right category if you are the one defending a number.

**Slide 8 — What accountancy and finance qualifications examine**
Recognition, measurement, disclosure, financial analysis and control. When revenue may be recognised, what a provision has to satisfy, how a set of accounts holds together. Not built to examine a critical path, and no reason it should be.

**Slide 9 — The join belongs to nobody**
Each category examines its own side thoroughly and by design. Slide 3 sits between two of them: a delivery measure, a commercial measure and a ledger measure, reconciled monthly by whoever is holding the report. That reconciliation is a scope, and it can be examined.

**Slide 10 — Three questions that settle the choice**
Which argument do you lose most often — scope and sponsors, method, or the join? Has an employer, a client framework or a tender already decided for you? And can you read the entire syllabus before you pay anything?

**Slide 11 — Where PCI stands**
PCL-AI covers the control cycle and how its numbers become reported numbers: 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas, published in full before anybody pays. PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles.

**Slide 12 — When to take a different category**
If an employer or a tender mandates a certification, take the mandated one; that is a requirement, not a preference. If your work is running projects rather than reporting them, the project management category is examined for exactly that. credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the three credentials named here — read the syllabus before you decide anything.
