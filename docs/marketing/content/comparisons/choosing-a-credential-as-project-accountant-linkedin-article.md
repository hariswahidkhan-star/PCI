# A credential for a project accountant: the input side

A credential for a project accountant is worth choosing on the input side, not the accounting side. If your qualification already covers recognition, measurement and disclosure, the gap is whether the estimate you divide by is current, whether the progress behind it was earned, and whether the accrual is complete. Those are delivery questions, and they decide your margin.

## One month, three defensible margins

Contract price **£20.0m**. Cost incurred to date **£6.0m**. Expected total cost at the last review **£16.0m**.

**Measured by cost.** Progress = 6.0 ÷ 16.0 = **37.5%**. Revenue = 0.375 × 20.0 = £7.5m. Margin = 7.5 − 6.0 = **£1.5m**.

**Measured by output.** The delivery team's certified figure, on units installed, is **32%**. Revenue = 0.32 × 20.0 = £6.4m. Margin = **£0.4m**.

**£1.1m of reported margin, decided by which measure of progress the contract set up** — a choice made once, at the start, usually by somebody who has since left.

Now hold the measure and move the estimate. The project team reforecasts expected total cost to **£17.0m** for delivery reasons: a rate agreed above allowance, a subcontractor replaced, three weeks of rework.

Progress = 6.0 ÷ 17.0 = **35.3%**. Revenue = £7.06m. Margin = **£1.06m**. A further **£0.44m** gone, from a number you did not build.

And the invoicing follows separately. If you have billed £5.5m, the contract asset is £2.0m on the first measure and £0.9m on the second, which then ages — and its ageing is a conversation with a commercial team about entitlement rather than with a ledger.

The accounting in all of that is not difficult, and if you are qualified you can already do it. The exposure is entirely upstream.

## What a project accountant signs, and where the input comes from

| What you put your name to | Which category examines it | Where the input comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Recognised revenue for the period | Accountancy and finance qualifications | A percentage complete built by delivery |
| Contract assets and liabilities | Accountancy and finance qualifications | Certification and invoicing timing |
| Provisions, including expected losses | Accountancy and finance qualifications | A forecast owned by a project team |
| Cost accruals at period end | Split across two categories | A cut-off calendar somebody else keeps |
| Margin reporting and movement analysis | Accountancy and finance qualifications | Estimate changes you may hear about late |
| Capitalisation and asset readiness | Accountancy and finance qualifications | A completion judgement made on site |

Every row in the middle column is examined thoroughly by an accountancy qualification. Every row in the third column arrives from a process that qualification does not set out to examine, and that asymmetry is the whole argument.

## What each category examines, in its own terms

Accountancy and finance qualifications examine recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control. That is a large, deep subject examined properly, and it is what most project accountants already hold.

Cost and scheduling credentials examine estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis. They cover how the estimate you divide by is built and maintained, which is the input side of every row above.

Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process. They cover whether the controls around those inputs operated, which is a third and separate question.

None of the three is deficient. They were designed to examine different things, and a project accountant lives where the first meets the second.

## Five questions worth being able to ask

A project accountant who can ask these is worth more than one who can only book what arrives, and none of them is confrontational.

When was the estimate at completion last rebuilt from the bottom up rather than escalated? What earning rule produced this month's progress, and would a different rule have produced a different number?

Which costs are accrued on an estimate rather than a valuation? What sits in the change register that has not reached the budget? And which amounts in the valuation depend on a negotiation that has not concluded?

Each has a right answer and each is checkable. None of them is examined by an accountancy syllabus, because none of them is accountancy. The month-end sequence that makes them answerable in time to matter is set out in [how a project month-end close actually runs](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/month-end-close-for-projects).

## When your existing qualification is the whole answer

If you are progressing inside a finance function — group reporting, treasury, financial control, a route to controller or director — finish the accountancy qualification and let it do its job. It is the recognised route, it is examined properly, and nothing project-side substitutes for a stage of it.

The same applies where your projects are short, repeatable and low-variance. Where estimates rarely move and valuations are agreed monthly, the upstream risk described here barely exists.

And if a role you want names a specific qualification, take the named one. That decision was made before you got there.

The case for looking further is narrow: long contracts, movable estimates, contested variations, and a reporting line where you are the person explaining a margin swing to a board.

## How to decide

Take your last four margin movements. For each, write down what caused it, when the causing event actually happened, and when you found out.

If the lag is routinely a month or more, the gap is upstream information rather than accounting judgement. If it is short and the causes were inside your own control, your qualification covers your job, and the category comparison in [where PFL-AI sits against accountancy and finance qualifications](https://credentialfinder.org/pfl-ai-and-accountancy-and-finance-qualifications) will tell you the rest.

## FAQ

**Should a qualified accountant take a project credential at all?**
Only for the delivery half. If a syllabus mostly repeats recognition, measurement and disclosure, you are paying to be examined on what you already hold. Read the domain list and count the material that is genuinely new to you before deciding anything.

**Is this not just industry experience rather than certification?**
Experience teaches it, slowly and unevenly, and certification makes it checkable by somebody who has never worked with you. If your experience already covers estimating discipline and earning rules, a credential is evidence rather than education — which is a fair reason to take one and a fair reason not to.

**How do I challenge an estimate I did not build?**
By asking how it was built, not whether it is right. Bottom-up or escalated, when it was last rebuilt, what the trend log shows, and what would have to be true for it to hold. Those questions are answerable, and they matter if you want an answer again next month.

**Should I take an audit credential instead?**
If you are moving into assurance, yes. Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process, which is a distinct profession rather than an add-on to a reporting role.

**Does understanding delivery change the numbers I report?**
It should not change the treatment; it changes when you know. Knowing in month seven that an estimate is drifting gives you a quarter to explain it. Finding out in month nine gives you a board meeting. Same accounting, different job.
