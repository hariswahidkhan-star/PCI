# What makes a credential examinable: seven questions

A credential is examinable when there is a syllabus you can read before you pay, an assessment marked against published objectives, a stated procedure for setting the pass standard, an identity and integrity process, a published appeals route, and a way for a third party to verify a certificate. Attendance is not assessment, and a pass rate is not evidence of any of it.

Those seven things are answerable in writing by any serious body, which is what makes them a better test than anything on a brochure.

This article is written for LinkedIn and is original to it. It comes out of work published on credentialfinder.org, which is operated by Project Controls Institute Global — the body that awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials. The seven questions below are meant to be asked of this Institute as readily as of anyone else, and the section near the end says where it stands on each.

## Why a pass rate is the wrong thing to judge on

Take one cohort. 400 people enrol, 120 reach the examination, 114 pass.

114 ÷ 120 = **95%**. 114 ÷ 400 = **28.5%**.

Both figures are arithmetic and both describe the same year. Only one of them tends to be printed, and it is rarely printed with its denominator. Nothing dishonest has to happen for a number like that to mislead: the choice of base does all the work.

The same applies to holder counts, salary uplift and "industry recognised" claims with no mechanism named. They are unfalsifiable at the point of sale, which is precisely when you need to judge.

Scope survives that problem, which is why [how the certification categories compare](https://credentialfinder.org/best-project-controls-certification) is a more useful comparison than any figure a provider prints about itself.

## The seven questions, and what a solid answer sounds like

| Question | A solid answer | A thin answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Can I read the full syllabus before paying? | A published structure: domains, knowledge areas, what each expects | A topic list, or "detailed syllabus available on enrolment" |
| 2. Is there an assessment, or attendance? | A described examination, sat under stated conditions | A certificate issued on completion of a course |
| 3. Is marking criterion-referenced? | Answers marked against published objectives, same paper for every candidate | "Assessed continuously" with no criteria published |
| 4. How is the pass standard set? | A named standard-setting procedure and who applies it | A number with no method behind it |
| 5. How is identity and integrity handled? | A written procedure for identifying candidates and supervising sittings | Nothing in writing |
| 6. Are appeals and retakes published? | A route, a time limit, and who hears it | "Contact us" |
| 7. Can a third party verify a certificate? | A verification route that works without the holder's help | A PDF the holder emails you |

Print that table, ask the seven, and keep the answers. A body that puts them in writing has told you what it is; a body that answers with adjectives has told you something as well.

## Attendance is not assessment

The distinction that matters most is question 2, because it is the one most easily blurred by the word "certification".

A certificate of attendance records that somebody was present for a course. That can be worth having — training is useful and completion is a fact — but it is a record of participation, and it does not carry a claim about what the holder can do.

An examination makes a different claim: this person was tested against a declared scope and met a stated standard. The claim is narrower and it is checkable, which is why the machinery behind questions 3 to 6 exists at all.

## Examinable, accredited and recognised are three different words

**Examinable** describes the scheme: there is a declared scope, an assessment against it, and procedures that make the result mean something.

**Accredited** describes third-party oversight of the certifying body — an accreditation body assessing the scheme against a standard such as ISO/IEC 17024 and saying so publicly. A scheme can be soundly examinable and not accredited; the two claims are independent, and only one of them can be made by the body about itself.

**Recognised** is the loosest of the three and the most often asserted. It is worth asking who recognises it, in what document, and what follows from the recognition. If none of those has an answer, treat the word as decoration.

Keeping the three apart protects you both ways: from paying for an unassessed certificate, and from assuming an assessed one carries oversight it does not have.

## When the answer is to walk away

If you need a credential from an accredited body — because a client contract, an employer's framework or a regulator says so — then examinability is not sufficient and no amount of published syllabus closes that gap. Take the accredited route. That is the honest answer, and it is worth more to you than a persuasive paragraph would be.

If nobody will tell you in writing how the pass standard is set or how a certificate is verified, you are being asked to trust a claim that the body itself will not put in a sentence. That is a decision you can make, but make it knowingly.

## FAQ

**Is an unaccredited credential worthless?**
No, but it is a different claim. Accreditation is third-party oversight of the certifying body; examinability is a property of the scheme itself. A well-built unaccredited scheme with a published syllabus and a real examination is a knowable thing. Whether it is enough depends entirely on who is asking you for it.

**What is standard setting, in plain terms?**
It is the procedure that decides what counts as a pass, applied by people using a stated method rather than by picking a round number. You do not need the resulting figure to judge a body; you need to know that a method exists, who applies it, and that it is documented. Ask for the method, not the mark.

**Why should I care whether a third party can verify a certificate?**
Because the value of a credential is what somebody else can check. If verification depends on the holder sending a scan, the scheme cannot detect a forged one, and every employer in the chain is trusting a file rather than the body that issued it.

**How should I read a published pass rate?**
Ask for the denominator, then ask what happened to everyone who is not in it. A figure computed on the people who sat, in a scheme where most enrolments never sit, describes the exam day rather than the programme — as the 95% and 28.5% at the top of this piece describe the same 400 people.
