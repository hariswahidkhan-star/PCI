# How a pass mark is set, and why the method matters

A defensible pass mark is judged against the actual items by people who know the work, and it moves when the paper's difficulty moves. A pass mark chosen before anybody saw the items — the familiar round 70% — is a policy, not a standard. Knowing how a pass mark is set changes what your own result means.

> **Who publishes this page.** credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. No cut score for any credential appears on this page, PCI's included; every figure below is illustrative arithmetic for a made-up three-item paper.

## Two ways to decide who passed

**Criterion-referenced.** The standard is a level of competence. You pass by meeting it, and it makes no difference how anybody else did. Everybody who meets the standard can pass; so can nobody.

**Norm-referenced.** The standard is a position in the cohort — the top proportion, or a fixed share. Your result depends on who sat with you, so the same performance passes in a weak sitting and fails in a strong one.

For professional certification, criterion-referencing is the defensible choice, because the question being answered is "can this person do the work?" rather than "how does this person compare with the people who happened to book the same week?"

| | Criterion-referenced | Norm-referenced |
|---|---|---|
| The standard is | A defined level of competence | A rank within the cohort |
| Your result depends on | What you know | What you know and who else sat |
| Suits | Professional certification, licensure | Selection when places are limited |
| Failure mode | Standard set badly and never reviewed | Standard drifts with the intake |

## What "the minimally competent candidate" means

Standard setting starts by describing a person: the candidate who is just good enough to be certified. Not the strong candidate, not the one you would hire. The one at the boundary.

That description has to be written down and agreed before any item is rated, because everything afterwards is judged against it. A panel that has not agreed the description is rating items against several different standards at once.

The panel should be practitioners who do the work the credential covers, not only academics and not only the body's own staff. That is checkable: ask who sat on the panel and what they do for a living.

## How a pass mark is set: the arithmetic on a three-item paper

Each judge looks at an item and estimates the proportion of minimally competent candidates who would answer it correctly. The estimates are averaged per item, and the item means are summed to give the cut score for that paper.

Five judges, three items. **These are invented numbers to show the method.**

| Item | J1 | J2 | J3 | J4 | J5 | Mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.55 | 0.60 | 0.50 | 0.65 | 0.55 | **0.570** |
| 2 | 0.40 | 0.45 | 0.35 | 0.40 | 0.40 | **0.400** |
| 3 | 0.80 | 0.75 | 0.85 | 0.80 | 0.80 | **0.800** |

Cut score = 0.570 + 0.400 + 0.800 = **1.770 of 3**, which is **59.0%** for this paper.

Two things follow immediately.

**The cut score is a property of the paper.** Item 2 is hard: the panel thinks a borderline candidate gets it right four times in ten. Item 3 is easy. A paper made mostly of item-2-style questions would produce a lower cut score, and that is the method working rather than a leniency.

**Judge spread is information.** On item 3 the judges are within 0.10 of each other. If one judge had said 0.30 while another said 0.85, the panel would need to discuss that item before the number was used, because a disagreement that wide usually means the item is ambiguous.

## Why a fixed percentage across every form is a problem

Suppose two forms of the same examination. Panel judgement puts form A's standard at **62%** and form B's at **57%**, because form A is harder.

A candidate scoring **60%** meets the standard on form B and misses it on form A. Under a fixed 70% policy, the same candidate fails both, and two candidates of identical competence get different outcomes depending on which week they booked.

Equating exists to stop that. It is unglamorous, it costs money, and it is one of the clearest signals that a body is running an examination rather than selling a test.

## What to ask, in three questions

**How was the standard set for the form I will sit?** You are listening for a method and a panel, not a number.

**When was it last reviewed?** Standards drift as items are retired and replaced. A standard set once at launch and never revisited is a decision that has stopped being maintained.

**What do I get if I fail?** Domain-level feedback tells you where you were weak, which is the only humane version of a negative result and also the strongest evidence that the paper is blueprinted.

If the answers are unavailable, that is an answer. So is a body that publishes a headline pass rate but cannot describe its standard-setting method. Preparing against a criterion-referenced standard means working problems until the method is automatic rather than memorising a percentage; [practice questions and how to use them properly](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/earned-value-practice-questions) shows what that looks like on one subject.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a lower cut score an easier examination?**
Not on its own. A cut score is a property of the paper it was set for, so a lower number can simply mean a harder set of items. Comparing cut scores across bodies is meaningless without the papers, which is why a headline percentage tells you almost nothing.

**Can a body change the pass mark after the examination?**
It can adjust a standard for a form during standard setting or equating, before results are issued, and a documented process for that is a sign of rigour. Changing a published result afterwards without a documented reason is different, and it is exactly what an appeals process exists to test.

**Why do bodies publish 70% so often?**
Because it is familiar and it reads as demanding. It survives as a convention rather than as a method. Ask what the number was judged against; if the answer is "it has always been 70%", you have learnt something about how the examination is maintained.

**Does a criterion-referenced standard mean everyone can pass?**
In principle, yes, and that is the point. If every candidate meets the standard, every candidate should be certified. A body that manages its outcomes towards a target proportion is norm-referencing while calling it something else.
