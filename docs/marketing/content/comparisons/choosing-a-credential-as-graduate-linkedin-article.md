# What a first credential as a graduate is really for

A first credential as a graduate is reasonable evidence of direction and weak evidence of competence. That is not a criticism of the credentials. Most of them assume working experience and examine judgement formed on real projects, so one sat too early proves less than the same one sat two years later.

This is a LinkedIn original. It is not a copy of anything on the PCI estate.

Disclosure, since this Institute has an obvious interest in the answer: credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials. PCI is not accredited. That is why "wait" and "take a different category" both appear below as real answers.

## What actually gets a graduate hired

Not the certificate. The ability to do the arithmetic in front of somebody, and to notice when two systems have been cut on different days.

Here is a case worth being able to argue about, because it is the one that separates people who have read a syllabus from people who have used one.

A package worth **£800k**, made of **16 identical units** at £50k each. At cut-off, **9 units are started** and **5 are complete**. Cost to date is **£320k**.

Now apply two ordinary earning rules.

**Rule one, 0/100.** A unit earns nothing until it is finished. Earned value = 5 × 50 = **£250k**.

**Rule two, 50/50.** A unit earns half on starting and half on finishing. Earned value = (5 × 50) + (4 × 25) = **£350k**.

Then the indices follow:

- CPI under 0/100 = 250 ÷ 320 = **0.781**, and the estimate at completion is 800 × 320 ÷ 250 = **£1.024m**
- CPI under 50/50 = 350 ÷ 320 = **1.094**, and the estimate at completion is 800 × 320 ÷ 350 = **£731k**

**Same site. Same spend. Same day. A £293k gap in the forecast, produced entirely by a rule somebody chose before you arrived.**

A graduate who can produce that, and then say which rule is appropriate for this kind of work and why, is more useful than a graduate holding a certificate and unable to do it. That is the whole argument for learning the method before buying the letters.

The row that matters most to a graduate is the second one. Nearly every category here is built for somebody who has already done the work, and that is deliberate rather than exclusionary.

## Where an early credential does earn its money

**Your employer funds it and protects the study time.** The cost question disappears and the only question left is which category matches your desk.

**Your role is narrow and technical.** You are given planning work, or estimating work, and little else. A cost and scheduling credential examines exactly that, in depth, and gives you a method instead of a habit.

**You are moving sideways from a related discipline.** Here a credential is reasonable evidence of a deliberate choice, which is worth something to a hiring manager reading a CV that does not yet make sense on its own.

Outside those three, the money usually buys more as training, or waits.

## The join you will meet in your first month-end

Project controls credentials examine the delivery side thoroughly, and are built to. Accountancy and finance qualifications examine when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy. Neither category sets out to examine the join between them, and the join is where the money goes.

You will meet it early. The planner's data date and the ledger cut-off fall on different days, somebody has to reconcile the two, and it is usually the most junior person in the room who is asked to explain the difference.

PCL-AI is built around that join: 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas covering the control cycle and how its output becomes reported financial information. Each Body of Knowledge is proportioned 40% finance and reporting, 40% project management and 20% governed AI — the document's proportions, not an examination weighting.

Whether that describes work you have actually seen is the test. If most of the domains describe work you have never met, a year on a live project will get you more than an examination now. A full month of earned value, worked end to end, sits in [a worked earned value month](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/earned-value-worked-example), and it is a fair way to find out whether this discipline suits you at all.

## The order I would use

1. Ask what your employer funds, and take it if the category is near your work.

2. Ask what your desk actually is, and match the category to the desk rather than to an ambition three jobs away.

3. Ask whether you need accreditation now. If yes, go where it already exists.

4. Ask whether you can read the whole syllabus first. If a body will not show it before payment, that is information about the body.

5. Consider waiting six months. Real month-ends make every one of these examinations easier.

## FAQ

**Should I take any certification straight out of university?**
Usually not, unless your employer funds it. Most credentials in this field assume working experience. If you want something to do now, work through earned value and forecasting until you can produce the numbers unaided and defend the earning rule behind them.

**Which category should a graduate aim at first?**
Match the desk you are given. Planning or estimating work points to cost and scheduling credentials. A mixed controls seat points to the established project controls certifications. Running whole projects points to project management certifications.

**Is a credential better than experience on a graduate CV?**
No. Experience is the stronger signal at every level. What a credential shows early is deliberate direction, which employers read as intent rather than as proof that you can defend a forecast.

**How do I judge a syllabus when I have nothing to compare it against?**
Trace one topic you do know from end to end, count the verbs, and look for what is excluded. The method is in [how to read a Body of Knowledge before you pay](https://credentialfinder.org/how-to-read-a-body-of-knowledge), and it works on any body's document.

**What if none of the categories matches the work I am being given?**
Then do not force one. Learn the arithmetic, keep a record of what you have actually done, and revisit the question in a year when your desk has a shape. A credential chosen to fill a gap on a CV rarely survives contact with an interview.
