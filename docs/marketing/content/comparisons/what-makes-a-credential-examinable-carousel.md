# Attendance is not assessment: judging a credential

*LinkedIn document post — 12 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

A 95% pass rate and a 28.5% pass rate can describe the same 400 people.
Only one of them ever reaches a brochure, and it arrives without its denominator.

Twelve slides on what actually makes a credential examinable — seven questions any serious body will answer in writing. The arithmetic is on slide 3.

---

**Slide 1 — One word, two very different things**
"Certification" covers a certificate issued for finishing a course and a credential awarded for passing an examination against a published scope. Both are legitimate products. They make different claims about the holder, and only one of them is checkable.

**Slide 2 — Attendance is not assessment**
A certificate of attendance records that you were present. That is a fact, and training is worth doing. It is not a statement about what you can do, and it is not built to be. An examination says something narrower and testable: this person met a stated standard against a declared scope.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic**
One cohort. 400 people enrol. 120 reach the examination. 114 pass.

114 ÷ 120 = **95%**
114 ÷ 400 = **28.5%**

Both figures are true and both describe the same year. **The choice of denominator does all the work**, and no rule requires anyone to print the one that flatters least.

**Slide 4 — Three words people merge**
**Examinable**: a declared scope, an assessment against it, procedures that make the result mean something. **Accredited**: a third party oversees the certifying body against a standard such as ISO/IEC 17024. **Recognised**: somebody says so — ask who, in what document, and what follows from it.

**Slide 5 — Question 1: can I read the syllabus first?**
Before payment, and in full: domains, knowledge areas, what each expects of a candidate. A topic list is a marketing artefact. A structure you can study from, and argue with, is a syllabus.

**Slide 6 — Questions 2 and 3: is it assessed, and how is it marked?**
Is there an examination, sat under stated conditions? Are answers marked against published objectives, with the same paper reaching every candidate? Criterion-referenced marking means your result depends on the standard, not on who else sat that week.

**Slide 7 — Question 4: how is the pass standard set?**
By a stated procedure, applied by people who can be named as a role. You do not need the resulting mark to judge a body — you need to know a method exists and is documented. Ask for the method, not the number.

**Slide 8 — Question 5: identity and integrity**
How is a candidate identified? How is a sitting supervised? What happens when integrity is questioned, and who decides? A body that has thought about this has it written down, because it has had to use it.

**Slide 9 — Question 6: appeals and retakes**
A published route, a time limit, and who hears an appeal. "Contact us" is not a policy; it is a hope that nobody will need one. Read this before you sit, not after a result you want to challenge.

**Slide 10 — Question 7: can a third party verify a certificate?**
The value of a credential is what somebody else can check. If verification depends on the holder emailing a scan, a forgery is undetectable by design, and every employer down the chain is trusting a file rather than the issuing body.

**Slide 11 — When the answer is to walk away**
If a client contract, an employer framework or a regulator requires an accredited credential, examinability is not sufficient and no published syllabus closes that gap. Take the accredited route. If a body will not put the seven answers in writing, you are being asked to trust what it will not state.
