# Choosing between two credentials, without a ranking

Run both through the same six questions: what each examines, what each assumes you already know, who decides whether you passed, what it costs in hours rather than fees, what changes on the day you hold it, and what happens if you fail. Score the answers against the decisions you personally sign. If they still tie, take the one whose scope matches the work you will be doing in two years.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. A page that ranked credentials for you, published by a body that awards three of them, would be worth nothing. This one gives you the method instead, and leaves the weights where they belong.

## The grid, which you fill in yourself

| Question | Credential A | Credential B | How to score it |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is examined, and at what depth? | | | Mark each decision you sign as examined, assumed or excluded |
| What does it assume you know? | | | Can you start now, or is there a gate you have not passed? |
| Who decides the result? | | | Preparation and decision in the same hands, or separated? |
| What does it cost in hours? | | | Your own honest estimate of study weeks, not the fee |
| What changes when you hold it? | | | A pay band, an approval limit, a client-facing role — or nothing |
| What happens if you fail? | | | A published resit route and a written appeal, or silence |

Six rows, two columns, one evening. That is the whole method, and it beats any ranking because the weights are yours.

## Start with the decisions you sign, not with the job titles

Write out every decision you put your name to in a normal month. A forecast. A valuation. A progress percentage. A programme reissue. A change-order recommendation. An accrual. Eight to twelve lines is typical.

Now read both syllabuses against that list and mark each line examined, assumed or excluded. Count the examined lines.

This turns a preference into a number, and the number usually decides it before you reach the other rows. It also catches the common and expensive error: holding two credentials that examine nearly the same subject, and none of the subject that has moved into your week since you last chose.

## The hours are the real cost

Fees are the visible number and rarely the deciding one. Preparation is the cost that lands on your evenings.

Estimate it honestly. Suppose credential A extends what you already do daily, and you judge it at **60 hours**. Credential B covers a subject half of which is new to you, so you judge it at **140 hours**.

At four hours a week — one weekday evening and part of a Sunday, which is realistic alongside a full-time role — A takes 60 ÷ 4 = **15 weeks** and B takes 140 ÷ 4 = **35 weeks**. If your real availability is two hours a week, the same estimates give **30 weeks** and **70 weeks**.

Twenty to forty weeks of difference is a season of your life, and it is the single largest input to the decision that nobody puts in a comparison table.

Then the second-order question: at 35 weeks, will the thing you are preparing for still be the thing you need? If your role is moving, the long route can be right anyway. Just make it a choice.

## Compare like with like, at category level

Two credentials rarely sit in the same category, and half the confusion comes from comparing across a boundary as though the two were competing versions of one thing.

| Category | What it examines | What it leaves to a different examination |
|---|---|---|
| Cost and scheduling credentials | Estimating, cost control, planning and schedule analysis | The accounting treatment of what has been measured |
| The established project controls certifications | Measurement, progress, forecasting and the control of delivery | The reported result those forecasts produce |
| Accountancy and finance qualifications | Recognition, measurement, disclosure and financial control | How the delivery data was produced before it arrived |
| Project management certifications | Managing projects across people, process and the business environment | Depth in any single control discipline |
| Chartered routes in surveying and engineering | Assessed professional competence over years of supervised practice | The single-sitting examination model entirely |

Those are different subjects, examined thoroughly by their own standards. A choice between two of them is not a choice about quality. It is a choice about which subject you want on the record.

Where both of your candidates sit inside one category, the syllabus comparison gets finer and the tie-breaks matter more. Where they sit in different categories, the count of signed decisions is nearly always decisive on its own.

## The tie-breaks, in order

**Which one covers the work you will be doing in two years.** Credentials are slow. Aim ahead of where you are.

**Which one is examined more independently.** Where the organisation taking the preparation fee also awards the result, the evidence is weaker, whatever the syllabus says.

**Which one you can verify from the outside.** Try to confirm a stranger's credential in that scheme, today, without an account. If you cannot, neither can a hiring manager in four years.

**Which one you can read before paying.** A published syllabus is a body inviting you to check its work.

## When the grid is not the decision

If one of your two is named in a client's prequalification, an employer's grading matrix or a framework agreement, take the named one and stop filling in the grid. Requirements outrank fit, and the grid was never going to change the requirement.

If a procurement rule in your market requires an accredited credential, that settles it too, and it settles it against this Institute today.

If one of the two is a chartered route and what you want is chartered standing, the grid does not apply either. That is an assessment of professional competence over years of supervised practice, not an examination, and nothing else substitutes for it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I just take both?**
Sometimes, in sequence, and only where they examine genuinely different subjects. Two credentials covering the same ground is a common and expensive duplication. If you do take both, take the one that unblocks you now first, and let the second follow the direction your role is moving in.

**What if I cannot tell what a syllabus examines?**
That is itself an answer, and a useful one. Write to the body with three specific questions about depth on the topics you sign for. How fast and how concretely they reply tells you what the document did not.

**How much should recognition weigh against scope?**
It depends on what is blocking you. If you are not getting interviews, recognition is doing the work. If the interviews come and the job itself is what hurts, scope is the only axis that changes anything.

**Is a harder examination a better one?**
No. Difficulty is not relevance, and a paper that is hard on material you never touch is a hard paper you did not need. Match the depth to the decisions you sign, then let difficulty fall where it does.

**My employer will fund one. Does that change the method?**
Only the fee row. The hours row is still yours, and so is the row about what changes when you hold it. Funded candidates give up at the same point as everybody else, usually because the answer to that last row was always going to be nothing.

**How do I judge recognition without a ranking?**
Read ten current advertisements for the job you want next, in the market you want it in, and note what they ask for. That is your own recognition data, gathered in twenty minutes, and it is more useful than any general claim about what employers prefer.
