# Choosing a credential for a PMO lead: the aggregate test

A credential for a PMO lead turns on one question: do you defend the aggregate numbers, or only assemble them? If your PMO sets method, runs assurance and reports the portfolio, a project management certification examines most of that. If you are the person a board questions about the portfolio forecast, you need the layer underneath, because an aggregate hides exactly the thing that gets asked about.

## Two projects, one physical state, two indices

Take two projects in your portfolio. Each has a budget at completion of **£10.0m**, split into ten equal work packages of £1.0m. Both are in precisely the same physical state: six packages finished, packages seven and eight started and part-done.

Project A earns on a **0/100** rule: nothing until a package completes. Earned value = **£6.0m**.

Project B earns on a **50/50** rule: half at start, half at completion. Earned value = 6.0 + 0.5 + 0.5 = **£7.0m**.

Each has spent **£6.6m**.

Cost performance index for A: 6.0 ÷ 6.6 = **0.909**.
Cost performance index for B: 7.0 ÷ 6.6 = **1.061**.

**The same physical progress, and one project looks 15 points worse than the other.** Nobody has miscounted. Two rules were chosen, on two different days, by two different planners.

Now aggregate. Combined earned value £13.0m against combined actual cost £13.2m gives **0.985**, which describes neither project and no site anywhere.

And forecast from it. Against a combined budget of £20.0m, an index of 0.909 gives an outturn of **£22.0m**, 1.061 gives **£18.85m**, and the aggregate 0.985 gives **£20.30m**. A **£3.15m** spread, with nothing in dispute about what has actually been built.

That is what a portfolio number does when the rules underneath it are mixed. Standardise the earning rules before you standardise the template.

## What a PMO lead signs

| What you put your name to | Which category examines it | What it leaves to somebody else |
|---|---|---|
| Portfolio status and the aggregate forecast | Project management certifications | How each component number was built |
| Method, templates and reporting standards | Project management certifications | Whether the method survives a hard month |
| Stage gates and assurance reviews | Audit credentials, in part | The technical adequacy of what is reviewed |
| Resource and capacity planning | Project management certifications | The schedule logic underneath the demand |
| Contingency and change governance | The established project controls certifications | The accounting treatment of the drawdown |
| Rules for how AI may be used in reporting | Rarely examined anywhere yet | Almost everything, in most schemes |

Rows one, five and six are the ones that expose a PMO lead, and they are the rows most often assumed rather than examined.

## What each category examines, in its own terms

Project management certifications examine managing projects across people, process and the business environment. For method, governance and stakeholder work that is the matching subject, examined properly, and it is where most PMO leads sensibly start.

The established project controls certifications examine measurement, progress, forecasting and the control of delivery. That is the layer under the portfolio numbers — the earning rules, the indices, the forecast method.

Audit credentials examine governance, risk, control and the assurance process. If your PMO's centre of gravity is assurance rather than delivery support, that is a closer match than either of the other two.

Each is doing its own job. A PMO lead's difficulty is that the role has drifted across all three in most organisations while the credential usually has not.

## The row nobody was examined on

Reporting is being drafted by models in a great many PMOs now, and the rules for it are being written by whoever happens to be in the room.

That is a governance question with a technical core: what a model may draft, what it may never decide, what evidence has to survive so a reviewer can trace a number back to its source, and what the reviewer is accountable for having checked.

If your PMO issues that policy, you are signing something almost no credential currently examines. A workable starting point is [an AI policy for project controls you can adapt](https://pciai.org/ai-policy-for-project-controls) rather than a clean-sheet draft.

## Where PCI fits, and what it will not claim

The PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI) is the one built for this seat: 16 domains and 63 knowledge areas, with the Body of Knowledge proportioned 40% finance and reporting, 40% project management and 20% governed AI. Those proportions describe the syllabus and not an examination weighting.

The 20% covering governed AI maps to the last row of the table. The 40% covering finance and reporting is what lets you decompose an aggregate in front of a finance director. Across the three volumes there are 92 sector case studies, and 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements sit underneath them.

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

## When a project management certification is the right answer

If your PMO is a method and governance function — standards, templates, gate discipline, reporting cadence, benefits tracking — a project management certification examines that work and will be read instantly by every executive you deal with. That combination is hard to beat.

If your organisation's grading matrix names a certification for the band you want, take the named one. That decision was made before you arrived, and a scope argument does not win it.

And if you run a PMO in a domain with little cost exposure — internal change, product, service transition — the arithmetic above is not your problem, and buying a syllabus to cover it is buying reading rather than evidence. What a credential in that category covers, and where it stops, is set out in [what a PMO-focused credential covers](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/pmo-certification).

## How to decide

Take the last three board packs your PMO issued. For each number on the front page, write down who built it, what method produced it, and whether you could reconstruct it under questioning without help.

Rows you can reconstruct are covered by whatever you already hold. Rows you cannot are the scope gap, and they are usually the forecast, the contingency position and anything with a percentage complete in it.

Then check whether the gap is examinable at all, because some of what a PMO lead does is judgement rather than knowledge, and the test for that is in [what makes a credential examinable](https://credentialfinder.org/what-makes-a-credential-examinable).

## FAQ

**Does a PMO lead need technical controls knowledge, or just governance?**
It depends who defends the numbers. If a controls manager sits in the room and answers the technical questions, governance depth is enough. If you are alone in front of a board with a portfolio forecast, you need to be able to decompose it, and that is technical.

**Two of my projects use different earning rules. Does that matter at portfolio level?**
Yes, and it is the commonest reason an aggregate misleads. Different rules produce different earned value from identical physical progress, so the sum describes a mixture rather than a position. Standardise the rules before you standardise the template.

**Should a PMO lead take an audit credential?**
If your PMO is an assurance function, that category examines governance, risk, control and the assurance process, and it is a serious fit rather than a curiosity. If your PMO supports delivery, it is a wide subject to study for a narrow part of your week.

**How should a PMO handle AI in reporting when no credential examines it?**
Write the policy anyway, and write it as a control rather than a ban: what may be drafted, what may never be decided, what evidence must survive, and who signs. Then review it quarterly, because the tools move faster than the policy.

**Is a portfolio index worth reporting at all?**
Only with its decomposition attached. On its own it invites a question it cannot answer. Reported alongside the two or three components moving it, and the earning rules behind them, it becomes a real summary rather than an average of unlike things.
