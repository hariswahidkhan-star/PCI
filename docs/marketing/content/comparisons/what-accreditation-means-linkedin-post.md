# What accreditation certifies, and what it does not say

*LinkedIn text post — 1,880 characters including hashtags. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

Two cost performance indices for the same month. 0.897 and 1.000. Both defensible.

The difference is £1.2m of instructed change that nobody has priced.

The figures. £30.0m fixed price, original budget at completion £26.0m. At cut-off, earned value on the base scope is £10.4m, which is 40%, and actual cost is £11.6m — of which £1.2m sits against the unpriced instruction.

Carry the change inside the reported figures: 10.4 ÷ 11.6 = 0.897.
Hold it outside, base scope only: 10.4 ÷ 10.4 = 1.000.

The entire apparent overrun is one instruction. Which presentation is right depends on a rule that lives half in the cost procedure and half in the accounting policy.

Which is why this is a post about accreditation.

Accreditation means a third party has assessed how a certifying body operates: impartiality, how examination questions are developed and retired, how the pass standard is set, who makes the certification decision, who hears an appeal, how records are kept. That is demanding, and a body that has passed it has done something real.

It says nothing about scope. An accreditation body is not a subject-matter authority in cost engineering or financial reporting, and is careful not to behave as one. So no accreditation assessment anywhere would tell you whether a syllabus makes a candidate able to reconcile that £1.2m across both sides.

Two axes, then. Has the process been independently assessed. Does the scope match the work you do. Asking only the first answers half the question.

And if a regulator or a tender requires an accredited credential, take the accredited one. That is a requirement, not a preference.
