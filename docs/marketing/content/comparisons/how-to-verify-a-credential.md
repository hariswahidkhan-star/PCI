# How to verify a credential somebody says they hold

Verification means checking the issuing body's own register, in a session you opened yourself, for four things: that the person is listed, that the record is current, that the credential named is the one claimed, and that the scope matches. A certificate file, a screenshot or a link supplied by the candidate is not verification.

> **Who publishes this page.** credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. A page that tells you to check with the issuing body, published by an issuing body, has an obvious interest. The method below is the one to use on PCI as well as on anybody else.

## The five checks

**One. Go to the issuing body directly.** Type the body's address yourself or find it through a search you ran. Do not follow a link from the CV, the email or the certificate.

**Two. Find the register, not the contact form.** A body that certifies people should publish a way to confirm a holder's status. If the only route is an email to the body's own sales address, treat the answer as a marketing reply until it arrives on headed terms.

**Three. Match the identity, not just the name.** Common names collide. Look for a credential number, a holder identifier or a date of award to tie the record to the person in front of you.

**Four. Read the status field.** Current, expired, suspended, withdrawn and "in progress" are different states, and the certificate document looks the same in all of them.

**Five. Read the scope.** Bodies award several credentials. A record confirming one credential is not confirmation of another, and a candidate may have restated an adjacent qualification as the one you asked about.

## What each field is telling you

| Field on a register | What it means | What it does not mean |
|---|---|---|
| Date of award | The examination was passed on that date | That the credential is current today |
| Status: current | The holder has met renewal requirements to date | That the holder is competent in your specific work |
| Status: expired | Renewal was not completed | That the examination was never passed |
| Credential name | The specific scheme awarded | Any adjacent scheme with a similar name |
| Scope or specialism | The area examined | Coverage of everything the holder does |
| Sanctions or withdrawal note | A decision has been taken about the holder | Details, which are usually confidential |

## The date arithmetic, worked

Lapsing is the most common finding, and it is not dishonesty. It needs to be read correctly.

A credential is awarded on **12 March 2023**. The scheme runs a **three-year** recertification cycle, expiring at the end of the anniversary month, so the record is current to **31 March 2026**.

A candidate presents it in **June 2026**. The register shows **expired**.

That means one thing only: renewal was not completed. The examination was still passed in 2023, and the knowledge did not evaporate on 1 April. What has lapsed is the body's ongoing confirmation of currency, and if your requirement is a current credential, the candidate does not meet it today.

Now the version that should stop a process. The register shows **no record at all**, and the candidate produces a certificate file. Either the credential was issued by a different body from the one named, or it was never issued. Ask for the credential number and check again before drawing a conclusion, because a mis-typed name defeats most registers.

| What you find | What it supports |
|---|---|
| Current record, identity matched, scope matched | The claim, as made |
| Expired record | The award happened; the currency claim fails |
| Record for a different credential | Ask which one the candidate meant, in writing |
| No record, certificate produced | Nothing yet; obtain the credential number, then escalate |

What the title on a CV actually proves, once the record checks out, is a separate question and a narrower one than most hiring processes assume: [what a certified professional title demonstrates](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/certified-project-controls-professional) sets out the limits.

## Red flags on a certificate document

A file is easy to make and hard to verify, which is why the register is the check and the file is not.

Watch for a certificate with no credential number, no issuing body address and no verification instruction on it. Watch for verification links that resolve to a domain slightly different from the body's own. Watch for "verification" that consists of an email from a free mailbox.

None of these proves anything on its own. All of them mean the register check has to happen before a decision.

## What PCI does not claim

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

Nor does verification prove quality. Confirming that somebody holds a credential tells you the body says so. What that is worth depends entirely on what the credential examines, which is why [what accreditation means, and what it does not prove](https://credentialfinder.org/what-accreditation-means) sits beside this page rather than after it.

## If you are the holder

Keep your credential number where you can find it, and put it on your CV next to the credential name. It shortens every check and it signals that you expect one.

Know your own expiry date, and read [what recertification is actually for](https://credentialfinder.org/recertification-and-what-it-is-for) before it arrives, because a lapse is far cheaper to prevent than to explain in an interview.

If a body you hold a credential from has no public register, ask it in writing how a prospective employer is meant to confirm your status. The answer is worth knowing before you need it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I rely on a professional profile listing a credential?**
No. A profile records what somebody typed. It is a starting point for the check rather than the check itself, and it carries no status information, so it cannot tell you whether the credential is current.

**What if the body will not confirm a holder's status?**
Some bodies confirm only with the holder's consent, which is legitimate under data protection rules. Ask the candidate to authorise the confirmation or to supply the credential number for a self-service register. A refusal from both sides is itself information.

**Is a lapsed credential a reason to reject a candidate?**
Only if currency is genuinely part of your requirement. A lapse usually means the renewal fee or the continuing development record slipped, often during a period of heavy work. Ask about it plainly, and treat it as a fact rather than a character finding.

**How do I verify a credential from a body I have never heard of?**
Same method, with one addition. Check that the body exists as an organisation, that it publishes a syllabus, and that its register is its own. A new body is not a warning sign in itself; an absent syllabus and an absent register are.

**Does a verification service that is not the issuing body count?**
It counts as a convenience, not as the source. Anything that aggregates records is only as current as its last update, and the issuing body's register remains the authority. Where an aggregator is operated by an issuing body, as this site is, treat its pages accordingly.
