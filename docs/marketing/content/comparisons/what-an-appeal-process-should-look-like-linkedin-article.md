# What a certifying body owes you when you appeal a result

A working appeal is a request to reconsider a result, heard by somebody who did not decide it, inside a window that binds the body as well as the candidate, and answered in writing with reasons. If a scheme cannot show you that in a published policy before you apply, you are being asked to trust a promise rather than a process.

credentialfinder.org is operated by Project Controls Institute Global, which awards the PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI credentials described here. The checks below are the ones to run on PCI as well, and PCI's own weak point is stated further down rather than left out.

## An appeal and a complaint are different things

An appeal challenges a decision the body has made about you: a result, an eligibility refusal, an integrity finding, the invalidation of a credential, a refused accommodation.

A complaint is about conduct or service: an examination centre that opened late, a question handled rudely, a delay nobody explained.

They should be different routes with different people and, usually, different deadlines. Sending an appeal into the complaints inbox is the commonest reason a genuine case is answered with an apology instead of a decision.

## The seven marks of a process you can rely on

| Mark | What a working policy says | What a thin one says |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Published on the site, readable before you apply | "Available on request" |
| Grounds | Names what can and cannot be appealed | "Candidates may raise concerns" |
| Time limits | A window for you and a deadline for the body | A window for you only |
| Who hears it | Somebody outside the original decision, named by role | "The relevant team" |
| Evidence | States what will be considered and what records exist | Silent |
| Outcome | In writing, with reasons, and the remedies listed | "You will be informed" |
| Status while open | Says what your record shows meanwhile | Silent |

The last row is the one candidates never think to check and the one that hurts. If a scheme publishes a provisional result and then withdraws it, the withdrawal has to travel to everyone who saw it, and it never does. A result held for review should show no score and no pass or fail until the review closes — held, and openly labelled as held, rather than a number that may be taken back.

## What counts as a ground

The useful distinction is between something checkable and something felt.

Checkable: the session was disrupted and the disruption is in the record. The paper asked for one thing and the marking scheme rewarded another. An eligibility rule was applied that is not the published rule. An accommodation was agreed and not provided.

Not a ground on its own: disagreeing with the syllabus, disagreeing with the pass standard, or believing the paper was harder than expected. Those are arguments about scheme design, and the route for them is a consultation or a complaint, not an appeal.

Here is a checkable one, worked.

A calculation item gives earned value of £26.6m and actual cost of £30.0m, and asks for the cost performance index to two decimal places.

- 26.6 ÷ 30.0 = 0.886666…
- To two decimal places: **0.89**
- To three: **0.887**

A candidate who wrote 0.89 answered the question that was asked. If the marking scheme accepted only 0.887, the scheme contradicted its own instruction, and that is a ground anybody can verify without knowing anything about the candidate. It is also the kind of defect a body should want to hear about, because it will have hit everybody who sat that paper.

That is the shape to aim for in an appeal letter: one sentence naming the decision, one naming the rule or instruction you say was not followed, and the evidence attached. Three lines beat three pages.

## What the remedies should be

A policy that can only say "upheld" or "not upheld" has not thought it through. The remedies worth seeing listed are a re-mark, a re-sit at no further charge, correction of the record, reinstatement of an invalidated credential, or a change to the item for everyone who sat it.

The last one matters more than it looks. If an appeal shows an item was defective, fixing it for one candidate and leaving it wrong for the rest is not a remedy, it is a settlement.

The policy should also say whether any charge for the appeal is returned when the appeal succeeds. The amount is the body's business; whether it is stated up front and returned on success is yours.

## Where PCI's own position is weak, stated plainly

PCI publishes an appeals policy and a separate complaints route, and both can be read before anyone applies. The review of a disputed decision sits with people who did not make it.

What PCI does not have is an external layer. In an accredited scheme, there is a further body you can tell that the scheme is not following its own rules, and that body can act on the scheme rather than on your case alone. PCI's route ends inside PCI.

PCI is a new, independent certifying body. It is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body, and does not claim to be. The scheme is built with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles. Read the published Body of Knowledge before you decide anything.

If that external layer is what you need — because a tender rule, a framework agreement or a regulator requires it, or because you have been burned before and want somebody above the body to complain to — take the accredited route. That is a real requirement and a good reason, and no amount of published policy substitutes for it.

## How to check a scheme in ten minutes

Open the appeals policy. If it is not on the site, that is your answer to most of the rest.

Find the deadline that binds the body. Search the page for a number of days that applies to them, not to you.

Find who hears it. Look for a role that is not the role that made the decision.

Find what your record shows while an appeal or a review is open. If the policy is silent, ask, and keep the reply.

Then send one email asking for the appeals policy, the impartiality policy and the syllabus. All three should be public already. How completely that email is answered tells you most of what the policies would.

## FAQ

**Can I appeal simply because I disagree with my result?**
Not usefully. An appeal needs a decision and a rule you say was not followed — a marking instruction contradicted, an eligibility rule misapplied, a disrupted session in the record. Disagreement on its own gives the reviewer nothing to check, and a reviewer who cannot check anything can only confirm the original decision.

**What should a result show while it is under review?**
Nothing that can be taken back. No score, no pass or fail, no credential issued — just that the result is held and the reason category. A published number that is later withdrawn cannot be recalled from the people who already saw it, and the candidate carries that cost, not the body.

**Should there be a charge to appeal?**
Bodies differ, and a charge is defensible as a filter against volume. What is not defensible is a charge that is unpublished, or one that stays with the body when the appeal succeeds. Look for the position stated in the policy before you apply, not quoted to you afterwards.

**How long should an appeal take?**
Long enough for a proper review and short enough to be usable, with the limit stated in the policy. The number matters less than whether it binds the body. A policy giving you a fixed window to submit and giving itself none is a queue, not a process.

**What if the appeal is about the invigilation or the software rather than the marking?**
That is still an appeal if it affected the decision, and the record should support it. Ask what session records exist and how long they are kept. A scheme that keeps a record of the session can check your account of it; one that keeps nothing can only take a view.
