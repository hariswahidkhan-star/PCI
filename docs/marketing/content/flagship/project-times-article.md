# Project controls vs project management: who owns the number?

*Earned value is produced by the project. Actual cost is produced by the ledger. Here are the four questions to ask before you sign what comes out of the division.*

Fourteen CPI points went missing in an accrual, not a schedule. Project controls produced the earned value. Finance produced the cost. Nobody produced the accrual that reconciles them, and the project manager signed the result. Project controls vs project management is not a reporting line. It is a number with two authors and one signature.

## What fourteen points looks like

The figures below are arithmetic. There is no client, no sector and no currency, and no claim about how often it happens.

Earned value is 2,200,000. Invoiced cost is 1,850,000.

> 2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19

The cost performance index is 1.19: every unit of cost has bought 1.19 units of work. The report says the job is running under budget, and on the evidence in the system it is right. A careful reviewer signs it.

Now the figure the report was never given. There is 240,000 of work performed and not yet invoiced at the reporting date. Accrue it.

> 1,850,000 + 240,000 = 2,090,000
>
> 2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05

Fourteen index points, moved by a journal entry. Nothing happened on site. The same people worked at the same rate on the same job, and the month closed twice: once for the ledger, once for progress.

There is a rule of thumb in that arithmetic worth keeping. Divide the accrual by the posted cost — here, 240,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 0.13 — and you have the size of the error. The index was 13 per cent high.

A forecast at completion built by dividing the budget by that index carries the same factor the other way. Correct the index and the forecast rises by about 13 per cent, and it does so whatever the budget is.

## Your schedule report will not warn you

The schedule performance index and schedule variance contain no cost term. They compare earned value with planned value, so an accrual cannot move them and its absence cannot move them either.

A clean schedule report alongside a flattering cost report is not two signals agreeing. It is one signal and one silence, and the silence reads like agreement on a summary slide.

## The same missing entry moves revenue the other way

Where progress for revenue is measured by an input method — cost incurred against total expected cost — the omission runs in the opposite direction. Understate incurred cost and you understate measured progress, and therefore the revenue recognised for the period.

So one month can report better cost performance and less revenue than the work earned, from the same missing entry. Each figure is locally plausible. Only a reconciliation of the two shows the contradiction, and nothing in either report asks for one. This is a description of how the two calculations behave, not accounting advice.

## Four questions before you sign

None of this needs a new system. It needs four questions, asked in the week before the report goes out rather than in the meeting where it is challenged.

| Ask | A good answer sounds like | What a weak answer tells you |
|---|---|---|
| What date did the cost ledger close for this period? | "The 25th. Here is the extract and the cut-off memo." | Nobody has compared that date with the data date, so nobody knows how wide the gap is. |
| What date was progress measured to? | "The 31st, from the site returns and the last measure." | Progress and cost may be describing different months, and the index divides one by the other. |
| What was accrued for work performed between those two dates, and on what evidence? | "240,000, from goods received not invoiced and the uncertified measure." | The accrual is either a round-number plug or it does not exist. Both are visible later. |
| When does that accrual reverse, and against the account earned value is measured on? | "First working day of next period, same account." | Next month carries a double count, and the correction will be read as a productivity collapse. |

Three lines on the front of the cost report settle most of it: the date the ledger closed, the date progress was measured, and the accrual raised for the gap with the evidence behind it. Any project can add that note this month. It costs nothing and it makes the assumption visible to the person carrying it.

When the accrual genuinely cannot be quantified in time, publish the index on both bases and say which is which. A stated range beats a precise number that is wrong, and it moves the conversation to the gap while there is still a month left to do something about it.

## An assistant will explain the wrong number fluently

A model drafting the monthly commentary reads the same field the report reads. It will explain a cost performance index of 1.19 clearly, cite the right formula, and produce a paragraph better written than the one it replaced. What it has no way of noticing is the accrual, because the accrual was never a record.

Automated checking has the same limit. It reconciles the figures that exist against the rules that apply to them, and a cost that was never posted breaks no rule. This is why the useful governance question is not whether the output is accurate but which figures a model may originate and which it may only restate, [written down before the tool is switched on rather than after a board paper goes out](https://pciai.org/ai-policy-for-project-controls).

## The gap is in the syllabus, not in the people

Accountancy examinations rarely test float. Engineering examinations rarely test cut-off. Both professions are examined hard and well on their own side of the line, and neither is examined on the handover between them, which is the one place the number is assembled.

That is the argument for examining the crossing directly, and it is what the Project Controls Institute was set up around. The PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) examines 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas, sitting on 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements — the Institute's certification requirements, not regulation.

The Body of Knowledge behind it is 40 per cent finance and reporting, 40 per cent project management, 20 per cent governed use of machine output.

That is the shape of the syllabus and not an examination weighting. The syllabus is settled; the exam blueprint is still an open decision. Anyone quoting you an exam weighting, for this credential or any other, is reading from a marketing document.

Read what any credential examines rather than what it promises, ours included. [A like-for-like reading of what the main controls credentials actually test](https://credentialfinder.org/best-project-controls-certification) collects that in one place — PCI publishes it, so treat it as a starting point and verify each entry against the awarding body itself. A syllabus that will not show you its domains is telling you something.

## The part that is yours

You sign the forecast. Miss the accrual and the number you defended was wrong before you saw it. Seniority means owning both ledgers, and a credential that examines only one half leaves you accountable for a gap that nobody taught you.

So take last month's cost report, the one already signed and issued, and run the four questions over it. Find the two dates. Find the accrual, or find out that there wasn't one.

Whichever you find, you will know something about your own reporting that the report itself does not say.
