# Estimate at completion: whose assumptions are you signing?

Someone else's. Every estimate at completion carries assumptions you did not make: the earning rules that produced earned value, and the accounting cut-off that produced actual cost. Two of the four standard methods divide by cost performance index, so both sets of assumptions arrive inside your forecast whether or not you were ever examined on either.

Accountancy exams rarely test float. Engineering exams rarely test cut-off. Projects need both.

## What is an estimate at completion?

An estimate at completion is the current forecast of what the whole job will cost when it is finished: cost already incurred, plus an estimate of the cost to complete. The four standard methods differ in one respect only, which is what each assumes about the work nobody has done yet.

The total is what gets argued about in the review. The assumption underneath it is what should be.

## Why does the forecast move when nothing on site has changed?

Round numbers that belong to no project.

Earned value is 2,200,000. Invoiced cost is 1,850,000.

**2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19.**

Cost performance index reads 1.19, so the report says the job is running under budget. But 240,000 of work is done and not yet invoiced. Accrue it, and cost becomes 2,090,000.

**2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05.**

Fourteen points moved on one missing accrual. The error is accounting. The damage is delivery. Neither training alone catches it.

Now do the same thing to the forecast. Take your own budget at completion, divide it by 1.19, then divide it by 1.05, and set the two answers side by side. Nothing happened on site in between.

The report error was fourteen points. The forecast error is fourteen points applied to every unit of work you have not built yet.

## Which estimate at completion assumptions are you actually defending?

Four standard methods. Each is a different claim about the remaining work, and each fails differently when the cost feeding it is short.

| Method | What it assumes about the work that is left | What an understated actual cost does to it | When it is the honest choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)** | The overspend so far was a one-off. The rest runs at the budget rate. | Passes through once, at its own size. | Early, or where the cause is closed out and cannot recur. |
| **EAC = BAC ÷ CPI** | Cost performance to date continues to the last day. | Scales the whole forecast. The wrong ratio hits all remaining work. | A stable job with a long run left and an unchanged basis of estimate. |
| **EAC = AC + [(BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI)]** | Cost and schedule performance both continue, and lost time is bought back with money. | Compounds with the schedule index, and this is the method people reach for when a job is already in trouble. | A job under schedule pressure where acceleration is being paid for. |
| **EAC = AC + a bottom-up estimate to complete** | The original basis has stopped describing the work left, so the remainder is re-priced. | Does not scale, but cost to date is still short and the re-estimate lands against a baseline nobody trusts. | After a change in scope, method or market. |

Read the third column again. Two of these four take the polluted ratio and apply it to everything still to come, so the accounting error does not sit still: it is multiplied by however much work is left. The method you use, chosen in most teams by convention rather than by argument, decides whether a missing journal entry stays the size it was or grows.

No method is more accurate than the others in general; each is accurate about a different claim. The review question is not which number to take, but which claim about the remaining work you are prepared to make this month.

## Why does neither professional examination cover this?

One profession produces the estimate at completion. Another consumes it, unchanged, in a statement that will be audited.

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, almost never on float or a rule of credit. An engineer is examined on progress measurement and the critical path, almost never on cut-off or a contract asset. Neither is examined on the handover between them.

This is not an argument about competence, and the version that blames people is both rude and wrong. The gap is in what each profession is examined on, and therefore in what each is assumed to have covered already. If you hold a cost or delivery credential and want to see where its boundary is drawn, [where the CCP's syllabus and the PMP's syllabus divide this ground](https://credentialfinder.org/aace-ccp-vs-pmp) says more than the letters after anyone's name.

## What happens when a model produces the forecast?

A model will return all four estimates at completion before you have opened the file, and the arithmetic will be right every time. It will also pick a method, usually the one that fits the columns it was handed rather than the job.

What it cannot do is see a cost that was never recorded. There is no field for an invoice that does not exist.

That narrows the governance question. Not whether the tool calculates correctly, but which figures a model may originate, which it may only restate, and whose name is on the output when it reaches a board pack. Settle that before the tool is switched on, alongside [how to measure whether a model's cost estimate is any good](https://pciai.org/ai-for-cost-estimating-in-construction), because a forecast nobody has scored is an opinion with a decimal point.

## The forecast goes out with your name on it

You sign the forecast. Miss the accrual and the number you defended was wrong before you saw it. Seniority means owning both ledgers, and a credential that examines only one half leaves you accountable for a gap that nobody taught you.

So do not take the syllabus on trust, ours included. Read [the PCL-AI syllabus, domain by domain](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/body-of-knowledge), all 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas of it, and hold it against your own last forecast.

Then find out which of the four methods produced that forecast, and who chose.

## Questions people ask

**Which EAC formula is the most accurate?**
None of them in general, because each is accurate about a different assumption. Completing at the budget rate assumes the variance was a one-off. Dividing budget at completion by cost performance index assumes performance continues. A bottom-up estimate assumes the original basis has stopped describing the work. The accuracy sits in the choice.

**Should actual cost include accruals when calculating EAC?**
Yes. Actual cost means cost incurred, not cost invoiced, so work performed and not yet billed belongs in it. Leave the accrual out and every method starting from actual cost is short by that amount, and the two dividing by cost performance index are short by more, because the wrong ratio then reaches all remaining work.

**Can an estimate at completion be lower than actual cost?**
Not unless a genuine credit is expected. Money already spent cannot be un-spent, so a forecast that dips below cost to date usually means actual cost is overstated, most often by a commitment counted twice or a cost posted to the wrong project. The same error will be in every ratio on the report.

**Who owns the estimate at completion, finance or project controls?**
Project controls usually produces it and finance usually publishes it, so neither owns it end to end. The earning rule behind earned value belongs to the project. The recognition rule and the closing date belong to finance. The failure is rarely in either rule. It is in the handover, which no single syllabus examines.
