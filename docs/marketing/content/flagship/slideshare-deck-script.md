### Slide 2 — The index measured the ledger, not the job

**Body:**

> Cost performance index divides earned value by actual cost. When actual cost is missing work that has been done and not yet invoiced, the index is measuring an incomplete ledger rather than the job. Accrue 240,000 of uninvoiced work into a cost of 1,850,000 and CPI falls from 1.19 to 1.05.

---

### Slide 3 — The arithmetic

**Body:** the whole slide is this table and the line under it. Nothing else goes on this slide.

| | |
|---|---|
| Earned value | 2,200,000 |
| Invoiced cost | 1,850,000 |
| **CPI as reported** | **2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19** |
| Work done, not yet invoiced | + 240,000 |
| Cost once the accrual lands | 2,090,000 |
| **CPI once accrued** | **2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05** |

> Nothing on site changed between those two numbers.

---

### Slide 4 — 1.19 and 1.05 are both arithmetically correct

**Body:**

> Both figures divide the same earned value by a different cost. Only one of the two costs is the cost of the work that has been done. The report was not wrong about its inputs. It was wrong about which inputs it needed, and no arithmetic check catches that.

---

### Slide 5 — Nobody was careless

**Body:**

> The cost engineer used the cost ledger. The ledger held what had been invoiced. The 240,000 was real, recorded on site, and absent from every cost report because no invoice had arrived. The error is accounting. The damage is delivery. Neither training alone catches it.

---

### Slide 6 — Why the gap survives good people

**Body:**

> Accountancy exams rarely test float. Engineering exams rarely test cut-off. Each profession is examined hard on its own half and lightly, or not at all, on the other. The gap sits in the two syllabuses, not in the competence of the people reading them.

---

### Slide 7 — The missing thing has a name

**Body:**

> In the ledger, work done and not yet invoiced is an accrual, and it belongs to the period the work happened in. That is cut-off. In earned value the same work entered EV the moment it was performed.

**Pull line**, set larger, in the accent colour:

> Earned value accrues itself. The cost ledger waits to be told.

---

### Slide 8 — Five questions for your next month-end

**Body:**

> 1. What was delivered this period with no invoice against it?
> 2. Which subcontractor valuations are approved and unpaid?
> 3. Does the cost cut-off date match the progress cut-off date?
> 4. Are materials on site counted as cost, as stock, or as both?
> 5. If CPI moved more than 0.05, which line moved it?

---

### Slide 9 — A cut-off gap reaches the forecast

**Body:**

> Every EAC method starts from the cost you believe you have spent. Understate it and all four inherit the error. They differ only in what they assume about the work still to come.

| EAC method | What it assumes | When that holds |
|---|---|---|
| AC + (BAC − EV) | The overrun was a one-off; the rest runs to plan | An identified, closed, non-recurring cause |
| BAC ÷ CPI | Performance to date continues to completion | Steady work, steady team, no change in method |
| AC + [(BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI)] | Cost and schedule pressure both persist | Recovering a late job by spending into it |
| AC + bottom-up ETC | The original plan no longer predicts anything | Major scope change, or a re-baseline in progress |

---

### Slide 10 — A cut-off gap reaches reported revenue

**Body:**

> On a contract satisfied over time, revenue follows measured progress towards satisfying the obligation. Measure that progress by cost incurred against total expected cost, and a missing accrual understates the numerator. The schedule and the ledger then describe one job to two audiences, in two different states.

---

### Slide 11 — CPI is not cash

**Body:**

> Value earned and cost incurred are both accounting measures, and a job can hold a healthy index while the account empties. Work certified but unpaid, retention held back and materials bought early all sit in that gap. The cash conversion cycle notices it. The cost report does not.

---

### Slide 12 — An AI forecast inherits the ledger it was given

**Body:**

> A model trained on cost reports that were missing accruals learns that CPI runs high. It will not report that the ledger is late, because lateness is not one of its features. Precision and recall are then measured against labels drawn from the same records.

**Pull line**, set larger, in the accent colour:

> A model can be accurate against the data and wrong about the job.

---

### Slide 13 — Which decisions stay human

**Body:**

> A model may propose an accrual. It may not approve one. Cut-off, valuation judgement and forecast sign-off carry personal accountability, and accountability does not transfer to a system that cannot be questioned under challenge. Governed AI is the discipline of keeping those two apart on purpose.

**Foot line:** PCI's position on what a model may and may not decide: pciai.org/ai-policy-for-project-controls

---

### Slide 14 — What a credential ought to examine

**Body:**

> A project lives in the overlap between the ledger and the schedule, and the overlap is where the money is lost. You sign the forecast. Miss the accrual and the number you defended was wrong before you saw it. A credential examining one half leaves you accountable for the other.

# 3. Build spec

## 3.1 Canvas and export

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Slide size | 16:9, designed on a 1920 × 1080 px grid |
| Export | PDF, one page per slide, fonts **embedded and not outlined** |
| Text layer | Live text on every slide. No slide may be a placed image of type |
| Tagging | Tagged PDF, reading order headline → body → table → foot |
| File | `pci-earned-value-missing-accrual-20-slides.pdf`, under 10 MB |
| Never | flattened images, transitions, a template's decorative furniture, stock photography of people pointing at a screen |

The text-layer rule is the whole SEO of this asset. SlideShare pulls the text out of the PDF and that extraction is what gets indexed and what a language model reads. A beautiful deck exported as pictures is a deck nothing can quote.

## 3.2 Palette

Shared with the flagship Pin, so a reader who meets both recognises the second one.

| Role | Hex | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Ground | `#0E1113` | every slide, full bleed |
| Panel | `#15191C` | the slide 3 table, the slide 9 and 15 tables |
| Primary type | `#F5F3EF` | headlines, figures |
| Secondary type | `#B6BCC1` | body copy |
| Muted type | `#8B9298` | eyebrow, foot line, table labels |
| Rules | `#262C31` | dividers, table hairlines |
| Accent | `#E4B95F` | the true CPI on slide 3, the three pull lines, the URL on slide 20 |

The accent is used six times in twenty slides. On slide 3 it goes on **1.05** and nowhere else: the false number is the plain one, the true number is the lit one. Reverse that and the deck teaches the wrong thing.

## 3.3 Type

| Use | Face | Size on the 1920 grid |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | Inter SemiBold | 76 px, 88 px leading, two lines maximum |
| Body | Inter Regular | 40 px, 58 px leading |
| Pull line | Inter SemiBold | 56 px |
| Figures and formulae | IBM Plex Mono Medium | 44 px, `tnum` on every numeral |
| Table text | Inter Medium | 34 px |
| Eyebrow and foot | Inter Medium | 26 px, uppercase, 0.16 em tracking |

Nothing goes below 26 px. A slide is read on a phone at roughly a fifth of this width, and type that fails there fails for most of the audience. Margins 120 px left and right, 96 px top, 88 px bottom. One idea per slide, left-aligned, ragged right, never justified and never centred except inside a table cell.

## 3.4 The furniture, and why it is thin

Every slide carries, at the foot in muted 26 px: the slide number, and `projectcontrolsinstitute.org`. That is all. Slides 3, 4, 5, 9 and 10 add `Illustrative arithmetic` to the same foot line, because those are the slides that travel alone and a screenshot of arithmetic with no such line is a screenshot of a case study PCI never ran.

No logo on any slide except 1 and 20.

## 3.5 Two tests before upload

**The screenshot test.** Take any slide out of the deck and read it cold. Does it state anything that is only true because of the slide before it? If yes, rewrite the slide, not the order.

**The thumbnail test.** Export, scale slide 1 to 480 px wide, look at it from arm's length. The two headline lines and both decimals must survive. Then do the same to slide 3: if the two divisions have gone soft, the table's leading is wrong, not its size.

# 4. After upload

**Embeds are the point.** SlideShare gives every deck an embed code, and a deck embedded in someone else's article is a citation nobody had to be asked for. That is the one honest link this format can earn, and it is why slide 3 has to be worth stealing. Do not gate the deck, do not require an email, do not disable downloading.

**Repurposing.** Slides 3, 8, 9 and 15 each stand alone as a single image elsewhere in the run. Slide 8 in particular is a checklist a cost team will pin up. Export those four separately at 2× rather than screenshotting the PDF.

**Measurement.** Embeds, downloads, and sessions arriving at the Body of Knowledge page in the days after an embed appears. SlideShare will not report a click on the slide 20 address, because that address is typed rather than clicked, so read it at the destination instead. The address printed on the slide is already the shortest path the hub serves; if the arrivals have to be attributed rather than inferred, stand up a short redirect on projectcontrolsinstitute.org itself that resolves to the same page and read it in the hub's own logs. Never a third-party shortener: it hides the domain the reader is being asked to trust, and it dies before the deck does. Views are the least informative number on the page: a deck is doing its job when it turns up inside somebody else's site, and that shows up weeks later.

**What not to do next.** Do not upload a second deck of the same twenty slides re-titled for a different search. One deck, one subject, revised in place. A second version competes with the first, and the first one is the one with the embeds.
