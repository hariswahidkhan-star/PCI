# Cost control in construction: a margin that was never there

*A valuation measures what has been built. A ledger records what has been invoiced. Retention withholds cash from one and nothing from the other. Your margin sits in the gaps.*

Accountancy examinations rarely test float. Engineering examinations rarely test cut-off. Cost control in construction needs both, because every reconciliation sets a measure produced by the commercial team against a cost produced by the ledger, and the margin turns on two things nobody is examined on: the date each side closed, and what was accrued between them.

## Eleven points of margin, and nothing happened on site

The figures below are arithmetic: no project, no client, no currency, and no claim about how often this happens.

Value of work executed at bill rates is 2,200,000. Cost posted to the ledger is 1,850,000.

> 2,200,000 − 1,850,000 = 350,000
>
> 350,000 ÷ 2,200,000 = 0.159

The reconciliation reports just under 16 per cent. It is correct on the evidence in the system, and a careful reviewer signs it.

Now the entry the system has not seen: subcontractors have executed 240,000 of work not applied for at the reporting date. Accrue it.

> 1,850,000 + 240,000 = 2,090,000
>
> 2,200,000 − 2,090,000 = 110,000
>
> 110,000 ÷ 2,200,000 = 0.05

Margin is 5 per cent. Close to eleven points moved on one journal entry, and nothing happened on site. The month closed twice: once for the ledger, once for the measure.

The job did not lose eleven points of margin this period. It never had them.

## Retention is not a discount on cost

Retention is cash withheld under the contract, released on its terms: a tranche at practical completion, the balance when the rectification period ends. It changes when money moves. It does not change what the work cost.

Value comes off the valuation gross of retention. Take cost from the payment run rather than from the value of work executed and it arrives net of the retention held from every subcontractor. The difference lands in the margin line, reported as though the work had been cheaper than it was.

Retention is cash you have not paid. It is not cost you have not incurred.

## The programme will not warn you

Schedule performance index and schedule variance carry no cost term, so a missing accrual cannot move either of them. A clean progress report beside a flattering commercial report is not two signals agreeing. The programme was never asked about cost, and on a summary slide a report with nothing to say reads as a report that agrees.

## The same missing entry cuts your turnover

Where progress for revenue is measured by an input method, cost incurred against total expected cost, the omission runs the other way. Understate cost and you understate measured progress, and with it the revenue recognised for the period.

One month can report better margin and less turnover from one missing accrual. Each figure is locally plausible, and only setting the two side by side exposes the contradiction. This is how two calculations behave, not accounting or legal advice.

## Where cost control in construction actually fails

| Join in the chain | The valuation holds | The ledger holds | Effect on margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cut-off dates | Works measured to the reporting date | Costs posted to the ledger close | Flattered if the ledger closed first |
| Payment applications | All measured work at bill rates | Applications received and certified | Flattered by work executed, not applied for |
| Retention | Value gross of retention | Cash paid, if cost follows the payment run | Flattered: withheld cash read as cost avoided |
| Instructed variations | Nil until the value is agreed | Labour, plant and materials from day one | Depressed: cost lands months before value |

The last row matters as much as the first: understating cost and understating value are the same failure at different joins.

Three lines on the front of the reconciliation settle most of it: the date the ledger closed, the date the works were measured to, and the accrual raised for the gap with its evidence, uncertified measure and goods received not invoiced. No new system is needed, and the assumption sits in front of whoever carries it.

## An assistant will explain the wrong figure fluently

A model drafting the monthly commentary reads the same fields the report reads. It will explain a margin of just under 16 per cent clearly, in a better paragraph than the one it replaced. What it cannot do is notice a cost that was never recorded anywhere. So the governance question is not accuracy but [which figures a model may originate and which it may only restate](https://pciai.org/ai-policy-for-project-controls), settled before the tool goes on.

Both professions are examined hard on their own side of the line. Neither is examined on the handover between them, which is the one place the number is assembled.

## The part that is yours

You sign the forecast. Miss the accrual and the number you defended was wrong before you saw it. Seniority means owning both ledgers, and a credential that examines only one half leaves you accountable for a gap nobody taught you.

So take last month's reconciliation, already issued and agreed. Find the two dates. Find the accrual, or find out there wasn't one. Then check whether the cost you divided by was gross or net of retention.

Whichever it is, you will know something about the reconciliation that the reconciliation does not say.
