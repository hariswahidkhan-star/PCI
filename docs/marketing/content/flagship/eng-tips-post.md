# Systematic error in CPI: we calibrate the numerator and eyeball the denominator

*The line above is the thread title. Everything below the rule is the post body exactly as it is typed in, followed by the signature block that sits under it.*

---

Interest declared first: I work with the Project Controls Institute, an independent certifying body that publishes a project controls syllabus. No link in this post and none in my replies. Every division below is shown so you can check it.

We lost fourteen CPI points to a timing difference in the ledger. Nothing was built any slower and no schedule moved.

Take CPI as an instrument reading and the fault is one we would catch anywhere else. We put real effort into the numerator: rules of credit, whether steel earns on delivery or on erection. Then we divide it by whatever the ledger dropped into the actuals column, which carries a systematic offset nobody states and nobody calibrates out.

Illustrative figures, picked so the division is clean. Not a job, not a client.

Earned value 2,200,000. Invoiced cost 1,850,000.

2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19

That reads under budget and the report says so. Then 240,000 of work turns out to be sitting received and unbilled. Cost becomes 2,090,000.

2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05

Nothing about the work changed between those two lines, and the schedule index does not move, so no delivery-side check catches it.

The two figures are stamped with different dates. Earned value carries the date the work was done. An invoiced cost carries the date a supplier's billing run reached it. Divide one by the other and the reading is the offset between two clocks, not a measure of the work.

The part worth pressing: this is bias, not noise. Random error averages out across control accounts. A timing offset does not. It runs the same direction on every account fed by the same ledger, accumulates while nobody accrues, and corrects in one step when somebody does. On a trend chart that step reads as a productivity collapse, and it gets investigated as one.

It also propagates. EAC = BAC ÷ CPI consumes the same figure:

1.19 ÷ 1.05 = 1.13

Two forecasts thirteen per cent apart, from one timing difference. Where a contingency release or a change threshold is keyed to EAC, that difference makes the decision and nobody knows it is in there.

A dimension without a datum is not a dimension. A CPI without a stated cost basis and cut-off date is not a measurement either, and we hand them over bare every month. One line under the table naming what sits in the denominator turns it back into something a reader can argue with: posted invoices, goods received not invoiced, certified subcontract progress, hours worked at posted rates.

Where the offset is small. On milestone-billed work the billing is triggered by a certified milestone, so the two dates nearly coincide. Over a full year it reverses and nets to nothing, but no decision waits a year. Re-baselining and contingency release happen on a month.

When a forecast moved and nobody in the review could say why, did anyone look at what was in the denominator? There is no published count of how often that is the cause, so I am asking rather than guessing.

The self-serving paragraph, marked as such. An accountant is examined on when a cost may be recognised, almost never on float or an earning rule. An engineer is examined on progress and the earning rule, almost never on cut-off or a contract asset. Both answer their own question correctly. Nobody is examined on the seam, and the seam is where those fourteen points live. The syllabus I work on puts both halves in one examination: PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI), 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas. Set that domain list against your own last close and see how much of it you have ever been tested on.
