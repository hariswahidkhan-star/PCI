# The worst mistake I made was not in a schedule

*LinkedIn founder post — 1,750 characters including hashtags. First person. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

The worst mistake I have made in project controls was not in a schedule.

It was an accrual I did not know to ask about.

I came up through planning. I could defend a critical path to anyone who asked, and often did. Then a finance director asked whether my cost report and her ledger closed on the same date. I said yes. I had not checked. It had never occurred to me that "actual cost" was a decision somebody else made, on a date I did not control.

She was not being difficult. It was the only question that mattered, and I was the only person in the room who could not answer it.

I had been examined on one half of the job and was accountable for both.

Here it is, in round numbers that belong to no project.

Earned value 2,200,000. Invoiced cost 1,850,000.
2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19. The report says you are under budget.

Now accrue the 240,000 of work done and not yet invoiced.
1,850,000 + 240,000 = 2,090,000.
2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05.

Fourteen points on one missing accrual. The error is accounting. The damage is delivery. Neither training alone catches it.

This is not a competence problem, and calling it one is why it never gets fixed. It is a syllabus problem. Accountancy exams rarely test float. Engineering exams rarely test cut-off. We hire people for the overlap, examine them on the halves, then look surprised when the overlap is where the money goes.

That is why the PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) examines 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas across finance, delivery and governed AI, rather than one side thoroughly and the other by hope.

Read the domains against your own last month-end. If you find nothing, tell me. I will have been wrong twice.
