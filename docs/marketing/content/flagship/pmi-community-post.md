# Every EVM question I sat handed me the actual cost. Month-end never has.

*The line above is the thread title. Everything below the rule is the post body exactly as it is typed in.*

---

Interest declared: I work with the Project Controls Institute, an independent certifying body that publishes a project controls syllabus. Discount me accordingly. You will not find a URL anywhere in this thread, including in my replies. None of what follows depends on where I work, and every division in it is one you can do without a calculator.

Every earned value question I have ever sat handed me the actual cost. It had to. You cannot examine a formula without supplying its inputs, and what was being examined was whether I could compute the index and say what it meant. That is a fair thing to examine and the question was well built.

Then you reach a real month-end, and nobody hands you AC. Somebody chooses it, on a date, out of a ledger, on a basis that is almost never written down in the report it ends up in.

Here is what that choice is worth. The figures are illustrative and chosen to divide cleanly. No project, no client, no sector.

Earned value is 2,200,000. The cost in the ledger, invoiced only, is 1,850,000.

2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19

CPI reads 1.19. Comfortably under budget, and that is the line that goes to the steering committee. Then 240,000 of work turns out to have been performed and received with no invoice against it yet. Put it into cost, and cost becomes 2,090,000.

2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05

Fourteen points. Same period, same progress, same data date, same schedule. SPI does not move. Nothing on the delivery side flags any of it, because nothing on the delivery side is wrong.

None of that is anybody being careless. The two figures date themselves differently. Earned value is dated to when the work was performed. An invoiced cost is dated to when a supplier decided to bill for it. Put one over the other and the index is reporting the distance between your progress claim and somebody else's billing run, which is not what anyone in the room reads it as.

I want to be precise about what I am not saying, because this is PMI's house.

I am not saying the PMP is deficient, and I am not suggesting anyone replace one. It is a general management credential examined across the whole job of delivering a project, and it treats earned value the way a general credential should: the definitions, the formulas, the interpretation. The finance side is not at fault either. An accountant is examined on when a cost may be recognised and what a provision has to satisfy, and they answer that question correctly, in the period the invoice belongs to.

Nobody is examined on the seam between them. That accrual needs one person who knows what a contract asset is and what an earning rule is, on the same afternoon, about the same number. Two syllabuses, each complete in itself, and the gap between them sits inside a figure that carries a project manager's name.

What I would do with this, in the order it is useful.

Ask your finance business partner one question: at my cut-off date, against my cost codes rather than the whole entity, what has been received and not yet billed? If the answer is that they already post it before your report goes out, both halves of your index are on the same basis and you have nothing to change. That is worth establishing rather than assuming, and it takes one email.

Then check whether your data date and their close date are the same day. Often they are not, and that is a second problem worth its own thread.

If you set the reporting standard for a portfolio, the durable fix is not a check somebody remembers to run. Make the cost basis and the cut-off date required fields in the report template, so the index arrives with its basis attached instead of depending on whoever compiled it that month. An index that arrives with its basis stated can be challenged on the spot. One that arrives bare gets believed, and a believed number is far harder to correct later than a contested one.

Now the paragraph that serves me, marked so you can skip it. I ended up where I am because the syllabus I work on puts both halves of that ratio inside one examination. It is the PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI): 13 domains, 61 knowledge areas. You need none of it to use anything above, and it is not a substitute for the credential you already hold. Put the domain list beside your own last close and mark every one you have actually sat an examination on. Whatever that mark-up comes to, the marking is the useful part.

One thing I would genuinely like to read from this community. If the cost basis and the cut-off date are already stated in the reports where you work, say so, and say who put them there and what it took to get them into the template. Nobody has published a count of how many portfolios do that, so I am asking rather than assuming.
