# The month ends twice: the cut-off date and the data date

*Two calendars close the same project month, and they close on different days. Everything that happens in between belongs to nobody.*

Picture the cell on a cost report going green. Cost performance index: 1.19.

Somebody says the word contingency out loud for the first time in months, and the room loosens by about a degree.

Nothing in that room is careless. Every figure on the screen is correct and traceable.

Fourteen CPI points went missing in an accrual, not a schedule.

They went missing between the ledger's cut-off date and the site's data date, which is where a project month quietly ends twice.

There is no room and there is no client. I have disguised nothing, because there is nothing to disguise. The numbers below are round and belong to no project, which is the point: they cost you nothing to test against your own last month-end.

## A project month ends twice, on the cut-off date and on the data date

Finance closes to a reporting calendar. Once the cut-off date passes, nothing further may be recorded in that period.

The cost report closes to a data date, set on site, so that progress can be measured while the measurement still describes the job.

The two are rarely the same day. Between them sits work that has been performed and not yet billed. It is real, it has a cost, and it is in neither system.

| | The ledger | The cost report |
|---|---|---|
| Closes on | the cut-off date, set by finance | the data date, set on site |
| Counts | cost that has been invoiced or recorded | work that has been performed and earned |
| Cannot see | work completed after the last valuation | cost that has not reached it yet |
| Bridged by | an accrual | the same accrual, if anyone asks for one |

Nothing produces that accrual on its own. It exists because somebody picked up the telephone and asked a supplier what had been done and not yet billed for.

## What fourteen points look like

Earned value: 2,200,000. Invoiced cost: 1,850,000.

**2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19**

Every unit of cost has bought 1.19 units of work. On the evidence in the system that is the right answer, and I would have signed it.

Now add the 240,000 of work that is done and not yet invoiced.

1,850,000 + 240,000 = 2,090,000

**2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05**

Fourteen points, and not one hour of work on site changed.

At 1.19 you release contingency and take the next package. At 1.05 you hold it and explain yourself. Same job, same month, and the distance between those two meetings is one question nobody was required to ask.

The correction is not free either. It arrives next period as a variance with no operational cause, the hardest kind to explain, because there is nothing to point at. The job did not get worse. The report got honest.

The error is accounting. The damage is delivery. Neither training alone catches it.

## Nobody in that room was unqualified

Accountancy exams rarely test float. Engineering exams rarely test cut-off. Projects need both.

The lazy version of this argument blames somebody, and it is wrong.

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, almost never on a critical path or an earning rule.

An engineer is examined on float and progress measurement, almost never on cut-off or a contract asset.

Both sat hard examinations and passed. Neither was asked about the handover between the two, and the handover is exactly where the 240,000 was sitting.

Syllabuses can be fixed. People do not need fixing.

## The machine does not catch it either

Give a model an earned value of 2,200,000 and an invoiced cost of 1,850,000 and it returns 1.19 in under a second, with a tidier variance narrative attached.

It is not wrong. It answered the question it was asked, using the only cost anybody had recorded. A figure that was never entered leaves no trace for it to find.

So the governance question is not whether the tool calculates correctly. It is which figures a model may originate, which it may only repeat back, and whose name is underneath the output when it reaches a board.

Automation changes how quickly a wrong number travels. It does not move the signature, which is why [the numbers a model may produce and the ones it may only restate](https://pciai.org/ai-policy-for-project-controls) is a shorter document to write than most teams expect.

## What the Institute examines, and what it will not claim

The Project Controls Institute exists to examine that handover rather than either side of it. Three credentials, each with its own Body of Knowledge and examination.

PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) examines 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas. PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI) examines 16 domains and 61 knowledge areas. PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI) examines 16 domains and 63 knowledge areas.

Beneath all three sit 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements. Those are certification requirements set by the Institute, not law, and nothing here is legal, tax or accounting advice.

The Bodies of Knowledge are built 40 per cent finance and reporting, 40 per cent project management, 20 per cent governed AI. That describes the Body of Knowledge, not the examination.

The syllabus is settled, the exam blueprint is still an open decision, and no weighting for it is published. If somebody quotes you one, it did not come from PCI.

A story built on arithmetic ought to say how its own arithmetic is checked. There are 15,613 machine calculation checks running against PFL-AI and PML-AI, all passing. PCL-AI has no equivalent suite. Dropping that scope would make the number sound larger and would also make it untrue.

Here is what you will not find. No accreditation, recognition, endorsement or partnership, because none has happened. No pass rate, no student numbers, no salary uplift, no promised outcome, and nothing that stands in for a statutory or chartered qualification where the work requires one.

If you already hold a credential and want to know what this adds, the useful comparison is [what each of the main certifications actually examines](https://credentialfinder.org/best-project-controls-certification), never who awards it.

## The part that is yours

You sign the forecast. Miss the accrual and the number you defended was wrong before you saw it. Seniority means owning both ledgers. A credential that examines only one half leaves you accountable for a gap that nobody taught you.

So do not take my word for it. Read [the PCL-AI syllabus, domain by domain](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/body-of-knowledge), all 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas, and hold it against the month you have just closed rather than a job description.

Then go and find the work that was done and never billed. If your cost report already knew about it, somebody has built a process worth writing down before they leave.

## The three things people say back

**Isn't the accrual finance's job?**
The posting is, and it stays there. What does not is knowing that a posting is needed at all. That sits with whoever measured the progress, in a system finance does not open. Sending it across is not an accounting skill. It is knowing the two calendars ended on different days.

**Our system posts accruals automatically.**
It posts what it has been told to post, on a rule somebody wrote. Automated accruals fire on a receipt, a goods-received note or a percentage. Work measured on site and never entered as a receipt fires nothing. Automation is only ever as complete as the trigger behind it.

**I am a planner, not an accountant.**
Nobody is asking you to post a journal. You are being asked to know that a number you produce lands in a statement somebody else has to defend, and to say so out loud when the two calendars disagree. That is a reading skill, not a qualification.
