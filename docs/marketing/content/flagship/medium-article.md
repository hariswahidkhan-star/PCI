# The missing accrual that moved CPI by fourteen points

*A cost report can be arithmetically perfect and still wrong. What sits between the ledger's closing date and the site's data date, and who is examined on it.*

Fourteen CPI points went missing in an accrual, not a schedule.

Nothing had happened on site. The job at the end of that month was the job it had been at the start of it, worked by the same people at the same rate. What moved was a journal entry nobody had made yet, and until somebody made it, the cost report said the project was running comfortably under budget.

Here is the whole of it, in round numbers that belong to no project.

Earned value is 2,200,000. Invoiced cost is 1,850,000.

**2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19.**

A cost performance index of 1.19 says every unit of cost bought 1.19 units of work. On the evidence in the system, that is correct. Most people would sign it, and they would be right to.

Now the part the system has not seen. There is 240,000 of work done and not yet invoiced. Accrue it.

1,850,000 + 240,000 = 2,090,000.

**2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05.**

Fourteen points, on one entry.

> The error is accounting. The damage is delivery. Neither training alone catches it.

## Both numbers were defensible, and that is the problem

A cost report and a ledger almost never close on the same date. Finance closes to a reporting calendar. Progress closes to a data date set by the site. Between the two sits work that has been performed and not yet billed.

Everyone in that example did their job properly. The planner earned the work when it was done, which is what earned value is for. The accountant recorded the invoices that existed, which is what a ledger is for.

The accrual is the only figure that lives outside both systems. It exists only if somebody remembers that the two systems disagree about what month it is.

And the correction is not free. It arrives the following period as a variance with no operational cause, which is the hardest kind to explain to people who have already been told the job was ahead.

## Accountancy exams rarely test float. Engineering exams rarely test cut-off.

This is not an argument about competence, and the version of it that blames people is both rude and wrong. A chartered accountant knows precisely what an accrual is and when it must be recognised. A planner knows exactly what earned value counts and what it does not.

The gap is in what each profession is *examined* on, and therefore in what each is assumed to have covered already.

| Subject | Accountancy syllabus | Engineering and PM syllabus | Who actually owns it on a project |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cut-off and revenue recognition | Core | Rarely | Finance, using the project's own numbers |
| Provisions and onerous contracts | Core | Rarely | Finance, triggered by the project's forecast |
| Contract assets and liabilities | Core | Rarely | Finance, driven by billing against progress |
| Critical path, float and logic | Rarely | Core | Planning |
| Rules of credit and progress measurement | Rarely | Core | Project controls |
| Estimate at completion | Consumed, not produced | Produced, not interpreted | Nobody, which is the entire point |
| Governed use of machine output | Emerging | Emerging | Rarely assigned to a named role |

Read the estimate at completion row twice. One profession produces that number. The other consumes it, unchanged, in a statement that will be audited. Neither is examined on the handover between them, so the handover is where the money goes, and the person who signs the report carries a risk that no syllabus prepared them for.

## A model will get the arithmetic right and the answer wrong

Give a model earned value of 2,200,000 and an invoiced cost of 1,850,000 and it returns 1.19, every time, faster than you and in better prose than the last variance narrative you wrote. The calculation is not where the risk sits.

What it cannot do is notice a cost that has not been recorded anywhere. There is no field for an invoice that does not exist.

That narrows the governance question usefully. It is not whether the tool computes correctly. It is which figures a model is allowed to originate, which it may only restate, and whose name goes on the output when it reaches a board pack.

A team that has not written that down is publishing generated commentary on a number nobody has tested. [What an AI policy for project controls has to settle in writing](https://pciai.org/ai-policy-for-project-controls) is a shorter document than most people expect.

## What we examine, and what we will not tell you

The Project Controls Institute exists because of the row in that table marked "nobody". Three credentials, each with its own Body of Knowledge and its own examination.

| | PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) | PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI) | PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Domains | 13 | 16 | 16 |
| Knowledge areas | 61 | 61 | 63 |
| Centre of gravity | The crossing: progress and earned value read against cut-off, recognition and the ledger | Money: recognition, forecasting, working capital, project finance | Delivery: contract, scope, stakeholders, integrated execution |
| What it makes legible | Measurement, to finance | Financial consequence, to delivery | The contract and the plan, to each other |

Underneath them sit 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements. Those are certification requirements established by the Institute. They are not law, and nothing PCI publishes is legal, tax or accounting advice.

The PCL-AI Body of Knowledge is built in proportions of 40 per cent finance and reporting, 40 per cent project management and 20 per cent governed AI. That describes the Body of Knowledge. It does not describe the examination. No examination weighting is published, because the syllabus is settled while the exam blueprint is still an open decision, and if you ever see a PCI exam weighting quoted, it did not come from us.

An article built on arithmetic ought to say how its own arithmetic is checked. There are 15,613 machine calculation checks running against PFL-AI and PML-AI, all passing. PCL-AI has no equivalent suite. Saying so costs us the cleaner sentence and is the reason the number is worth anything.

Here is the rest of what we are not going to say, since every other certification article you have read this year said some of it.

No accreditation, recognition, endorsement or partnership. None has happened, and a senior practitioner checks.

No pass rates, no student numbers, no salary uplift, no guaranteed outcome, because we either do not have those figures or would not publish them if we did. And no suggestion that any of this substitutes for a statutory or chartered qualification where the work requires one.

What a credential does is give an employer a defensible reason to believe you can do specific things. If you already hold one, [a like-for-like comparison of what the main controls certifications examine](https://credentialfinder.org/best-project-controls-certification) is more useful than another brochure.

## The part that is actually yours

You sign the forecast. Miss the accrual and the number you defended was wrong before you saw it. Seniority means owning both ledgers, and a credential that examines only one half leaves you accountable for a gap that nobody taught you.

So do not take anyone's word for the syllabus, including ours. [Read the PCL-AI Body of Knowledge in full](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/body-of-knowledge), all 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas of it, and hold it against your own last month-end.

Then go and find the accrual nobody asked about.

## Questions people ask

**Isn't the accrual finance's job?**
The posting is. The knowledge that a posting is needed usually is not. Only the person tracking progress knows that work was completed after the last valuation, and only the ledger knows it was never invoiced. The entry belongs to finance; the trigger belongs to the project, and it fires only if somebody is reading both.

**Why not take an accountancy qualification and a project management one?**
You can, and some people do. It takes years and still leaves the handover unexamined, because each syllabus stops at its own boundary. Neither will ask you what a revised estimate at completion does to recognised revenue, which is the question a live project poses every single month.

**What does "governed AI" mean in an examination?**
The governed use of machine output in decisions someone signs. Reading precision, recall and F1 rather than a headline accuracy figure. Knowing the base rate before believing the score. Recognising when a model's training data no longer resembles the job in front of you. Keeping an audit trail when generated text enters a report with your name on it.

**Does a PCI credential replace a chartered qualification?**
No, and it is not built to. Where statutory or chartered status is required for a piece of work, that requirement is met by the relevant statutory or chartered qualification. These credentials examine the crossing between finance and delivery, which is a different question and one that neither of those routes asks.
