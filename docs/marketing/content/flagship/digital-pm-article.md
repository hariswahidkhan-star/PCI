# Agile earned value: what your sprint report can't see

*Your velocity is real. Your cost report has a hole in it, and the hole is the invoice that hasn't arrived.*

Your best sprint this quarter was probably the one whose invoices hadn't arrived yet. Agile earned value is the budget attached to work your team has had accepted, divided by the cost your ledger has posted. Accepted work closes at sprint end. Cost closes at the ledger's cut-off. Two dates, one division, and nothing on your board says they disagree.

## Agile earned value in three numbers

Earned value needs three numbers. Delivery produces two of them; finance produces the third, which is where this breaks.

Earned value ÷ actual cost is the cost performance index, CPI. Earned value ÷ planned value is the schedule performance index, SPI. A burnup and a cost extract run both.

| Term | On a sprint delivery | Failure mode | The rule that fixes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planned value | Budget for stories due for acceptance today | Re-pointing the backlog rewrites it | Fix the points-to-money conversion per release; log re-points as scope change |
| Earned value | Budget for stories accepted, not stories in review | "Done" that means merged | Credit only on acceptance by someone who can refuse it |
| Actual cost | Posted cost: payroll, contractors, cloud, licences, media | Bills that haven't arrived | Accrue work performed and not invoiced at the cut-off |
| CPI = EV ÷ AC | Accepted work bought per unit of cost | Reads highest when cost is latest | Publish the ledger close date beside it |

## Agile fixed earned value's oldest problem by accident

The hardest part of traditional earned value is percent-complete. Somebody declares a six-month work package 60 per cent done, an opinion with a decimal point attached.

Small stories delete the opinion. A story is accepted or it isn't, so credit is binary and nobody estimates a fraction of anything. Earned value's strictest rule, no credit until complete and then full credit, is unusable on long packages and near-perfect on two-day ones.

So the old objection has it backwards. Measurement is the half agile does better. Cost is the half that breaks.

## The arithmetic that costs fourteen points

Arithmetic, not a case study: no client, no currency, no frequency claim.

Earned value is 2,200,000. Cost posted against the job is 1,850,000.

> 2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19

CPI reads 1.19. Every unit of cost has bought 1.19 units of accepted work, and on the evidence in the system that is right. You'd sign it. I'd sign it.

Now the entry the system hasn't seen. At the reporting date, 240,000 of work is performed and not yet invoiced: the contract front-end team, the month's cloud, media booked and not billed. Accrue it.

> 1,850,000 + 240,000 = 2,090,000
>
> 2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05

Fourteen index points on one journal entry. No ticket moved. Nobody worked differently. The month closed twice: once for the ledger, once for the board.

## Your burnup can't warn you, and velocity is worse

SPI and schedule variance carry no cost term, and velocity has no money in it at all. An accrual cannot move any of them, and neither can its absence.

A clean burnup beside a flattering CPI is not two signals agreeing. One of them has nothing to say about cost, and on a summary slide that reads as confirmation. A team can hold velocity exactly while the cost of holding it doubles.

SPI also expires. As delivery completes, earned value approaches planned value whatever the date, so SPI drifts to 1.0 on a project that is plainly late. Read it mid-delivery, not at the end.

## What to ask finance before you publish a CPI

**When did the cost ledger close?** If it closed on the 25th and your sprint on the 31st, six days of cost is missing from the division.

**What is accrued for work done and not invoiced, on what evidence?** A good answer names goods received not invoiced and uncertified subcontractor work. A round number is a plug, and plugs surface six weeks later.

**When does that accrual reverse?** Into the wrong period, and next month's CPI collapses while a room of people reads a bookkeeping entry as a productivity problem.

## For agencies, the entry moves revenue the other way

Where progress for revenue is measured on an input basis, cost incurred against total expected cost, understating cost understates measured progress and therefore revenue recognised.

So one month reports better cost performance and less revenue from the same omission. Only a reconciliation shows the contradiction, and neither report asks for one. That is how the calculations behave, not accounting advice.

## An assistant will explain the wrong CPI beautifully

The model drafting your sprint summary reads the field your report reads. It will explain a CPI of 1.19 clearly and write a better paragraph than the one it replaced.

What it cannot do is notice the accrual, because the accrual was never a record. No automated check catches it either: a cost nobody posted breaks no rule. So the question worth settling is not whether the output is accurate, but [which numbers a model may originate and which it may only repeat back](https://pciai.org/ai-policy-for-project-controls).

## The part that's yours

You sign the forecast. Miss the accrual and the number you defended was wrong before you saw it. Seniority means owning both ledgers, and a credential that examines only one half leaves you accountable for a gap nobody taught you.

So read [the 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas in full](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/body-of-knowledge) against your last cost report, which is a harder test than any brochure. Then find the accrual nobody asked about.

## Common questions

**Can you do this without story points?**

Yes. Earned value needs a unit of accepted scope with money attached, not points. Budget per feature, per work item or per release increment all work, provided the conversion is fixed for the period and applied on acceptance, not on start.

**Isn't actual cost just the timesheet?**

No. Timesheets record effort; the ledger records cost, including contractors, cloud and licences that arrive weeks after the work. CPI on timesheets is an internal efficiency number; CPI on posted cost with no accrual is worse, because it looks like the real one.

**Does this work on fixed-price delivery?**

Yes, and it matters more there. On time and materials, unbilled cost eventually reaches the client. On fixed price it reaches your margin instead, so the accrual is where a healthy-looking engagement turns out to have spent its contingency already, one sprint at a time.
