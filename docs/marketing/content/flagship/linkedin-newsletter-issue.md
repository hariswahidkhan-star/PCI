# The number your ledger and your schedule disagree on

*The Overlap, issue one. A monthly letter about the places where project finance and project delivery report the same month differently. It arrives in the week after most month-ends close, while the answer still matters.*

Accountancy exams rarely test float. Engineering exams rarely test cut-off. A project needs both, and the overlap is where the money is lost.

That is what this letter is for. Each issue takes one number that two systems report differently, works the arithmetic in full, and names the question that would have caught it. Issue one starts with a cost performance index that was defended in good faith and was wrong before anyone read it.

## One month, two numbers

Round figures that belong to no project. Run them against your own last close.

Earned value: 2,200,000
Invoiced cost: 1,850,000

2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19

CPI is 1.19. The cost report says the job is running under budget, and the report is not lying. It is telling you exactly what the ledger has seen.

Now add the 240,000 of work that is done and not yet invoiced.

1,850,000 + 240,000 = 2,090,000
2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05

Fourteen points, on one accrual.

At 1.19 you release contingency. At 1.05 you have a conversation. Same month, same site, and both numbers are defensible in the room.

Nobody here was careless, and that is the part worth your attention. Earned value has already counted that work, because the work happened. The ledger has not seen it, because no invoice has arrived. The figure sits outside both systems, visible only to someone who thinks to ring a supplier and ask what they have done and not yet billed for.

You cannot ask for a figure you have never been taught exists.

The error is accounting. The damage is delivery. Neither training alone catches it.

## The question that would have caught it

One sentence, short enough to say out loud in any project review:

**"Does my cost report close on the same date as the ledger, and what is sitting between the two?"**

Ask it before the pack goes out, not after the variance is queried. If the answer comes back as a pause, the pause is the number.

## Which syllabus examines this

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision has to satisfy. Almost never on a critical path or an earning rule.

An engineer is examined on float and progress measurement. Almost never on cut-off or a contract asset.

Both are competent at what they were examined on. The gap is not in the people, it is in the syllabus each of them sat, and we keep staffing the overlap with specialists in the halves.

You sign the forecast. If the accrual was missed, the number you defended was wrong before you saw it, and the seniority that let you sign it is precisely what makes it yours.

The Project Controls Institute exists to examine the overlap rather than either half of it. Three credentials, and this is their shape:

**PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI)**: 13 domains, 61 knowledge areas.
**PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI)**: 16 domains, 61 knowledge areas.
**PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI)**: 16 domains, 63 knowledge areas.

Beneath them sit 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements. Those are certification requirements set by the Institute. They are not law, and nothing in this letter is accounting, tax or legal advice.

The Body of Knowledge is 40 per cent finance and reporting, 40 per cent project management, 20 per cent governed AI. That is the Body of Knowledge's shape and not the examination's. The exam blueprint is an open decision, so you will not read a weighting for it here until it is settled, and you should distrust anyone who quotes you one before then.

A letter built on arithmetic ought to say how its own arithmetic is checked. 15,613 machine calculation checks run against PFL-AI and PML-AI, all passing. PCL-AI has no equivalent suite, and I would rather write the longer sentence than the shorter one.

## The AI part, honestly

A model will produce a forecast faster than you can check it, and it will produce one whether or not the accrual was ever posted. What it cannot do is tell you the ledger closed on the 25th while the site kept working until the 30th. Automation changes how quickly a wrong number arrives. It does not move whose name is on it.

That is why governed AI is examined as a discipline and not treated as a tool: what a model may propose, [what it may never decide on its own](https://pciai.org/ai-policy-for-project-controls), and what a human has to be able to reconstruct months later when someone asks how the figure was arrived at.

## What every issue will do

Four fixed parts, so you know what you are subscribing to.

**One number.** A figure that two systems report differently, worked in full with the division shown. Illustrative arithmetic every time, round figures, no client and no sector, so you can check it instead of admiring it.

**One question.** The sentence that would have caught it, short enough to ask in a meeting without a slide.

**One piece of syllabus.** Which part of the discipline examines the thing, and where the professions' examinations stop short of it.

**Marked wrong.** Anything this letter gets wrong, corrected in the next issue, in the same place, at the same length. This issue has nothing to correct. Later ones will, and you will not have to go looking for it.

There is no shortage of material. The three Bodies of Knowledge carry 92 sector case studies between them, 26, 33 and 33 across the volumes, and every one of them is a month where two systems could disagree.
