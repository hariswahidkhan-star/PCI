# In EVM, should Actual Cost (AC) be invoiced cost or accrued cost?

*The heading above is the question this answers, not text you type. Everything below the rule is the answer body exactly as it goes into the answer box, in Stack Exchange markdown. It contains no link, no institute name and no disclosure, because it promotes nothing.*

---

Accrued cost: the cost incurred for the work performed, at the same cut-off date the earned value is claimed at. Not invoiced, and definitely not committed.

The reason is structural rather than clerical. CPI divides two numbers that have to sit on the same basis. Earned value is accrual by construction: it is claimed when the work is performed. If the denominator only lands when a supplier's billing run catches up, the ratio is measuring your suppliers' invoicing habits, not your team's performance. Every mainstream EVM definition of AC turns on the word *incurred*, and incurred is an accrual concept, not a payment one.

| Basis | What it records | In AC? |
|---|---|---|
| Committed | orders placed, nothing received | No: intent, not consumption |
| Invoiced | the supplier has billed you | No: measures billing lag |
| Incurred | work performed, goods received, hours worked before the cut-off | Yes |

Illustrative figures, not a real job:

Earned value 2,200,000, invoiced cost 1,850,000. 2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19.

Now accrue 240,000 of work done and not yet invoiced. Cost becomes 2,090,000. 2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05.

Fourteen CPI points, with the same work, the same progress date and the same schedule. The error is in the ledger cut-off and the damage lands on delivery.

It does not stop at the CPI cell. Every CPI-based estimate at completion consumes AC, so BAC ÷ 1.19 sits below BAC ÷ 1.05 by the ratio of the two indices: 1.05 ÷ 1.19 = 0.88, about 12 per cent. If contingency release or a change threshold keys off EAC, an accrual nobody chased is making that decision.

**In practice:**

1. Write the AC definition down at control-account level and publish it beside the number: posted invoices, plus goods received not invoiced, plus certified subcontract progress, plus hours worked at posted rates.
2. Make the progress date and the ledger cut-off the same date. A Friday progress date reported against a month-end close manufactures a swing every month and reverses it the next.
3. While the accrual is still an estimate, report CPI both ways. If the pair straddles 1.00 you have no evidence of performance in either direction, and saying so is more defensible than picking whichever number is kinder.

**Where it matters less:** on milestone-billed work the invoice follows a certified milestone and the drift is small, and over a full year the timing difference washes out anyway. Nothing is decided annually, though. Re-baselining, contingency release and a board's view of the job are all decided on a month.

**Where people over-correct:** accruing commitments. A purchase order raised is not a cost incurred, and putting it in AC pushes CPI down for the mirror image of the reason invoicing lag pushes it up. Accrue receipt and performance, never intent.

**One diagnostic worth keeping.** If CPI climbs steadily through the year and drops when the auditors arrive, look at the accrual before you read it as a productivity trend. An unposted timing difference corrected in one go produces exactly that shape.
