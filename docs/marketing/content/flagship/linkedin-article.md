# Your cost report and your ledger close on different dates

Your cost report cut-off and your ledger close almost never fall on the same date, and the difference between them is work that is done and not yet invoiced. Earned value has already counted it. The ledger has not seen it. Until somebody accrues it, cost performance index reads high, the forecast reads comfortable, and both numbers are defensible.

Both systems have a date. Finance closes to its own reporting calendar. Planning closes on the data date. Nobody owns the days in between, and that is where this lives.

## Why does CPI move when nothing on site changed?

Take round numbers that belong to no project.

Earned value is 2,200,000. Invoiced cost is 1,850,000.

2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19.

The report says the job is running under budget, and on the evidence in front of you it is.

Now take the 240,000 of work that is done and not yet invoiced. Accrue it.

1,850,000 + 240,000 = 2,090,000.

2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05.

Fourteen points, on one journal entry. Nothing on site slowed down. A cost that already existed was recorded, and the number you were about to defend moved.

The error is accounting. The damage is delivery. Neither training alone catches it.

## What sits between the cost report cut-off and the data date?

Five things sit in that gap. Each is invisible from one side and obvious from the other, which is why it survives to month-end.

| What sits in the gap | What earned value has done with it | What the ledger has done with it | Effect on CPI if nobody acts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Work done, not yet invoiced | Earned it in full | Nothing, until it is accrued | Reads high |
| Materials on site, not yet invoiced | Earned it, if the rules of credit allow materials to earn | Nothing, until goods-received-not-invoiced is posted | Reads high |
| Invoice settled for work not yet performed | Earned nothing | Recorded the cost in full | Reads low |
| Subcontractor application under assessment | Earned at your assessed quantity | Accrued at the certified value, which is a different number | Moves either way |
| Retention held | Earned in full | Carries a liability, holds the cash | None, but the cash curve is wrong |

Row three is the one people forget, and it does the opposite damage. Cost is in, value is not yet earned, CPI reads low, and a healthy job gets put on a recovery plan it did not need. Both errors are single entries. Both are defensible in isolation. Neither is visible unless somebody is reading the two systems side by side.

## Why doesn't either professional examination catch it?

Accountancy exams rarely test float. Engineering exams rarely test cut-off. Projects need both.

This is not a competence argument, and the version of it that blames people is both rude and wrong. A chartered accountant knows precisely what an accrual is and when it must be recognised. A planner knows exactly what earned value counts and what it does not. Neither is short of the ability to learn the other's half.

The gap is in what each profession is examined on, and therefore in what each is assumed to have covered. Nobody is examined on the handover. So the handover is where the money goes, and the person who signs the cost report carries a risk that neither syllabus prepared them for.

You are not being asked to become an accountant. You are being asked to know which of your numbers was produced under someone else's assumptions, and on what date those assumptions stop.

## What happens when an AI drafts the report?

A model reproduces the arithmetic it is given, faster and more consistently than you do, and with better prose. Given earned value of 2,200,000 and cost of 1,850,000 it will return 1.19 every time, correctly, and write a confident paragraph explaining the favourable variance.

It cannot see an invoice that does not exist.

That is the whole of the governance question, and it is narrower than the debate usually allows. The issue is not whether the model calculates correctly. It is which figures a model may originate, which it may only restate, and whose name goes on the result.

A team that has not decided that in writing is publishing generated commentary on a number nobody has tested. The practical controls for [generative AI in project reporting](https://pciai.org/generative-ai-project-reporting) are worth settling before the tool is switched on, not after the first board pack.

## What is the test to run at your next month-end?

Four questions. None of them needs a system change, and the answers take an afternoon.

1. **What date does the ledger close, and what date does progress cut off?** If they differ, write down the gap in days. That number is your exposure window, and most teams have never stated it out loud.
2. **What is sitting in goods-received-not-invoiced at that date, and has earned value already counted any of it?** Anything counted on one side and not the other is your accrual, whether or not anyone has posted it.
3. **Which invoices in this month's actual cost pay for work that has not yet been earned?** Advance payments and mobilisation are the usual answers. Left in, they make a healthy job look sick.
4. **Who signs the accrual, and does that person read the earned value report?** If they are two different people who have never met, you have found the seam.

If nobody in your team can answer all four from memory, that is not a failure. That is the finding, and it is worth more than the report you were about to issue.

## Questions people ask

**What is an accrual on a project cost report?**
An accrual records a cost that has been incurred but not yet invoiced, so the period carries the cost of the work actually done in it. On a project this is usually subcontractor progress, plant on hire or materials delivered late in the month. Without it, actual cost understates the true position and every ratio built on actual cost inherits the error.

**Why does a missing accrual make CPI look better than the job is?**
Cost performance index divides earned value by actual cost. Earned value counts the work as soon as it is performed, while actual cost waits for an invoice. Omit the accrual and the numerator includes work the denominator has not paid for yet, so the ratio reads high. It corrects itself next month, which is the trap: the correction arrives as a variance nobody can explain.

**Should earned value and the ledger use the same cut-off date?**
Ideally yes, and often they cannot, because the ledger answers to a reporting calendar and progress answers to the site. Where the dates cannot be aligned, the gap must be stated, measured and accrued for every period, and the same convention has to be used every month. A gap you have quantified is a control. A gap you have not is a surprise.

**Is this a finance problem or a project controls problem?**
It belongs to whoever signs the number, which in practice means both. Finance owns the recognition rule and the closing date. Project controls owns the earning rule and the progress date. The failure is never in either rule; it is in the handover between them, which no single role is examined on and both assume the other has covered.
