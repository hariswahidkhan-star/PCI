# ACWP: invoiced, committed or incurred? A basis question worth fourteen CPI points

*The line above is the thread title. Everything below the rule is the post body exactly as it is typed in, followed by the signature block that sits under it.*

---

Interest declared: I work with the Project Controls Institute, an independent certifying body that publishes a project controls syllabus. Discount me accordingly. Every division below checks in your head.

Fourteen CPI points can go missing in an accrual. Not in a schedule, not in a progress claim, not in anything a planner did.

Illustrative round figures, chosen because they divide cleanly. Not a real job.

BCWP is 2,200,000. Cost on the ledger, invoiced, is 1,850,000.

2,200,000 ÷ 1,850,000 = 1.19

Under budget, and the report goes up saying so. Then 240,000 of work turns out to be performed and received, not yet invoiced. Cost becomes 2,090,000.

2,200,000 ÷ 2,090,000 = 1.05

Same period, same progress, same logic, same data date. Fourteen points, and SPI does not move, so nothing a planner checks first flags it.

None of that is a mistake in the method. ACWP is the actual cost of work performed. Incurred, not invoiced. The accrual is not a correction applied to ACWP, it is part of what ACWP means. What we divide by is whatever the system drops into the actuals column, usually one of three:

Committed. Orders placed, not necessarily received. Overstates cost, understates CPI, and puts unperformed work into a ratio about performed work.

Invoiced. What suppliers have got round to billing by your cut-off. Understates cost, overstates CPI, and its timing belongs to somebody else's billing run.

Incurred. Performed and received, invoiced or not. The one the definition asks for.

BCWP is accrual by construction: you claim it when the work is done. Put invoiced cost underneath and the two halves sit on different bases, so the ratio has stopped measuring performance. It is measuring the gap between your progress claim and your suppliers' administration. And that is before the second problem: your data date and the ledger cut-off are rarely the same day.

We will argue for an hour about whether an activity is 40 or 45 per cent complete. On a single activity at the scale above, that argument is worth a fraction of a CPI point. Nobody in the room asks which of the three quantities is under the line, and that is worth fourteen.

The bit I expect to be told I have wrong. I think the basis statement belongs to the planner, not to finance. Not the accrual itself: posting that is the cost engineer's or the QS's job, and they are not careless to leave it, because the ledger is answering its own question correctly. But the CPI goes out under a report with your name on it, and by the time anyone asks what was in the denominator it has been your number for two quarters. A line under the table naming the cost basis and the cut-off costs nothing and is the only version of that number anyone can defend.

Counter-arguments I know are coming. Cost is not the planner's remit. On milestone-billed work the drift is small. Over a year it reverses anyway. That last one I would push back on: nothing is decided annually. Re-baselining, releasing contingency, telling a board the job is fine, all of it happens on a month.

So: which of the three is in your actuals column at cut-off, and were you told or did you find out? I have not counted this and I will not guess at how common each answer is. Post yours and the thread is worth more than the post.

Why I argue it. An accountant is examined on when a cost may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, almost never on float or an earning rule. A planner is examined on float, progress and the earning rule, almost never on cut-off or a contract asset. Nobody in that description is incompetent. Each side is examined on one half of a number that only exists as a ratio of the two. The credential I work on examines both halves: PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI), 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas. Read them against your own last close and count how many you have been examined on: https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/body-of-knowledge

Tell me where the argument fails. I would rather be corrected on a board than in a meeting.
