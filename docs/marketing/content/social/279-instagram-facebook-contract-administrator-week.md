# What a contract administrator does all week, day by day

*Instagram / Facebook carousel — 10 slides, 1080 × 1350. Caption below; the first 125 characters carry it before the fold.*

**Caption (first 125 characters before the fold):**

The valuation was £2.31m. The payment was £2.86m. Nobody miscounted. Two notices went out late.

Ten slides on what a contract administrator actually does between Monday and Friday, and the arithmetic on slide 3 that explains why the dates matter more than the numbers.

Full guide in the link in bio.

**Slide 1 — The job, defined**
Contract administration is the day-to-day operation of an agreement after signature: issuing and receiving notices inside the periods the contract sets, keeping records capable of substantiating entitlement, valuing and certifying payment, administering instructions, and maintaining the programme against which time is measured. It creates no rights. It preserves the ones already bought.

**Slide 2 — The week in one sentence**
Monday's instruction becomes Tuesday's notice, becomes Wednesday's record, becomes Thursday's valuation, becomes Friday's certificate. Break the chain anywhere and the money moves without the evidence behind it.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic**
Application for **£2.86m** submitted on the due date, **4 March**. The true valuation is **£2.31m**.

Payment notice due within 5 days: **9 March**. Not served.
Final date for payment, 21 days after the due date: **25 March**.
Pay-less notice due 7 days before that: **18 March**. Not served.

With no valid notice, the notified sum is the sum applied for. **£2.86m** falls due on 25 March.

Cost of two missed diary entries: **£550,000**, recoverable later, funded now.

**Slide 4 — Monday: instructions and the change register**
Every instruction gets a number, a date, a scope description and an initial view on cost and time, on the day it arrives. An instruction sitting in an inbox is an unpriced variation, and unpriced variations are the largest single reason a final account is a surprise.

**Slide 5 — Tuesday: the diary of dates**
Keep one register of every live period: notices due, responses due, decisions due, certificates due. Notice periods run from awareness, not from convenience. This register is the cheapest document on the project and it protects the most value.

**Slide 6 — Wednesday: records that substantiate**
"Capable of substantiating" is a higher standard than "we have photos". It means allocated labour, plant on and off hire, quantities installed, delays logged against activities, all made at the time and tied to the same references the claim will use. Records assembled afterwards read like records assembled afterwards.

**Slide 7 — Thursday: valuation**
Measure the work, apply the contract rates, value the instructed changes, deal with materials on site, and reconcile to the schedule of values. Value what has been done, not what the programme said would be done. Those two agree less often than anyone expects.

**Slide 8 — Friday: certification**
Certify to the mechanism, in writing, by the date. A certificate issued a day late is not a small administrative point; in many forms it changes what becomes payable. This is the slide people screenshot after it has happened to them once.

**Slide 9 — The monthly overlay**
On top of the week: the programme update, the position on every open extension of time, the movement in the final account forecast, and the release or top-up of contingency. All to one cut-off, so that the cost report, the certificate and the accounts describe the same month. A certificate that slips past that cut-off moves real money into the next one, which is why [building a cash flow forecast from certified value](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/project-cash-flow-forecasting) is part of this job and not only the finance team's.

**Slide 10 — The five-minute test**
Pick an instruction from three months ago. Ask for four numbers: date of awareness, date of notice, value quoted, days it moved completion. If that takes longer than five minutes, the regime is not running, whatever the file structure looks like.
