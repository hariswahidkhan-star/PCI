# FIDIC 1999 vs 2017: the claims change most teams still miss

*LinkedIn text post — 1634 characters including hashtags. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

The 2017 change most teams still miss is not the 84-day detailed claim.
It is that the employer is on the clock too, and a late employer claim can now be barred the same way yours can.

Under the 1999 forms the 28-day notice sat in the contractor's claims clause and worked as a condition precedent. Employer claims ran on a looser test, notified as soon as practicable, which in practice meant at final account with a schedule of deductions attached.

The 2017 forms put both parties through one claims procedure. Same 28 days from awareness. Same fully detailed claim by day 84. Same determination route through the Engineer.

What that is worth:

An employer raises £1.2m of alleged delay damages and defect costs on day 340. Under 1999 you argue about "as soon as practicable", then about quantum.

Under 2017 you ask one question before quantum: where is the notice from day 28? Day 340 is 312 days late, and justifying that is their burden rather than yours.

Two more changes worth diarising. A late notice can survive where the claiming party justifies it and the other party does not object within 14 days, so that 14-day window is now a live obligation on your side too. And where the Engineer gives no determination inside the time limit, the claim counts as rejected, which puts the DAAB in reach instead of leaving you waiting.

Administer a 2017 contract with 1999 habits and you keep your own clock while ignoring theirs. That is a one-sided time bar you have chosen to operate.

Particular conditions amend all of this. Read them, and take legal advice from lawyers.
