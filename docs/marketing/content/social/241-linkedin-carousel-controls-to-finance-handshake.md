# Month-end close: the controls-to-finance handshake

*LinkedIn document post — 10 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

Your CPI fell from 0.970 to 0.928 last month and nobody spent a penny more than planned.
Site was surveyed on the 28th. The ledger closed on the 31st. That is the whole story.

Ten slides on the handshake between project controls and finance, and the five reconciliations that stop it going wrong.

---

**Slide 1 — The handshake**
Month-end close is the point where delivery hands its numbers to finance and finance turns them into revenue, margin and a balance sheet. The handshake is the cut-off date. Almost every bad close starts there.

**Slide 2 — Two calendars, one truth**
Progress gets surveyed when the site can be walked. The ledger closes when the ledger closes. If those are different dates, cost sits against work that has not been counted, and the indices report a performance problem that does not exist.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic** Control account, month 9. Earned value measured to the 28th is £4.10m. Actual cost to the 31st is £4.42m.

CPI = 4.10 ÷ 4.42 = **0.928**. Three days of production at £62,000 a day is £186,000 of work done and not surveyed. Accrue it into earned value and EV becomes £4.286m.

CPI = 4.286 ÷ 4.42 = **0.970**. On a BAC of £48m, EAC = BAC ÷ CPI gives £51.72m at 0.928 and £49.48m at 0.970. **£2.24m of forecast overspend created by a calendar.**

**Slide 4 — Accruals are evidence, not a percentage**
An accrual needs a goods received note, a signed timesheet, a subcontractor's application, a delivery record or a measured quantity with a rate against it. "The same as last month" is not evidence. It reproduces last month's error and hides the drift.

**Slide 5 — Earn to the same instant you spend**
Fix the rules of credit before the first measurement, not during the argument. Whatever method you use — milestones, units complete, physical measurement — apply it to the same cut-off as actual cost, every period, on every control account.

**Slide 6 — The five reconciliations**
Commitments to the ledger. Accruals to goods received. Earned value to the signed progress claim. Forecast to the baseline change log. Cost report to the trial balance. Each one is a sum that ties or does not, and the close is not finished until all five tie.

**Slide 7 — The forecast is a decision**
Lock the estimate at completion with a named cause beside it, not just a number. "Rate error on cable pulling, embedded in the remaining 1,350m" is a forecast. "CPI trend" is an extrapolation wearing a forecast's clothes.

**Slide 8 — What finance does with your number**
Under IFRS 15, where control transfers over time, revenue is measured by progress towards satisfying the obligation. A cost-to-cost input method takes cost incurred over total expected cost, so your EAC is the denominator of somebody's revenue figure. Move the EAC, move the revenue.

**Slide 9 — And the test nobody warns you about**
When expected costs exceed expected consideration, the loss is recognised in full, in the period it becomes expected. Not spread over the months remaining. A forecast that quietly crosses the contract price is a same-period accounting event, and the person who finds out last is usually the project director.

**Slide 10 — Five moves, in order**
Freeze. Accrue. Earn. Forecast. Reconcile. Compress the days for a small job, but do not reorder the moves.
