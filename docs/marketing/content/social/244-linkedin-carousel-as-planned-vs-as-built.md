# As-planned vs as-built: reading the two bars side by side

*LinkedIn document post — 10 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

Planned in 44 weeks. Built in 57. The claim says 13 weeks and the defence says prove it.
Both are right, because 13 weeks is a total, and a total has no cause attached to it.

Ten slides on how to read an as-planned versus as-built comparison without over-claiming what it shows.

---

**Slide 1 — What the method is**
Two bar charts stacked on the same time axis. The accepted baseline above, the as-built record below, activity for activity. It is the simplest delay comparison there is, and the most commonly misused.

**Slide 2 — What it shows, and what it does not**
It shows when things actually happened. It does not show why, it does not carry logic, and it cannot tell you which activity was controlling completion on any given day. Read it as a picture of the outcome, not as an explanation of it.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic** Planned duration 44 weeks. As-built duration 57 weeks. Gross slip **13 weeks**.

Split into four windows: Weeks 1–12: **0 weeks** lost. Weeks 13–28: **6 weeks** lost — piling, ground conditions. Weeks 29–44: **2 weeks** lost — steel deliveries.

Weeks 45–57: **5 weeks** lost — commissioning, incomplete test packs. 6 + 2 + 5 = 13. Same total, four different causes, at least two different owners.

**Slide 4 — Build the as-built from records, not memory**
Site diaries, daily allocation sheets, delivery notes, inspection and test records, progress photographs with intact metadata, signed progress claims. Where two sources disagree, show both and say which you relied on. An as-built assembled from an updated programme alone inherits every update error in it.

**Slide 5 — Match the two programmes before you compare them**
A 3,200-line baseline against a 400-line as-built produces a comparison nobody can check. Roll both to the same level, keep the activity identifiers aligned, and state the mapping. Any activity that exists in only one of the two needs a sentence explaining why.

**Slide 6 — Windows turn a picture into an analysis**
Cut the project at events that changed it: a milestone, a design freeze, a variation instruction, a hand-over of an area. Measure the slip inside each window against the position at the start of that window. This is the step that converts 13 weeks into an argument.

**Slide 7 — The critical path moves, and that is the point**
In window two the controlling path ran through piling. By window four it ran through commissioning. A single end-to-end comparison averages those together and reports one delay with one cause, which is how a claim ends up attributing commissioning delay to ground conditions.

**Slide 8 — Where it sits against the other methods**

| Method | Uses logic? | Retrospective? | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| As-planned vs as-built | No | Yes | Small jobs, poor records, first look |
| Windowed as-planned vs as-built | Partly | Yes | Where the critical path moved |
| Time impact analysis | Yes | No, prospective | Live events, contract notice cycle |
| Collapsed as-built | Yes | Yes | Where a full as-built network exists |

**Slide 9 — Say what it cannot do, before the other side does**
It carries no concurrency test. It cannot separate employer delay from contractor delay inside the same window without further evidence. It sits low in the hierarchy of accepted techniques for a reason, and presenting it as more than it is damages the rest of the claim.

**Slide 10 — When it is still the right choice**
Poor records, a modest sum, an early commercial conversation, or a sense check on somebody else's expensive analysis. If a time impact analysis says eight weeks and the two bars say twenty-two, one of them is wrong, and finding out which is an afternoon's work.
