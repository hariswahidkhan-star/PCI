# BAC is fixed. EAC is an argument. Know which you quote.

**Post body (1,759 characters):**

Budget at completion is a fact from the baseline. Estimate at completion is a claim about the future, and there are four defensible ones. Quote a forecast without naming the method and you have said nothing.

One set of numbers, four forecasts. BAC £12.0m, EV £5.2m, AC £6.1m, PV £6.4m. So CPI = 0.852 and SPI = 0.813.

1. EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) = 6.1 + 6.8 = £12.90m
Assumes the overrun so far was a one-off and the remaining work runs to budget. Defensible when you can name the event, price it, and show it has closed.

2. EAC = BAC / CPI = 12.0 / 0.852 = £14.08m
Assumes today's cost efficiency continues to the end. The default on most mature packages, because efficiency is usually a property of the setup, not of the month.

3. EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) / (CPI × SPI) = 6.1 + 6.8 / 0.693 = £15.91m
Assumes schedule pressure keeps costing money: acceleration, overtime, extended preliminaries. The pessimistic case, and the right one when recovery is being bought.

4. EAC = AC + a resourced bottom-up ETC
Assumes nothing from history. The most accurate when the remaining work genuinely differs from the work done, the most expensive to produce, and the one most exposed to the optimism the indices were protecting you against.

Spread: £12.90m to £15.91m. Just over three million pounds on a twelve million pound budget, and every figure is arithmetically correct.

Variance at completion, VAC = BAC − EAC, ranges from −£0.9m to −£3.9m depending only on which assumption you accept.

So the useful board paper is not a single number. It is the range, the method behind each end of it, and the specific thing that has to be true for the low end to hold.

Report method 2 as the base, method 1 or 3 as the case, and be ready to defend which event you priced.
