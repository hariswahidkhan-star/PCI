# A time impact analysis is not a delay claim. Here's why

*LinkedIn text post — 1553 characters including hashtags. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

A time impact analysis answers one question: what would this event have done, from where we actually stood?
It does not answer what happened. Those are different exercises, and submitting one as the other is how claims get lost.

The method, in order. Take the accepted programme updated to a data date immediately before the event. Build a fragnet that models the event and nothing else. Insert it. Re-run the critical path. The movement of the contract completion date is the entitlement.

Worked:

Contract completion: 14 March. Update at data date 30 September: completion 2 April. Eighteen days of slip already sit in the programme before anything is inserted, and that slip is not the employer's.

Fragnet for late release of the foundation design: 21 days. Total float on the affected path: 6 days.

Completion moves to 17 April. Entitlement: 15 days, not 21.

The six days nobody claims are the six the schedule was carrying. Who owns float is a contract question rather than a planning one, and it is worth settling before the first claim instead of during it.

Where the method fails, honestly. It is prospective. It runs off a forecast programme, so it is only ever as good as the update it starts from, and as-built records can contradict it later. After the fact a windows analysis usually persuades more, because it argues from what happened.

Concurrency does not disappear either. If eight days of contractor-culpable delay run inside the same window, the analysis has to say so.
