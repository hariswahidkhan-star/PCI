# Qualitative risk analysis: the full workshop run sheet

*LinkedIn document post — 12 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

Your matrix ranked a risk at 12 and another at 10.
In money the one scoring 10 was worth three times the one above it.

That is not a scoring error. It is what happens when you multiply rank numbers instead of quantities. Twelve slides on running the workshop properly.

---

**Slide 1 — What qualitative analysis is for**

Qualitative risk analysis ranks identified risks by probability and impact so a team can decide which ones deserve money, modelling or management attention. It is a triage step. It is not a forecast and it does not produce a contingency figure.

**Slide 2 — Do it before the quantitative work, not instead of it**

The workshop's job is to produce a short list worth modelling and a long list worth watching. If every risk goes into the simulation, the model takes a fortnight and the output is dominated by rows nobody validated.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic that breaks the matrix**
Two risks on a 5 × 5 matrix, with the bands the team agreed at the start:

**Risk A** — probability band 2 (5–10%, take 7.5%), impact band 5 (over £5m, take £12m)
Matrix score = 2 × 5 = **10**
Expected value = 0.075 × £12m = **£900k**

**Risk B** — probability band 4 (40–60%, take 50%), impact band 3 (£250k–£1m, take £600k)
Matrix score = 4 × 3 = **12**
Expected value = 0.50 × £600k = **£300k**

The matrix ranks **B above A**. The money ranks **A at three times B**.

The impact bands rise geometrically; the scores rise by one. Multiplying two rank positions is not multiplying two quantities, and the higher the impact bands climb, the worse the distortion gets.

**Slide 4 — The fix, and it takes ten minutes**

Publish the band midpoints on the wall before scoring starts, and carry a money column beside the score column. Rank on score to structure the discussion, then re-sort on the money column before anyone leaves the room.

Where the two orders disagree, that disagreement is the most useful output of the day.

**Slide 5 — Pre-work: the workshop starts a week early**

Circulate the current register, the programme with the critical path marked, the estimate basis and the top ten risks from the last comparable project. Ask each attendee for three risks in writing beforehand.

People who arrive having already written something contribute. People who arrive cold agree with whoever speaks first.

**Slide 6 — Who is in the room**

The disciplines that own the causes: engineering, construction, procurement, commercial, and the estimator who set the rates. Between eight and twelve people. Above about fourteen the quiet ones stop speaking and the register becomes one loud person's opinion.

**Slide 7 — The run sheet**

| Time | Block | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:15 | Scope, bands, what is out of scope | Shared definitions on the wall |
| 0:15–0:25 | Calibration on two known risks | Scoring drift removed |
| 0:25–1:10 | Identification, silent then round-robin | Raw list, no debate |
| 1:10–1:25 | Break | — |
| 1:25–2:25 | Scoring, cause and effect confirmed | Scored rows with owners |
| 2:25–2:45 | Money re-sort and challenge | Short list for quantification |
| 2:45–3:00 | Owners, actions, dates | Actions in the register, not in notes |

Three hours. Longer than that and scoring quality falls measurably as the room tires.

**Slide 8 — Calibrate before you score**

Take two risks everyone already understands and score them together out loud. You will find one person's "likely" is 40% and another's is 80%, and you find it in ten minutes rather than in the output.

Calibration is the cheapest quality step in the whole day.

**Slide 9 — Identify silently first**

Give the room six minutes to write on their own before anybody speaks. Anchoring is real: the first risk voiced shapes the next twenty, and silent generation gets you the risks that only one person can see.

Then go round the table one item each until the room is empty.

**Slide 10 — Score the risk, not the project**

Impact is scored on the assumption the event happens, not on the event's likelihood — that is what the probability column is for. Scoring both together is the most common error in the room and it double-counts likelihood, pushing rare high-consequence risks down where nobody looks.

**Slide 11 — The no-consensus rule**

When two people cannot agree a band, record both, record the reason, and move on. Do not average them. The disagreement is usually about a fact somebody can go and check, and that check is a better action than a compromise number.

**Slide 12 — What leaves the room**

A scored register with named owners, a short list for quantitative analysis, and a dated action against every row in the top quartile. Anything else is a workshop that generated a document.
