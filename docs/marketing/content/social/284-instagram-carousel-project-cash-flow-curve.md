# How to read a project cash flow curve, month by month

*Instagram and Facebook carousel — 9 slides, 1080 × 1350. Instagram captions carry no clickable link, so the link goes in the bio; on Facebook it goes in the post.*

**Caption (the first 125 characters have to earn the swipe):**

You earned £2.40m this month and you will bank £2.052m of it, 59 days from now. That is the whole problem.

A project cash flow curve is three curves that people draw as one. Nine slides: where the £348,000 goes, the cash conversion cycle worked out at 57 days, what that costs in working capital, and why fixing it by paying your supply chain later is not a fix.

Save it for the next funding conversation.

---

**Slide 1 — A cash curve is three curves**

Value earned is what you have done. Value certified is what the client agrees you have done. Cash received is what has arrived in the bank.

They have the same shape and different dates, and the distance between them is where contractors fail while reporting profit.

**Slide 2 — One month, in order**

Work done in the month, valued at **£2.40m**. Application submitted at month end. Certification 14 days later. Payment 45 days after certification.

The client certifies **£2.16m**, holding £240k pending records. Retention at 5% takes **£108k**. Net certified: **£2.052m**, landing **59 days** after the month end.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic**

Earned £2.40m → banked £2.052m. Gap **£348,000**, of which £240k is recoverable with better records and £108k is retention you have genuinely earned and cannot spend.

Now the cycle. Work-in-progress days (work done before it can be applied for) **30**. Days to collect once applied **59**. Days you take to pay the supply chain **32**.

Cash conversion cycle = 30 + 59 − 32 = **57 days**

At a spend run-rate of **£2.2m a month**, that is £72,368 a day.
Working capital tied up = 57 × £72,368 = **£4.13m**

You are lending the project £4.13m at all times, in a contract nobody wrote.

**Slide 4 — The S-curve and what a straight line means**

Cumulative spend curves are S-shaped because mobilisation is slow, the middle is dense, and commissioning tails off. That shape is a fact about how work is resourced, not a drawing convention.

A straight-line cash flow forecast means nobody profiled the work. It will understate the peak, understate the funding requirement, and look accurate at completion because the total is right.

**Slide 5 — The peak matters more than the total**

Funding is sized on the deepest point of the negative cash position, not on the contract value. Two contracts of the same value with different payment terms need different facilities.

Find the peak, add the worst realistic delay to one certification, and re-read it. A 30-day slip in one certification near the peak moves the funding requirement by roughly one month of run-rate — here, about £2.2m.

**Slide 6 — Retention, honestly**

Retention is typically 5%, half released at practical completion and half at the end of the defects period. On a £24m contract that is £1.2m held, £600k of it for a year or more after you finish.

It sits on your balance sheet as an asset and does nothing for your bank position. Forecast its release dates explicitly, because the two dates are usually the largest single cash events after handover.

**Slide 7 — Paying later is not a fix**

Push days-to-pay from 32 to 45 and the cycle falls to 44 days. Working capital drops to **£3.18m**, freeing **£941,000**.

That money did not appear. It came from subcontractors who now finance you for an extra 13 days, price it into their next tender, or fail. Do it deliberately, price the consequence, and never call it an efficiency.

**Slide 8 — Reading someone else's curve**

Three checks. Does the cash curve lag the value curve by the actual contractual terms, or by a round number somebody typed? Is retention shown as a separate release, or buried in the tail? Does the negative peak carry a funding line beside it?

If any of the three is missing, the curve is a picture of the programme, not a forecast of the bank account.

**Slide 9 — Cash is not revenue and neither is profit**

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, almost never on a certification lag. An engineer is examined on progress and float, almost never on a contract asset. This month produces three different numbers from one set of facts: £2.40m of work done, whatever revenue the measure of progress supports, and £2.052m of cash arriving in two months.

A project that manages one of the three and reports the other two by accident is the standard failure. The PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI) examines all three together, across 16 domains and 61 knowledge areas.
