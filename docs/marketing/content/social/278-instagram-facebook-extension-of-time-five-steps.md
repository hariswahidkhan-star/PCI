# Extension of time in five steps, with the arithmetic

*Instagram / Facebook carousel — 8 slides, 1080 × 1350. Caption below; the first 125 characters carry it before the fold.*

**Caption (first 125 characters before the fold):**

The event lasted 21 days. The award was 14. The other 7 belonged to float, and float is counted first.

Eight slides on running an extension of time: the five steps in order, the time impact analysis on slide 3, and the date that ends the claim before anyone reads it.

Full guide in the link in bio.

**Slide 1 — What an extension of time actually does**
An extension of time moves the contractual completion date. It relieves the contractor of damages for that period, and it keeps the programme a live instrument rather than a historic one. On its own it pays nothing. Money follows separately, under the cost provisions of the contract.

**Slide 2 — Step 1: notice, and the date that ends it**
Most standard forms require notice within a fixed period of the contractor becoming aware of the event, and in several of them that notice is a condition precedent. Diarise the date the day the event happens, not at month end. A well-argued claim served late is still late.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic**
Revised foundation design issued **7 May**.

Update the accepted programme to **6 May**, the day before the event, so it reflects actual progress and nothing else.
Insert a fragnet of **21 days** for the redesign and re-approval.
Total float on the affected activity: **7 days**.
Contract completion moves from **12 November** to **26 November** = **14 days**.

Prolongation, priced separately: 14 × £9,800 a day = **£137,200**.

**Slide 4 — Step 2: name the event and its cause**
"Delay to foundations" is not an event. "Revised foundation design issued 7 May under instruction 214, superseding drawing S-104 rev C" is. The claim stands on the instruction number, the drawing revision and the date, not on the narrative built around them.

**Slide 5 — Step 3: fix the programme you analyse from**
Choose the programme before you know the answer. The accepted baseline as updated to the last cut-off before the event is the defensible choice. Analysing from a programme built after the event, with the delay already showing in it, is the first thing the other side will attack.

**Slide 6 — Step 4: impact it and measure the movement**
Insert the fragnet, re-run the critical path, and read the movement of the contractual completion date. That movement is the entitlement. Time impact analysis is one accepted method of showing it; [how the main delay analysis methods differ](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/delay-analysis-techniques) decides which one holds up when it is challenged. The duration of the event is not the entitlement, which is why 21 days became 14.

**Slide 7 — Step 5: price the time separately**
Time-related cost is site establishment, supervision, plant on hire and insurances for the extended period. It is not the tender preliminaries rate reused because it was to hand. Price the actual period, evidence it from the ledger, and keep it apart from the entitlement argument.

**Slide 8 — Concurrency is decided by records**
Where two delays overlap, the outcome turns on what the contemporaneous records show and on what the contract says about competing causes. The records are made in the weeks before anyone has a dispute, by the person updating the programme. That is why this work belongs to project controls.
