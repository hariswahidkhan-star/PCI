# Progress measurement without the 90 per cent guess

*Instagram and Facebook carousel — 9 slides, 1080 × 1350. Instagram captions carry no clickable link, so the link goes in the bio; on Facebook it goes in the post.*

**Caption (the first 125 characters have to earn the swipe):**

The site said 85 per cent. The rules of credit said 76.6 per cent. That gap was worth £302,000 and nobody lied.

Progress measurement is the difference between a number you can stand behind at month-end and a number somebody felt. Nine slides: one foundations package, 120 units, every earning step shown, and the four rules that decide which method you are allowed to use.

Save it before the next progress meeting.

---

**Slide 1 — Progress is a measurement, not an opinion**

Physical per cent complete is the proportion of defined, verifiable work that has been finished at the cut-off. It is measured against steps agreed before the work started.

Anything else is a forecast of how the person filling in the form feels about the job.

**Slide 2 — Rules of credit, written first**

A rule of credit splits an activity into steps and fixes what each step is worth. Concrete foundations, 120 units, budget **£3.6m** at **£30,000** each:

Set out 5% · Excavate 20% · Blinding 5% · Rebar 25% · Formwork 15% · Concrete 20% · Strip and backfill 10%

They sum to 100%, and nobody may add a step later to explain a slow month.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic**

| Status at cut-off | Units | Credit | Equivalent units |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Complete | 62 | 100% | 62.00 |
| Concrete poured, not stripped | 18 | 90% | 16.20 |
| Rebar complete | 25 | 55% | 13.75 |
| Not started | 15 | 0% | 0.00 |
| **Total** | **120** | | **91.95** |

Per cent complete = 91.95 ÷ 120 = **76.6%**
Earned value = 91.95 × £30,000 = **£2,758,500**
Claimed at 85% = 0.85 × £3.6m = **£3,060,000**

Difference: **£301,500** of value claimed and not earned, on one package, in one month.

**Slide 4 — Which earning rule applies**

| Rule | Use it when | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Units complete | Repetitive, countable work | Units differ in effort |
| Weighted milestones | Long activities with verifiable steps | Steps are not independently checkable |
| 0/100 | Short work inside one period | Anything spanning two cut-offs |
| 50/50 | Two-period work, low value | Used to smooth a bad month |
| Percentage judgement | Nothing better exists | Always, eventually |
| Level of effort | Time-based support work | Applied to anything that produces a deliverable |

Pick the rule per work package, record it in the work package description, and do not change it mid-contract without a documented reason.

**Slide 5 — Level of effort is the one that hides trouble**

Level of effort earns value with the passage of time, so it always reports on schedule. Site management, project controls and quality assurance are genuine cases.

The danger is scale. Once level of effort passes roughly a tenth of the budget it starts propping up the whole project's schedule performance index, and the index stops meaning anything.

**Slide 6 — Every step must be checkable by a stranger**

"Rebar complete" works because someone who was not there can count bars against a drawing and sign it.

"Engineering 60% advanced" does not, because two competent people will give two answers and both will be arguable. If a step cannot be verified by a stranger with the drawings, it is not a step.

**Slide 7 — The 90 per cent problem**

The 18 poured-but-not-stripped units are at 90%, and they will sit at 90% for weeks if stripping and backfill get deprioritised.

That is the correct behaviour. A package that stalls at 90% is telling you exactly where the work stopped. A package that drifts 88, 91, 93, 94 by judgement is telling you nothing except that somebody is still typing.

**Slide 8 — When no rules were written**

Start with the packages carrying the most budget, not the most activities. Write the earning steps for the top ten by value, agree them with the people doing the work, and restate the current position on the new rules.

Expect the restated number to be lower. Report the movement as a change of measurement basis, not as a month of slippage, and say so in the narrative.

**Slide 9 — Why finance is watching this number**

Under an input method of measuring progress, cost incurred against total expected cost decides how much revenue may be recognised. A progress claim that overstates by £302k does not stay in the cost report — it overstates recognised revenue and creates a contract asset that later has to be unwound.
