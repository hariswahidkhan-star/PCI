# How to build a risk register people actually update

*LinkedIn document post — 12 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

Most risk registers stop being updated in month four.
Not because people are lazy — because the register stopped telling anyone anything they could act on.

Twelve slides on the fields, the arithmetic and the cadence that keep one alive.

---

**Slide 1 — A register is a decision log, not a list**

A risk register records uncertain events that would change cost, time or scope, with an owner, an action and a date against each one. If a row has no owner and no next action, it is a note, and notes do not get updated.

**Slide 2 — Why registers die**

They die when the output is a colour. A heat map that has been amber since March tells a project director nothing, so they stop asking, and once they stop asking the register stops being maintained. Money is the thing that keeps attention.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic**
Expected monetary value = probability × cost impact. Five live risks:

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Expected value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Late permit for the north bay | 30% | £1.20m | **£360k** |
| Ground conditions worse than survey | 15% | £2.00m | **£300k** |
| Rework on cable terminations | 60% | £250k | **£150k** |
| Single-source switchgear failure | 10% | £4.00m | **£400k** |
| Winter access restriction | 45% | £180k | **£81k** |
| | | **Total** | **£1.291m** |

The switchgear risk is the least likely on the register and the most expensive item on it. On a probability-first sort it was fifth. On money it is first.

**Slide 4 — Expected value is a ranking tool, not a contingency figure**

£1.291m is the average of many possible worlds, and this project runs once. It tells you where to spend management attention. It does not tell you how much to fund — that comes from a simulation that keeps the tail intact.

**Slide 5 — Write the risk as cause, event, effect**

"Ground conditions" is not a risk. "Because the site investigation covered only the south plot, ground conditions in the north bay may require additional piling, which would add cost and delay the substructure" is a risk, and it names three things you can act on.

The cause tells you what to mitigate. The effect tells you what to model. The event is the only part most registers record.

**Slide 6 — One named owner, and never a department**

"Engineering" cannot be chased. A named person can, and the act of putting a name in the box is itself a test — if nobody will accept the row, the risk is either not real or not understood.

The owner is the person who can act on the cause, not the person who suffers the effect.

**Slide 7 — Inherent and residual, kept apart**

Inherent is the exposure before your planned response. Residual is what remains after it, assuming the response actually happens. Recording only residual hides the value of the mitigation, and recording only inherent makes the register look untreated forever.

If residual equals inherent, the response is not a response.

**Slide 8 — The fields that earn their place**

| Field | Why it survives the cull |
|---|---|
| ID and raised date | Ages the risk; a row untouched for 90 days is a flag |
| Cause / event / effect | Makes the row actionable and modellable |
| Owner (a person) | Someone to ask |
| Probability and impact | Feeds expected value and the simulation |
| Response and response owner | Turns the row into work |
| Residual position | Shows whether the response is working |
| Trigger condition | The observable that says it is happening now |
| Closure criteria | Written when raised, not argued about later |

**Slide 9 — The trigger is the field that changes behaviour**

"Piling rig not mobilised by 14 March" is a trigger. It can be checked in ten seconds against the programme and it converts a risk into an early warning without a meeting.

Risks without triggers get discussed. Risks with triggers get caught.

**Slide 10 — Closure criteria, written on day one**

Decide when the row closes at the moment you raise it. "The permit is issued" closes cleanly. "The risk has reduced" never closes, and a register that only grows is a register people stop reading.

**Slide 11 — Cadence: two rhythms, not one**

A short fortnightly pass on live rows only — triggers, actions due, anything moved. A full quarterly re-scoring with the estimate and the programme open beside it, because probabilities move when the schedule moves.

Re-scoring everything every month produces the same numbers copied forward, which is how a register becomes fiction.

**Slide 12 — The register is the front end of the money**

Every quantified row here becomes an input to a cost simulation, and the contingency that simulation returns becomes a funded line that finance has to hold on the balance sheet. A register that is not maintained produces a funding request nobody can defend.
