# Actual cost is where finance and delivery stop agreeing

**Post body (1,751 characters):**

Delivery says the package has cost £6.1m. Finance says £5.4m. Both are reading their own ledger correctly, and the £0.7m between them is steel that arrived on the 27th and was invoiced on the 4th.

Actual cost (AC) is the cost incurred for the work included in earned value, over the same period, to the same cut-off. Incurred. Delivered steel that has not been invoiced is still cost.

Run the month both ways. Budget at completion £12.0m, earned value £5.2m.

Invoices only: CPI = 5.2 / 5.4 = 0.963
Incurred, accruals included: CPI = 5.2 / 6.1 = 0.852

Forecast on EAC = BAC / CPI:
12.0 / 0.963 = £12.46m
12.0 / 0.852 = £14.08m

Same month, same work, same team. £1.6m of difference, produced entirely by a cut-off convention nobody wrote down.

This is the overlap where projects lose money. A chartered accountant is examined on cut-off, accruals and when a cost may be recognised, and almost never on a critical path.

An engineer is examined on progress and float, and almost never on cut-off. The forecast needs both, and the person who owns it usually holds one half.

Three rules that end the argument for good.

Match the boundary. If EV is cut on the last calendar day of the month, AC is cut on the same day. Never EV to site date and AC to ledger date.

Accrue goods received and not invoiced, and subcontract work in place and not yet certified. If the work is in EV, its cost belongs in AC.

Book to the same control account. Cost that lands in an overhead pool while its scope sits in a control account produces a CPI that flatters the package and a corporate result nobody can reconcile.

Do those three and the monthly reconciliation between the cost report and the ledger becomes a five-minute check, not a two-day forensic exercise.
