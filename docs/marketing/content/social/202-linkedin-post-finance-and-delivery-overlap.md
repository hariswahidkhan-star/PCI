# One month. An accountant and a planner. Two answers.

*LinkedIn text post — 1596 characters including hashtags. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

The planner reported 62% complete. The accountant recognised revenue at 56.7%.
Both were right. £3.18m sat between them, and neither could explain the other's number.

Take a £60m fixed-price contract measured by cost-to-cost.

Costs incurred to date: £29.0m.
Total expected costs: £50.0m.
29.0 / 50.0 = 58%.

Now the adjustment most delivery teams have never been shown, and it comes from IFRS 15 rather than from the contract. Of that £29.0m, £1.5m is cable delivered to site and not installed. Cost incurred that is not proportionate to progress comes out of the measure, and the material is carried at cost with no margin until it goes in.

27.5 / 48.5 = 56.7%.
Revenue: 0.567 × 60 = £34.02m.
The planner's 62% on the same contract price implies £37.20m.
Difference: £3.18m.

Nobody is being difficult. The engineer is examined on float, quantities and progress measurement, and almost never on cut-off or a contract asset. The accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, and almost never on a critical path.

The project lives in the overlap. The overlap is where the money is lost, and it belongs to nobody's syllabus.
