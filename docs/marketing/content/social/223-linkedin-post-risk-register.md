# A risk register with 140 rows is a filing exercise

**Post body (1,817 characters):**

A risk register with 140 rows is not risk management. It is a filing exercise with a meeting attached.

A risk register is the controlled list of identified threats and opportunities, each carrying an owner, an assessment before response, a response with a date, and an assessment after it.

Here is why the row count is the problem. Price a real 140-row register and the total expected value comes to £4.2m. The top eight rows carry £2.98m of that. Seventy-one per cent.

The remaining 132 rows share £1.22m between them. That is £9.2k a row on a job where a single change order clears £50k without anyone waking up.

Give every row equal airtime in the monthly meeting and you spend 94% of the session on 29% of the exposure. The register did not fail. The reading of it did.

Pricing a row properly takes a minute. Row 12: probability 40%, cost impact between £0.8m and £2.4m, most likely £1.2m.

Triangular mean = (0.8 + 1.2 + 2.4) ÷ 3 = £1.467m
Expected value = 0.40 × 1.467 = £587k

Do that for all 140 and the sort order does the triage for you.

Six fields make a row usable. Anything else is decoration.

A cause, event and effect written as one sentence — "because the ground investigation stopped at 4m, we may hit rock below foundation level, which would add excavation cost and delay the substructure". Not "ground risk".

An owner who is a named person, not a department.

A probability as a number, not a colour.

An impact as a range, because a single point is a guess pretending to be data.

A response with a date and a cost, so it can be tracked like any other action.

A residual assessment after the response, because a response that does not move the numbers is not a response.

Rows that cannot carry those six fields are not risks. They are worries, and worries belong in a different document.
