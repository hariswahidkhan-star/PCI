# Parametric estimating and the day the driver breaks

**Post body (1,883 characters):**

Parametric estimating fits cost to something you can measure. Pounds per square metre, per MVA, per tonne of steel. It is fast, it is defensible, and it holds right up to the day the driver stops explaining the cost.

The method has four steps. Collect completed projects. Normalise them to a common date and location. Fit cost against the candidate driver and check how much of the variation it actually explains. Then apply the relationship to the new scope and quote a range, never a point.

Worked through. Say you hold 14 completed substation projects, normalised, giving a mean of £182,000 per MVA. A new 240 MVA scheme prices at 240 × £182,000 = £43.7m. Clean answer, one line of arithmetic, ready for the sanction paper.

Then look at the residuals. Two of the fourteen sit far above the fitted line, and both are urban sites that each carried roughly 400 metres of cable tunnel.

Tunnel cost tracks route length, not transformer capacity. Strip those two and the mean falls to £164,000 per MVA, so the same scheme prices at £39.4m, with route length entering as a second parameter priced separately.

£4.3m was sitting in a variable nobody had plotted. The relationship was not wrong. It was being asked to carry a cost driver it had never contained.

Three checks before a parametric number leaves the building.

Does the driver have a physical reason to drive the cost, or does it merely correlate on this sample? Correlation without a mechanism breaks the moment the sample changes.

Are the residuals patterned? A cluster above the line is a missing parameter, not noise.

Is the new job inside the range of the sample? Extrapolating a rate built on 50 to 150 MVA schemes onto a 240 MVA scheme is where the method quietly fails.

State the driver, the sample, the normalisation basis and the range. A parametric estimate without those four is a rate of unknown parentage.
