# Your P80 means nothing until someone owns the P50 gap

**Post body (1,867 characters):**

A board approves a project at P80. The project manager is measured against P50. The difference between the two is real money with no owner, and it is spent by month eight almost every time.

Quantitative risk analysis combines uncertainty in the base estimate with discrete risk events, simulates the combination many thousands of times, and produces a distribution of outcomes. Confidence levels are read off that distribution. P80 means 80% of simulated outcomes came in at or below that figure.

Take a worked case:

P50 £52.4m
P80 £58.9m
Gap £6.5m, which is 12.4% of the P50

The board sanctions £58.9m. The delivery target is £52.4m. Nobody writes down what the £6.5m is, who may release it, or what has to happen first.

Two things follow, and they are the same two things every time.

The gap becomes the budget. Not through dishonesty — through ordinary decisions taken with headroom in view. All of it is consumed whether or not a single modelled risk occurred.

Or nobody names it, so the first genuine risk event has to go back to the board as a re-forecast, because the project manager has no authority over money that was never assigned to anyone.

The fix is administrative, not technical. Hold the gap as management reserve one level above the project. Release it only against a named risk register row being retired. Report drawn, remaining and rate of drawdown every month.

One more thing about P80 that gets missed. The P80 cost and the P80 date are two separate readings from the same run, and committing to both is not an 80% commitment.

If they were independent it would be 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64. They are not independent, because schedule slip is one of the things that drives cost, so the true figure sits somewhere between 0.64 and 0.80.

You do not have to guess it. Read the joint outcome out of the same simulation. The runs are already there.
