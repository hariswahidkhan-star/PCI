# Extension of time: the evidence pack, slide by slide

*LinkedIn document post — 11 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

The steel arrived 34 days late, so the claim is for 34 days. That claim loses.
The activity had 22 days of total float, and float is the first thing the other side will count.

Eleven slides on what an extension of time claim has to carry before anyone reads the narrative.

---

**Slide 1 — An EOT claim is an evidence problem**
The drafting takes a day. The evidence takes the whole project. Claims fail on missing records and missing notices far more often than on the merits of the delay itself.

**Slide 2 — The clock runs before the analysis**
Most standard forms require notice within a fixed period of the claiming party becoming aware, and in some of them that notice is a condition precedent. Miss it and the analysis never gets read. Diarise the date on the day the event happens, not at the end of the month.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic** Pipe rack steel, delivery 34 calendar days late. Total float on the steel delivery activity at the last update before the event: **22 days**. Critical delay = 34 − 22 = **12 days**.

Contract completion moves from 30 November to 12 December. Prolongation, agreed at £9,400 a day: 12 × 9,400 = **£112,800**.

Claimed on the full 34 days it would read £319,600. The £206,800 difference is float you did not own.

**Slide 4 — Five documents carry the claim**
The accepted baseline programme. The last accepted update before the event. The contemporaneous records of the event. The as-built record. The fragnet showing what the event did to the network. Anything else is supporting material.

**Slide 5 — The baseline has to be the accepted one**
Not the tender programme, not the one on the wall, not the one reissued last week with the logic tidied. If the baseline was never accepted, say so and explain which programme you are measuring against and why. That sentence belongs at the front of the claim, not in a footnote.

**Slide 6 — Use the update immediately before the event**
Delay is measured against the position the project was actually in, not the position it was meant to be in. Take the update dated before the event, insert the fragnet, re-run, and read the movement in the completion date. That is time impact analysis, and it is prospective by design.

**Slide 7 — Cause and effect, activity by activity**
For every day claimed, name the event, the activity it hit, the logic path it travelled and the completion date it moved. A table of dates with a narrative wrapped round it is not cause and effect. If the path cannot be traced through the network, the day is not recoverable.

**Slide 8 — Concurrency: state your test, then hold it**
Two delays, different parties, overlapping in time and both on the critical path. Different jurisdictions and different forms treat that differently, and the answer usually changes the money more than the time. Declare which test you are applying at the start and apply it to your own delays as strictly as to theirs.

**Slide 9 — Float is owned by whoever the contract says owns it**
Some contracts say float belongs to the project and is available to whoever needs it first. Some say the contractor owns it. Many say nothing, which is the expensive case. Read the clause before you build the argument, because slide 3 is worth £206,800 and it turns entirely on this.

**Slide 10 — Time and money are two claims**
An extension of time relieves you from delay damages. It does not, by itself, pay you anything. Prolongation cost is a separate demonstration: the resources actually held on site during the extended period, evidenced by allocation sheets and invoices, not by a percentage of the tender preliminaries.

**Slide 11 — The pack, in order**
Notice with its date. Contract clause relied on. Baseline and the pre-event update. Fragnet and the re-run. Records: site diaries, delivery notes, correspondence, photographs with metadata. Concurrency position. Time claimed. Money claimed separately.
