# Class 5 to Class 1: what an estimate class promises

**Post body (1,864 characters):**

An estimate class describes how much of the project is defined. It says nothing about how hard the estimator worked, and no amount of effort moves the class on its own.

AACE's recommended practice 17R-97 sets out five classes, numbered 5 down to 1. In plain terms: Class 5 is a concept number built when almost nothing is drawn, and Class 1 sits near complete definition, produced to check a bid or to set a final control estimate. What moves you down the numbers is the maturity of the deliverables underneath, which then dictates the method and the credible accuracy.

Method follows maturity. Capacity factors and parametric models at the loose end, because there is nothing to measure. Take-offs priced against unit rates and quoted packages at the tight end, because there is.

Why it matters at sanction. Say the basis supports a range of ±30% on a £40m estimate. That is £28m to £52m, a spread of £24m.

Approve the point number and you have approved a range nobody in the room discussed. Take the definition far enough that the range is ±10% and the spread falls to £8m.

The gap between those two spreads is the value of front-end definition, and it is almost always cheaper than the overrun it prevents.

Two things get confused constantly. An accuracy range is a property of the estimate. Contingency is a funded amount inside the estimate. A range is not funding, and quoting a range instead of holding contingency is how projects arrive at financial close with no money for the risks they had already listed.

So put three things on the front page of every estimate: the class, the deliverables the class rests on, and the exclusions. If the class is not written down, the reader will assume Class 1 and commit to the number.

The estimator who states a class honestly at concept is more use than one who produces a confident number at the same stage.
