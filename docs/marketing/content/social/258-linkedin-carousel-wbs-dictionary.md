# The WBS dictionary: structure, example and the 100% rule

*LinkedIn document post — 11 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

The parent element was budgeted at £8.4m. Its four children summed to £8.1m.
Nobody noticed for five months, and the missing £300k arrived as a variation.

The 100% rule is arithmetic, not philosophy. Eleven slides on the dictionary that enforces it.

---

**Slide 1 — What a WBS dictionary is**

A WBS dictionary is the definition behind every element of the work breakdown structure: what the element covers, what it excludes, who owns it, what it is worth and how completion is judged. The WBS is the skeleton. The dictionary is what stops two people reading the same code differently.

**Slide 2 — Why the codes alone are not enough**

"1.3.2 Cable trenches" reads clearly until someone asks whether it includes backfill, ducting, or the trenching under the access road. Three people will answer differently, and each will price and progress it accordingly.

The dictionary entry answers that question once, in writing, before anyone bids it.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic of the 100% rule**
Parent **1.3 Substation civils**: budget **£8.40m**, **33,800 hours**.

| Element | Budget | Hours |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1.3.1 Foundations | £3.10m | 12,400 |
| 1.3.2 Cable trenches | £2.20m | 8,900 |
| 1.3.3 Control building | £1.90m | 7,600 |
| 1.3.4 Perimeter and access | £0.90m | 3,300 |
| **Sum of children** | **£8.10m** | **32,200** |
| **Parent** | **£8.40m** | **33,800** |
| **Gap** | **£0.30m** | **1,600** |

At a composite rate of £68 per hour, the 1,600-hour gap is worth **£108,800**.
The remaining **£191,200** of the budget gap is therefore material, plant or subcontract that has been budgeted at the parent and assigned to nobody.

The rule is a sum. If the children do not add to the parent, some scope has no owner, no schedule activity and no earned value.

**Slide 4 — The 100% rule, stated properly**

The children of any element must account for 100% of that element's scope, budget and hours — no more and no less — and the rule applies at every level, not only at the bottom.

More than 100% is double counting, which shows up as work being earned twice. Less than 100% is orphaned scope, which shows up as a variation. Both are found by adding a column.

**Slide 5 — The fields that make a dictionary work**

| Field | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| WBS code and title | Two teams using different codes for the same work |
| Scope statement | Arguments about what is included |
| **Exclusions** | The variation nobody saw coming |
| Deliverables | Progress claimed against nothing tangible |
| Acceptance criteria | A deliverable that is "nearly done" for two months |
| Control account and owner | Scope with no named accountable manager |
| Budget and hours | Breaks in the 100% rule |
| Rules of credit | Two people measuring progress differently |
| Assumptions | Forgotten basis, unarguable later |
| Interfaces | The gap between two packages |

**Slide 6 — Exclusions is the field that earns its keep**

Most dictionaries describe what is in. The costly ambiguity always sits in what is out. "Excludes temporary works design, excludes dewatering beyond 2m, excludes reinstatement of the haul road" is three variations that will not be argued about.

Write exclusions at the same moment you write scope, while the boundary is still fresh.

**Slide 7 — Acceptance criteria turn a deliverable into a date**

"Foundations complete" is not a criterion. "All 46 bases poured, cured, surveyed within tolerance and signed off by the resident engineer" is, and it can only be true or false.

An element whose completion is a matter of opinion will sit at 90% for a quarter.

**Slide 8 — Rules of credit belong in the dictionary, not in an argument**

The rule of credit is how physical progress converts to earned value for that element, and it is decided before the first measurement.

| Method | Suits | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| 0/100 | Short, discrete tasks | Lumpy monthly earned value |
| 50/50 | Two-period tasks | Half credit for starting |
| Units complete | Repetitive quantities | Units that are not equal effort |
| Weighted milestones | Long engineering packages | Milestone weights set optimistically |
| Level of effort | Supervision, project services | Earns on time, so hides real variance |

**Slide 9 — Every element maps to one control account**

A control account is where scope, budget, schedule and a named manager meet, and it is the lowest level at which cost and schedule performance are formally reported. Each dictionary entry names its control account and the manager who owns it.

If an element cannot be assigned to one control account, it has been decomposed along the wrong axis.

**Slide 10 — A worked entry**

**1.3.2 Cable trenches.** Scope: excavation, bedding, duct installation, backfill and compaction for HV and LV cable routes inside the substation fence, 1,240m total. Excludes: trenching outside the fence line, duct supply, cable pulling, reinstatement of the access road. Deliverable: trenches surveyed and signed as ready for cable.

Acceptance: as-built survey within ±25mm of the design invert, compaction test results submitted. Control account CA-1300, owner named. Budget £2.20m, 8,900 hours.

Rule of credit: units complete, by metres backfilled and tested. Assumption: no rock below 1.5m.

Eight lines. It removes about a dozen arguments.

**Slide 11 — Version it, or it decays**

A dictionary entry changes only through the change control that changed the scope, and the entry carries the change reference. An entry edited quietly is worse than no entry, because people trust it.

The dictionary is also what lets finance tie a cost line to a deliverable, which is the test for whether an item is capitalised or expensed and for what a contract asset actually represents.
