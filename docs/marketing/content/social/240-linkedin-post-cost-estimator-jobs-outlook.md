# Cost estimator jobs are shrinking. Openings are not.

**Post body (1,878 characters):**

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects the number of cost estimators to fall, and roughly 17,000 openings a year while it falls. Both statements are true. Almost everyone reads the first one and stops.

They are compatible because openings and growth are different quantities. Annual openings = growth, plus people leaving the workforce, plus people transferring to another occupation. In an occupation with an older age profile, the last two dwarf the first.

Illustrative decomposition. Take a stock of 200,000 estimators.

Projected decline of 1% a year: −2,000 posts
Exits from the workforce at 4%: +8,000
Transfers to other occupations at 5.5%: +11,000
Annual openings = −2,000 + 8,000 + 11,000 = 17,000

A shrinking occupation still hiring 17,000 people a year, and no contradiction in it. Check the current Occupational Outlook Handbook before quoting either number: BLS revises the projection annually, and the rates above are mine, there to show the arithmetic.

None of that means these jobs are safe as they stand. Software absorbs the mechanical part first: take-off, extraction from models, applying rates at first pass. If that is most of your day, the decline number is about you.

What survives is the part an estimate cannot measure. Which productivity assumption applies to this crew on this site. What steel does between award and order.

How much risk allowance the estimate carries, and on what basis. And whether you can name the class of estimate you produced, because a screening figure and a sanction figure are different products with different accuracy expectations, which is what AACE International's classification exists to keep straight.

Then the part nobody trains for. Your estimate becomes a contract price, then the total expected cost, and that figure drives reported progress and margin. Estimating is a finance discipline in a hard hat.
