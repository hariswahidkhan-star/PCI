# What is cost estimating? The three methods at a glance

*Instagram and Facebook carousel — 10 slides, 1080 × 1350. Instagram captions carry no clickable link, so the link goes in the bio; on Facebook it goes in the post.*

**Caption (the first 125 characters have to earn the swipe):**

A plant 75 per cent bigger does not cost 75 per cent more. Scale it properly and the gap is £6.3m.

Cost estimating is the discipline that stops a sanction paper being an act of faith. Ten slides: the three families of method, the scaling arithmetic on slide 3, what a basis of estimate has to say, and the four money words people use interchangeably and should not.

Save it for the next front-end review.

---

**Slide 1 — What cost estimating is**

Cost estimating is the process of predicting what defined scope will cost, at a stated price base, with a stated accuracy and a written list of what has been assumed and excluded.

Every part of that sentence is load-bearing. A number without a price base and an exclusions list is a rumour with decimal places.

**Slide 2 — Three families of method**

**Analogous** takes a completed project and adjusts it. **Parametric** builds a relationship between a cost driver and cost, then applies it. **Bottom-up** measures quantities and prices them line by line.

They are not a maturity ladder. They are what the available definition allows you to do, and most real estimates use all three in different places.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic: scaling an analogue**

A 20 MLD water treatment plant was built for **£18.0m**. You need 35 MLD.

Capacity factor: Cost B = Cost A × (Capacity B ÷ Capacity A)^n, with n around **0.6** for process plant.

Ratio = 35 ÷ 20 = **1.75**
1.75^0.6 = **1.399**
£18.0m × 1.399 = **£25.18m**

Scale it linearly instead and you get 18.0 × 1.75 = **£31.5m**. That is **£6.32m** of imaginary cost, before a single item has been priced.

Then finish the chain. Escalate five years at 5%: 1.05^5 = 1.276, so **£32.14m**. Apply a location factor of 1.10: **£35.35m**.

The analogue was the start of the estimate, not the answer.

**Slide 4 — Analogous: fast, and only as good as the analogue**

You need a completed project close enough in scope, technology and delivery model that the differences can be listed. If you cannot write the differences down, you do not have an analogue, you have a memory.

Adjust for capacity, price base, location, currency and delivery model in that order, and show each adjustment as its own line so a reviewer can argue with one without rejecting all of them.

**Slide 5 — Parametric: the driver must actually drive cost**

Cost per square metre, cost per tonne of steel, cost per kilometre of track. The relationship is only as good as the dataset behind it and the range it was fitted over.

Two rules keep it honest. Never apply a rate outside the range of the data it came from, and state the sample: "twelve UK distribution warehouses, 2019 to 2024, rebased to 2026" is an estimate. "Industry norm" is not.

**Slide 6 — Bottom-up: quantities times rates**

Take off the quantity, build the rate from labour, plant, materials, subcontract and the productivity norm, add site overhead and margin, and sum.

Bottom-up is the most accurate and the most expensive, and it fails in one specific way: it prices what is drawn. Everything not yet drawn quietly costs nothing, which is why a bottom-up estimate on immature engineering reads low and confident.

**Slide 7 — The class is a statement about definition**

An estimate class describes how much of the project is defined when the estimate is made. It is a property of the engineering, not of the estimator's effort, and no extra hours move the class while the deliverables stay immature.

AACE International publishes the classification framework and its accuracy ranges in its recommended practices. Read the ranges there rather than from anybody's slide, this one included.

**Slide 8 — The basis of estimate is the estimate**

Scope described. Price base and currency. Exchange rates and where they came from. Quantities and their source. Productivity assumptions. Delivery model. Exclusions, in a list a reader can check against the contract.

If the basis is missing, the reader assumes the number is firm and commits to it. That assumption is the single most expensive thing in the front end.

**Slide 9 — Four money words that are not synonyms**

| Term | What it covers | Who releases it |
|---|---|---|
| Base estimate | Defined scope at today's prices | Nobody — it is the arithmetic |
| Contingency | Quantified risk inside the scope | Project, against closed risks |
| Escalation | Price movement between price base and spend | Held to the spend profile |
| Management reserve | Scope change beyond the baseline | The sponsor, outside the baseline |

Merge any two of these and you lose the ability to say whether a project overran or was simply funded at the wrong price base.

**Slide 10 — Where the estimate lands in the accounts**

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, almost never on a capacity factor. An engineer is examined on quantities and productivity, almost never on cut-off or a contract asset. The estimate is where the two meet.

The sanction estimate becomes the authorised budget, the earned value baseline, and the denominator in every cost-to-cost measure of progress for the next five years. Get it wrong and every downstream number inherits it.
