# Build the cash flow spreadsheet once. Use it for years.

**Post body (1,871 characters):**

A construction cash flow spreadsheet has one job: produce the peak funding requirement, and the date it happens. Everything else in the file exists to get you to that one number.

Five tabs. Cost curve from the resourced estimate. Value curve from the schedule of values against progress. Cash in. Cash out. Net position.

The tabs that matter are the two nobody builds properly, because cash in and cash out are not the value and cost curves shifted by a month. They are shifted by contract clauses.

Worked month 9 on a £12m job:

Value applied £780k
Certification lag 14 days
Payment 30 days from certification
Retention 5% = £39k
So £741k arrives in month 11, not month 9

Cash out the same month: subcontractors £430k on 45-day terms, landing in month 11. Own labour and plant £190k, paid in month 9.

Cumulative at month 11: £6.42m in, £7.31m out. Net −£890k. That is the peak, and it is the number the finance director needs before sanction, not after.

Now the diagnostic the spreadsheet gives you almost for free. The cash conversion cycle on this job:

Days from work done to application: 25 (monthly cycle, plus the valuation cut-off)
Days from application to cash: 44 (14 to certify, 30 to pay)
Days you hold subcontractor money: 45
CCC = 25 + 44 − 45 = 24 days

At a £620k monthly cost run rate, £20,667 a day, 24 days is £496k of working capital tied up permanently in the job. Not a one-off. A standing balance you fund for the whole duration.

Negotiate the certification lag from 14 days to 7 and the cycle falls to 17 days. £351k. One clause, £145k of working capital released, and it costs nothing to ask for at tender stage.

Build it in a way that survives. Monthly columns, no hard-coded dates inside formulas, terms in a single assumptions block so a change of payment terms is one cell.

Do that once and the same file prices the next twenty jobs.
