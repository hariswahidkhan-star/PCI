# Planned value: quoted by everyone, derived by few

**Post body (1,682 characters):**

Planned value is not the budget. It is not what you have spent. It is what the baseline said would be finished by Thursday, priced in baseline money.

Most people can quote PV. Far fewer can derive it in front of a sceptical reviewer, which is the moment it matters.

Here is the derivation. Substructure package, budget at completion £12.0m, four work packages, and we are at the week 20 cut-off.

Excavation, budget £2.0m, baseline says 100% done → £2.0m
Piling, budget £3.0m, baseline says 60% done → £1.8m
Pile caps, budget £4.0m, baseline says 20% done → £0.8m
Ground beams, budget £3.0m, baseline says 0% → £0.0m

PV = 2.0 + 1.8 + 0.8 + 0.0 = £4.6m

Halfway through the calendar, the baseline planned to have earned 38% of the budget. That is the S-curve, and it is why "we are 50% through the programme so we should be 50% spent" is wrong on almost every job.

Two things people get wrong after that.

First, PV moves only through approved change control. If the schedule is re-statused and the baseline dates drift with it, SV = EV − PV quietly converges on zero and the variance disappears without anyone deciding anything.

Second, SPI = EV / PV is money, not time. As the job finishes, EV climbs to BAC and PV climbs to BAC, so SPI returns to 1.00 whether you delivered on the planned date or nine months late.

A schedule performance index of 0.98 in the last quarter tells you nothing. That is the reason earned schedule exists: it converts the same curves into time units and keeps reading after the point SPI goes blind.

Derive PV once, in writing, work package by work package. Then the argument in the review is about the work, not about where the number came from.
