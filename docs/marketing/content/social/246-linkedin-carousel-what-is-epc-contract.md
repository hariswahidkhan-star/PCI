# What is an EPC contract? A nine-slide guide for planners

*LinkedIn document post — 9 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

The scope estimates at $240m. The EPC price is $268m. Somebody will call the $28m a rip-off.
It is the price of a fixed date and a fixed number, and it holds only as long as the scope holds.

Nine slides on what an EPC contract is and what it changes for the person running the programme.

---

**Slide 1 — EPC in one line**
Engineer, procure, construct. One contractor takes the design, the supply chain and the construction under a single contract, usually for a fixed price and a fixed completion date, and hands over a facility that works.

**Slide 2 — What the owner is actually buying**
Single point of responsibility. When the pump does not perform, there is no argument between the designer, the supplier and the installer, because they are all the same contracting party. That is the product, and the price reflects it.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic** Facility, owner's estimate **$240m**. EPC lump-sum price: **$268m**. Fixed, subject to variations.

EPCM alternative: works at cost, expected $240m, plus a services fee of 9% = $21.6m → **$261.6m expected**. Now let the works run 12% over: 240 × 1.12 = $268.8m, plus the $21.6m fee = **$290.4m**, and the owner carries all of it. **The $28m premium buys the difference between those two rows — for the scope defined on the day of signature.**

**Slide 4 — The premium is not padding**
It is priced risk: quantity growth, productivity, escalation, subcontractor default, liquidated damages exposure. A contractor who prices none of that is either better informed than you or planning to make the money back through variations.

**Slide 5 — What changes for a planner**
The contractor owns the programme. The owner does not get to re-sequence the work, and asking for a level 4 schedule does not make it the owner's schedule. Owner-side control becomes verification: are the declared milestones real, and is the evidence behind them the same evidence every month?

**Slide 6 — Your own obligations are the biggest claim risk**
Permits, land, access, free-issue equipment, tie-in windows, operator availability for testing. On a lump-sum job these are the events that put the contractor on the front foot, because they are the only delays that are visibly yours. Programme them with dates and owners, and record them when they move.

**Slide 7 — Payment is lumpy, and it is not progress**
Milestone payment schedules pay for defined achievements, not for measured quantity. Cash flow steps rather than curving, and the contractor's declared percentage complete and the payment schedule will diverge. Report both, and never let the payment schedule become the progress measurement.

**Slide 8 — How the models compare**

| | EPC lump sum | EPCM | Design-bid-build |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who designs | Contractor | Owner's team, via consultants | Employer's designer |
| Price certainty at sanction | High, for fixed scope | Low | Medium |
| Owner control of sequence | Low | High | Medium |
| Cost of change | Highest | Lowest | Medium |
| Who carries overrun | Contractor | Owner | Split by cause |

**Slide 9 — When EPC fails** It fails when the scope was not settled at signature. Every owner change reopens the price, and a contractor who has priced tightly will recover margin through the change mechanism because that is now the only place margin exists.
