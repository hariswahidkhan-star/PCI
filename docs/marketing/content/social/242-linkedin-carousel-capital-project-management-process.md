# Capital projects: concept to handover in 12 slides

*LinkedIn document post — 12 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

A gate is not a document review. It is a question about how wrong your number is allowed to be.
Sanction a £180m scheme at concept and you are asking a board to approve something that could land £72m either side.

Twelve slides on the capital project process, from the first idea to the day operations take the keys.

---

**Slide 1 — What a capital project actually is**
A sequence of decisions to spend progressively larger sums, each taken with better information than the last. The process exists to stop the largest commitment being made on the weakest information.

**Slide 2 — The gates, and the question each one asks**

| Gate | The question | What is approved |
|---|---|---|
| Identify | Is the problem worth solving? | Money to study it |
| Select | Which option? | Money to define one option |
| Define | Do we know enough to commit? | The investment case |
| Execute | Is it still the same project? | Continuation |
| Handover | Does it work? | Acceptance |

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic of a gate**
Take a scheme estimated at £180m.
Concept estimate carried at ±40%: 180 × 0.60 = **£108m** and 180 × 1.40 = **£252m**. Range = **£144m**.
The same scheme after front-end engineering, carried at ±10%: 180 × 0.90 = **£162m** and 180 × 1.10 = **£198m**. Range = **£36m**.
Say the front-end work costs £5.4m. It closes 144 − 36 = **£108m** of range.
**That is what a gate buys.** Not the pack. Not the meeting.

**Slide 4 — Identify: the problem, not the solution**
The single most expensive error in capital delivery is arriving at gate one with a solution already chosen. Write down the need, the constraint and the date it bites. Everything downstream inherits this page.

**Slide 5 — Select: options, on a common basis**
Compare options on whole-life cost, not capital cost. An option that saves £14m to build and adds £2.1m a year to operate is more expensive after seven years. Say the discount rate you used and say who set it.

**Slide 6 — Define: freeze the scope, then estimate it**
Front-end engineering exists to make the scope specific enough to price. Out of it come the work breakdown structure, the execution plan, the risk register and an estimate with a written basis: what is included, what is excluded, what rates, what date, what currency.

**Slide 7 — The basis of estimate is the deliverable**
An estimate without its basis is a rumour with a decimal point. The basis says where each rate came from, what escalation was applied and what was excluded. It is also the only document that lets you tell a scope change from an estimating error two years later.

**Slide 8 — Sanction: what the board is really approving**
Three numbers, kept apart. The base estimate. The contingency, sized against the identified risks. The management reserve, held outside the performance baseline for scope nobody has thought of yet. Blend them and nobody can tell a drawdown from an overrun.

**Slide 9 — Execute: the baseline becomes the reference**
Time-phase the approved budget, set the rules of credit, then measure against it monthly. Every change goes through one register with a before and after value. A baseline that moves without a log is not a baseline.

**Slide 10 — Commission: three completions, three meanings**
Mechanical completion says it is built. Ready for start-up says it can be operated safely. Taking over says the client owns the risk. Write which one triggers which payment and which one starts the defects period, because on most jobs the argument at the end is about exactly this.

**Slide 11 — Handover and close: the boring slide that saves the year**
As-built drawings, operating manuals, spares, training records, the snag list with owners, the final account, retention release dates and the defects liability end date. Close the cost accounts and stop the charging. Projects rarely fail here; they just bleed for eighteen months.

**Slide 12 — What gates cannot do**
A gate cannot make a bad project good, and a gate held after the order is placed is theatre.
