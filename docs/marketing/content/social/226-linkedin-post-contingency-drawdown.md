# Contingency drawdown: the curve that tells the truth

**Post body (1,851 characters):**

Contingency does not fail at the end of a project. It fails at the halfway point, in a number nobody plotted.

Plot it and the warning arrives about ten months early. Here is the whole method.

At sanction: base estimate £48m, contingency £6.0m. Twelve and a half per cent.

Draw the planned drawdown curve at the same time. It tracks risk retirement, not spend, so it is not a straight line. A workable shape is 15% released by quarter progress, 45% by halfway, 75% by three quarters, all of it by handover.

Planned at 50% complete: £2.7m drawn, £3.3m remaining.

Actual at 50% complete: £4.1m drawn, £1.9m remaining.

Drawdown index = 4.1 ÷ 2.7 = 1.52

Apply it the way you would apply a cost performance index to the remaining work:

Forecast total need = 4.1 + (3.3 × 1.52) = £9.12m
Held: £6.0m
Shortfall: £3.12m

That number exists at month 10 of a 20-month job. Not at month 18, when the only options left are a claim and an apology.

What has to be in place from day one for the curve to mean anything.

A baselined drawdown curve, approved at sanction alongside the estimate. Without a plan there is no variance, only a falling balance that always looks reasonable.

A drawdown register with a change number per release, and a rule that every release names the risk register row it retires. A release that retires nothing is not a drawdown.

Monthly reporting of three figures only: drawn, remaining, index. Anything more and it stops being read.

The discipline that keeps it honest: contingency spent on scope growth is not contingency. It is a budget increase in fancy dress, and it must be trended separately or the index will quietly absorb it and tell you the project is fine.

The curve is worth the effort because it converts an argument about optimism into a ratio. Ratios get acted on. Opinions get scheduled for further discussion.
