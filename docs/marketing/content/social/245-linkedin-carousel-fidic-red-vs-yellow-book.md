# FIDIC Red vs Yellow Book 2017: who carries the design

*LinkedIn document post — 10 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

Same job, same 192 cubic metres of extra foundation concrete, same £190 a cubic metre.
Under one form the employer pays £36,480 without a claim being raised. Under the other the contractor absorbs it.

Ten slides on the difference between the FIDIC Red and Yellow Books, written for the people who have to price and programme them.

---

**Slide 1 — One difference sits under all the others**
Who owns the design. Everything else — how the work is priced, how payment is measured, what completion means, which risks travel where — follows from that single answer.

**Slide 2 — The two forms in a sentence each**
The Red Book is for works designed by or on behalf of the employer, with the contractor building to that design and the work generally measured. The Yellow Book is for plant and design-build, where the contractor designs to the employer's stated requirements for a lump sum.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic** Foundation concrete. Bill quantity 620 m³ at £190/m³ = **£117,800**. Measured on completion: 812 m³ at £190/m³ = **£154,280**.

Difference: 192 m³, **£36,480**. Red Book: the works are remeasured, so the £36,480 flows to the employer as a matter of course. Yellow Book: that quantity sits inside the contractor's lump sum unless it follows a change to the employer's requirements or a physical condition of the kind the contract puts on the employer.

**Same concrete. Different party. No new event.**

**Slide 4 — What changes between the two**

| | Red Book | Yellow Book |
|---|---|---|
| Design | Employer's | Contractor's, to stated requirements |
| Price | Generally remeasured against a bill | Lump sum |
| Quantity risk | Employer | Contractor |
| Design standard | Build to the drawings | Typically fitness for the stated purpose |
| Payment | Measured progress | Schedule of payments or milestones |
| Completion | Works complete | Complete and tested against performance |

**Slide 5 — The Employer's Requirements are the risk document**
On a Yellow Book job the requirements document decides who pays for almost every argument that follows. A vague performance statement is a contractor's problem when it is tight and an employer's problem when it is loose. Read it as a risk register, because that is what it is.

**Slide 6 — Two design standards, and they are not the same**
Designing with the reasonable skill and care of a competent professional means you are judged on the process. Designing to a fitness for purpose obligation means you are judged on the result. Professional indemnity cover often responds well to the first and poorly to the second, which is a commercial question long before it is a legal one.

**Slide 7 — What it does to your cost report**
Remeasured work gives you a monthly quantity to earn against, so earned value falls out of the measurement. Lump sum milestone payment does not. On a Yellow Book job you need rules of credit written before the first claim, or your earned value and your payment schedule will describe two different projects.

**Slide 8 — Testing is where the Yellow Book bites**
The plant has to perform, and there are usually tests before takeover and tests afterwards against guaranteed figures, with damages attached to missing them. Programme the test packs, the utilities and the operator availability with the same care as the construction sequence, because that is the part that slips.

**Slide 9 — Claims run the same way in both 2017 forms**
The 2017 editions put both parties through one claims procedure, with the same notice period from awareness and the same route to determination. Whichever book you are on, the claims discipline is identical and it is the calendar, not the argument, that decides most of them.

**Slide 10 — What to do differently on Monday** On Red, protect the measure: agree the method of measurement, keep the quantity records, and reconcile the bill monthly. On Yellow, protect the requirements: log every interpretation, freeze the design deliverables list, and programme the tests.
