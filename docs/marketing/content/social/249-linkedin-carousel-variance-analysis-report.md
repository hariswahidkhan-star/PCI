# Variance analysis: the report that survives a board

*LinkedIn document post — 11 slides, 1080 × 1350. No link in the body; the link goes in the first comment.*

**Post caption (the first two lines carry the post):**

"Cable pulling is £15,800 over due to productivity." That sentence has cost people their credibility in board meetings.
It describes the past, names no rate, and says nothing about the £11,530 still coming.

Eleven slides on the structure that makes a variance analysis report worth reading.

---

**Slide 1 — Why most variance reports die in the room**
They explain what happened. A board is deciding what to do, so a report that stops at explanation invites the question you cannot answer: what does this mean for the final number?

**Slide 2 — The rule**
A variance analysis report is a forecast document that happens to start with history. Every variance you record has to end at a changed estimate at completion, or an explicit statement that the estimate has not changed and why.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic**
Cable pulling. Budget 3,200 m at £84.00/m = **£268,800**.
At cut-off: 1,850 m installed → EV = 1,850 × 84 = **£155,400**. AC = **£171,200**.
CV = 155,400 − 171,200 = **−£15,800**.
Actual rate = 171,200 ÷ 1,850 = **£92.54/m**.
Rate variance = (92.54 − 84.00) × 1,850 = **£15,800**. Quantity variance: **nil**.
Remaining 1,350 m at £92.54 = £124,930 against a budget of £113,400 → **another £11,530**.
EAC = 171,200 + 124,930 = **£296,130**. VAC = **−£27,330**.

**Slide 4 — Split rate from quantity, every time**
Same total variance, opposite meaning. A quantity variance says the scope was wrong and the estimate needs re-basing. A rate variance says the price of every remaining unit is wrong and it will keep arriving. Report the split or the reader cannot choose an action.

**Slide 5 — The six columns**

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Variance | £ and % against budget, this period and cumulative |
| Cause | Rate, quantity, scope or timing — one of four, not a paragraph |
| Effect on EAC | The number, with the method used |
| Action | What changes, physically |
| Owner and date | A person and a date, never a function |
| Precedent | Has this action worked on this project before? |

**Slide 6 — Report by exception, and write the rule down**
Set the threshold in the procedure before the first report: for example, variances greater than ±5% or ±£100,000, whichever is larger, at control account level. Without a threshold, every account is reported and nothing is read. With one, an omission is itself information.

**Slide 7 — Narrative rules**
Name the control account. Give the rate. Say what the remaining quantity is. No adjectives, no "being monitored closely", no "due to productivity" without the two numbers that show it. Four sentences beats four hundred words, and it survives being quoted back at you.

**Slide 8 — Cross-check the forecast two ways**
CPI here is 155,400 ÷ 171,200 = 0.9077, so BAC ÷ CPI = 268,800 ÷ 0.9077 = **£296,130**. The bottom-up build gave the same figure. When two independent routes agree, say so. When they disagree, the difference is the interesting number and it belongs in the report.

**Slide 9 — Schedule variance belongs, but not in pounds**
PV at cut-off was £168,000, so SV = 155,400 − 168,000 = −£12,600 and SPI = 0.925. Both are true and neither means much on their own, because SPI returns to 1.00 at completion however late you finish. Convert to physical terms: 150 m short, at 50 m a week, is three weeks on this account.

**Slide 10 — What the board is actually asking**
Is the final number moving. Is it moving because of something you can act on. And has the action you are proposing ever worked here. Answer those in the first paragraph and the rest of the pack becomes supporting evidence rather than a defence.

**Slide 11 — The running order**
Headline movement in EAC. Variances above threshold, worst first. Cause split by rate and quantity. Forecast effect. Actions with owners and dates. Changes to the baseline log. Then the detail.
