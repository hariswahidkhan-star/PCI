# When bottom-up ETC beats the formula, with the maths

*X / Threads thread — 6 posts, each under 280 characters and each able to stand alone. The link sits in the final post. Character counts are for production; X counts any URL as 23 characters, so the live figures run lower.*

**Post 1/6 — the hook** (234 characters)
The formula says the job finishes at £60.5m. The bottom-up estimate says £56.1m.

Same data, same cut-off, £4.4m apart. The gap is not a spreadsheet error. It is a disagreement about whether what went wrong is going to keep happening.

**Post 2/6 — what bottom-up ETC is** (222 characters)
A bottom-up estimate to complete prices the remaining scope again, package by package, from current rates and quantities. The index method extrapolates from performance to date instead. One re-estimates. The other assumes.

**Post 3/6 — the arithmetic** (185 characters)
Month 10. BAC £54.0m.
EV £21.6m, AC £24.2m.
CPI = 21.6 ÷ 24.2 = 0.893.
Formula EAC = 54.0 ÷ 0.893 = £60.5m.
Bottom-up ETC of the remaining scope = £31.9m, so EAC = 24.2 + 31.9 = £56.1m.

**Post 4/6 — the four tests** (254 characters)
Use bottom-up when the remaining work is unlike the work done, when the overrun has a discrete cause that is now closed, when the indices were built on mobilisation months, or when scope has moved. Otherwise the index is cheaper and harder to argue with.

**Post 5/6 — the case, and the weakness** (262 characters)
Here £6.2m of the overrun was a temporary works redesign that cannot repeat, and 62% of remaining hours are commissioning at different rates. That is the case for re-estimating. The weakness is that the people writing the ETC are the people being measured by it.

**Post 6/6 — how to keep it honest** (243 characters)
Bridge each month's ETC to last month's, line by line, and keep the formula EAC beside it as the challenge number. Divergence is a conversation, not an error.
