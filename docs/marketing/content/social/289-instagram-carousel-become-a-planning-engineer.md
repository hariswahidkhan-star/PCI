# How to become a planning engineer: the honest route

*Instagram and Facebook carousel — 10 slides, 1080 × 1350. Instagram captions carry no clickable link, so the link goes in the bio; on Facebook it goes in the post.*

**Caption (the first 125 characters have to earn the swipe):**

"You are 12 days late. Here are three activities you can crash. Buy the days back for the least money."

That is the question that separates people who can drive the software from people who can plan. Ten slides on how to become a planning engineer: the route in, the arithmetic you will be tested on, what "knowing P6" actually means to an employer, and the half of the job most planners never learn.

Save it if you are trying to get in, or trying to get someone in.

---

**Slide 1 — What a planning engineer actually does**

A planning engineer builds and maintains the model of how a project will be delivered in time, updates it against measured progress, and explains what the difference means to the people paying for it.

Three quarters of the job is the last part. The model is the easy half.

**Slide 2 — There is no single route in**

Most planners arrive from a construction or engineering degree, from a site role such as engineer or supervisor, or sideways from quantity surveying or document control.

What all three routes share is time on a live project. Employers hire people who can tell whether a duration is plausible, and that judgement comes from having watched the work, not from a course.

**Slide 3 — The arithmetic they will test you on**

**Question one.** An activity has ES 42, EF 57, LS 49, LF 64. Total float and is it critical?

Total float = LS − ES = 49 − 42 = **7 days**
Check: LF − EF = 64 − 57 = **7 days**
Duration = EF − ES = **15 days**. Seven days of float, so **not critical**.

**Question two.** You are 12 days late. Three critical activities can be crashed:

| Activity | Days available | Cost | Cost per day |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| X | 4 | £18,000 | £4,500 |
| Y | 6 | £9,000 | £1,500 |
| Z | 5 | £30,000 | £6,000 |

Buy cheapest first. Y all 6 days = £9,000. X all 4 days = £18,000. That is 10 days for **£27,000**. The last 2 days come from Z at £6,000 = **£12,000**.

**12 days for £39,000**, averaging £3,250 a day. Buying Z first would have cost £30,000 for 5 days.

**Slide 4 — The answer most people miss**

After buying 10 days, check whether a parallel path has become critical. If the next path had only 8 days of float, you can take 8 before it starts driving the finish, and days 9 to 12 have to be bought on two paths at once.

Say that out loud in the interview. It is the difference between a candidate who knows a formula and one who has actually recovered a programme.

**Slide 5 — The technical base**

Critical path method, forward and backward pass, total and free float. Progress measurement and rules of credit. Earned value: planned value, earned value, actual cost, and the four ways of forecasting a final cost. Delay analysis, at least well enough to know what records a claim needs.

Everything else is layered on those. Someone who has them can learn any tool. The reverse is not true.

**Slide 6 — What "knowing P6" means to an employer**

Not that you can build a bar chart. That you can set a calendar correctly, apply a data date, read a total float column and know why it lies when a constraint is set, run a schedule check on someone else's file, and explain why the longest path and the critical flag disagree.

Learn the settings that change the answer. There are about a dozen, and they are what separates a schedule from a picture.

**Slide 7 — The first role: what to ask for**

Take the role that puts you on a live update cycle, even if the title is planning assistant or scheduler. A monthly cut-off, a real progress meeting and a report someone challenges will teach you more in six months than two years of building baselines nobody uses.

Ask in the interview who reads the schedule and what happens when it says something unwelcome. The answer tells you whether the job is planning or formatting.

**Slide 8 — What gets you promoted**

Not tool depth. The narrative. A senior planner is someone who can look at a network, say which three things are driving the finish and what each would cost to fix, and be right often enough that people stop arguing.

Write the narrative every month even when nobody asked for one. It is the fastest way to build the judgement the next role is buying.

**Slide 9 — The half most planners never learn**

Your programme decides when milestones are certified, which decides when revenue may be recognised and when cash arrives. A slip that crosses a period end is a finance event before it is a schedule event.

Planners who understand that side stop being the person who reports the delay and become the person in the room where it gets decided. It is also the fastest differentiator available, because almost nobody does both.

**Slide 10 — A twelve-month plan**

Months 1–3: master float, the forward and backward pass, and your tool's settings that change the answer. Months 4–6: own a monthly cycle end to end and write the narrative.

Months 7–9: learn earned value properly and forecast a package. Months 10–12: sit on the finance side of a month-end and follow one milestone all the way to the ledger.
