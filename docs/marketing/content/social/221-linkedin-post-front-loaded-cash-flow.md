# Front-loaded cash flow is a signal, not a preference

**Post body (1,833 characters):**

Front-loaded cash flow is not a scheduling preference. It is a signal, and the arithmetic tells you exactly what it is signalling.

A £10m contract over 20 months. Preliminaries and mobilisation are priced in the schedule of values at £1.8m. They cost £0.9m to deliver.

By month 4:

Certified value £2.9m
Cost incurred £1.6m
Retention at 5% holds £145k, so £2.755m has been received
Cash position: £1.155m in hand

That looks like performance. Measure it against physical progress and it is not.

Planned value at month 4 was £2.6m. Earned value, measured on physical progress against the baseline, is £2.05m.

SPI read off the certificates: 2.9 ÷ 2.6 = 1.12. Ahead.
SPI read off earned value: 2.05 ÷ 2.6 = 0.79. Schedule variance −£550k.

The £850k gap between what has been certified and what has been earned is the whole story. It is money billed ahead of work performed.

On the finance side that gap has a name and a home. Billing in excess of the performance actually satisfied is a contract liability, not revenue. It sits on the balance sheet and it unwinds in the back half of the job, when the preliminaries are still burning and the retention is still held.

So front-loading is not fraud and it is not clever. It is a borrowing arrangement with your own project, and the interest is paid at the end, in the months where there is no positive cash left to draw on.

Two questions worth asking the moment you see the curve.

Which items carry the loading, and what do they cost to deliver? The difference between the two is the size of the loan.

Does the earned value system measure physical progress, or does it read the payment certificate? If it reads the certificate, your schedule performance index is measuring the commercial team rather than the site, and it will keep saying 1.12 right up to the month it says 0.6.
