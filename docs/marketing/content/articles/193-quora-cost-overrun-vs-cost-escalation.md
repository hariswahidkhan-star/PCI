# Cost overrun vs cost escalation: what is the difference?

A cost overrun is an outcome: final cost exceeds the approved baseline for the same scope. Cost escalation is a cause: the price of the same inputs rises over time, through labour rates, material prices and market conditions. The cost overrun vs cost escalation confusion matters because escalation can be budgeted in advance, and budgeted escalation is not an overrun.

Escalation without an overrun happens whenever the allowance was adequate. An overrun without escalation happens whenever the work simply took more hours.

## What exactly is a cost overrun measured against?

An overrun is meaningless without a named baseline. The same project can be over one baseline and under another on the same day, and both statements can be true.

The three baselines that get quoted are the estimate at sanction, the approved cost budget, and the sanctioned total including contingency. Say which one you mean before you quote a percentage.

An overrun is also a point-in-time statement about a forecast, not only a final fact. A project forecasting above its budget in month nine is in overrun in month nine, whatever happens later.

## What exactly is cost escalation?

Escalation is the change in the price level of an unchanged basket of inputs between the date the estimate was priced and the date the money is spent. It is a time effect, not a scope or productivity effect.

Every estimate carries a price base date, and escalation is the bridge from that date to the spend curve. If your estimate has no stated base date, it has no defensible escalation position.

Published construction cost and output price indices exist in most markets and are used to set and later test the allowance. Naming which index a contract or an estimate follows is part of the estimating basis, and it belongs in writing rather than in someone's memory.

## Cost overrun vs cost escalation, side by side

| Axis | Cost overrun | Cost escalation | Scope growth | Contingency drawdown |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Outcome | Cause | Cause | Funding action |
| Measured against | A named baseline | The price base date | The approved scope | The contingency pot |
| Driven by | Everything below combined | Market prices over time | Requirements, design, site | Risks that have materialised |
| Budgeted in advance? | No — it is the failure to stay within budget | Yes, as an escalation allowance | Only as a design development allowance | Yes, by definition |
| Typically owned by | The project sponsor | The estimator, then the market | The design authority | The sponsor or steering group |
| First visible in | The forecast, not the actuals | Rate agreements and quotations | The change log | The risk register |
| Recoverable? | Not as such | Sometimes, via a price adjustment clause | Sometimes, via variations | It is your own money |

The fourth row is the one to read twice. Escalation is a forecastable cost that a competent estimate carries an allowance for, so escalation only becomes part of an overrun to the extent that it exceeds that allowance.

## What does the split look like in numbers?

Take an onshore substation and cabling package. The estimate is priced at a first-quarter base date and comes to £10.00m before escalation.

The approved budget adds a 4.5% escalation allowance over the spend curve, so £0.45m, giving a cost budget of £10.45m. A separate risk contingency of £0.80m is held by the sponsor, giving a sanctioned total of £11.25m.

The job finishes at £11.60m.

| Element | Amount | Explanation |
|---|---:|---|
| Base estimate at the price base date | £10.00m | Same scope, same rates |
| Escalation allowance in the budget | £0.45m | 4.5% over the spend curve |
| **Approved cost budget** | **£10.45m** | The baseline the overrun is measured against |
| Escalation actually experienced | £0.62m | 6.2% against 4.5% assumed |
| Escalation variance | £0.17m | 0.62 − 0.45 |
| Quantity and route growth | £0.55m | 850 m of additional cable route |
| Productivity against estimate | £0.43m | Hours above the estimating norms |
| **Outturn cost** | **£11.60m** | 10.45 + 0.17 + 0.55 + 0.43 |

Check the arithmetic: 0.17 + 0.55 + 0.43 = £1.15m, and 10.45 + 1.15 = £11.60m. The overrun against the cost budget is £1.15m, or 11.0% of £10.45m.

Now the sentence that most reports get wrong. Escalation *cost* the project £0.62m, which is 5.3% of the outturn. Escalation *contributed* £0.17m to the overrun, which is 14.8% of it. Quoting the first number as the cause of the overrun overstates escalation by more than three times.

And the baseline question bites here too. Against the sanctioned £11.25m, the same project is £0.35m over, not £1.15m. Both figures are correct; only one of them will be in the headline, and it is worth deciding which before someone else does.

## Which of the two can you actually control?

Escalation is largely outside a project team's control, but the exposure to it is not. Fixing rates early, placing material orders against a firm price, and agreeing a price adjustment mechanism are all decisions taken by people on the project.

Productivity and quantity growth are inside the team's control, and in the example above they account for £0.98m of the £1.15m. That is the number a monthly review should be spending its time on, and the trending and commitment disciplines that [catch an overrun before the outturn](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/cost-control-in-construction) are what make it visible in month four rather than month fourteen.

The practical test: if the same work, at the same rates as the day it was priced, would still have cost more than budget, escalation is not your problem. Something else is.

## Where does this land in the accounts?

On a fixed-price contract, escalation above the allowance sits with the contractor and reduces margin directly. On a contract with an indexation or price adjustment clause, part of it moves to the client, and the recovery is variable consideration rather than a certainty.

The forecast effect is immediate. Where progress is measured by a cost-based input method, the measure is costs incurred divided by total expected costs, so raising the forecast raises the denominator and lowers the percentage complete.

Take a contract price of £11.00m with £6.00m of cost incurred. At a forecast of £10.45m the measure is 6.00 ÷ 10.45 = 57.4%, and cumulative revenue is 57.4% × 11.00 = **£6.32m**. At a forecast of £11.60m it is 6.00 ÷ 11.60 = 51.7%, and revenue is **£5.69m**. The same delivery, £0.63m less revenue, purely from the forecast.

There is a second consequence. At an outturn of £11.60m against a price of £11.00m, the contract is expected to lose £0.60m, and an expected loss on a contract is generally recognised in full in the period it becomes apparent rather than spread over the remaining programme. Nothing here is accounting advice, but the operational point stands: the month your forecast crosses the contract price is the month finance needs to hear about it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is inflation the same as escalation?**
General inflation is an economy-wide price change; escalation is what happens to your specific basket of labour, plant and materials. They diverge sharply, and construction inputs regularly move very differently from a headline consumer index. Estimate escalation from construction-specific indices and from your own supply chain quotations, not from a national inflation figure.

**Should escalation sit in the base estimate or in contingency?**
In the base estimate, as a separate identified allowance. Escalation is an expected cost, and contingency is for uncertain events. Blending them hides whether an overrun was funded by a drawdown against a named risk or was simply unfunded, which is the question a sponsor is actually asking.

**How do you calculate an escalation allowance?**
Apply forecast rate movements to the spend curve rather than to the total, because money spent in month three escalates less than money spent in month thirty. Split the estimate into labour, materials and plant, apply the relevant index movement to each, and state the base date and the source for every rate you use.

**Can a project have escalation but no overrun?**
Yes, and it is common on well-estimated work. If escalation runs at 4.0% against a 4.5% allowance, escalation has cost real money and has still returned a favourable variance. That is exactly what the allowance was for, and it is worth saying out loud when the market is blamed for an overrun that productivity caused.

**Who owns escalation risk in a contract?**
Whoever the contract says owns it. Firm-price contracts push it to the contractor; contracts with a price adjustment or fluctuation clause share it against a named index. Read the clause before the tender is priced, because the pricing strategy for a firm-price two-year job and an indexed two-year job should not be the same.

**How do you report the two separately each month?**
Bridge from budget to forecast with named components: escalation variance, quantity growth, productivity, rate changes and risk drawdown, adding to the total movement. A bridge with a residual line called "other" larger than any named line means the cost analysis has not been done yet.
