# IFRS 15 for construction contracts: measuring progress

IFRS 15 for construction has no method called percentage of completion. It asks whether control passes over time, and if it does, the entity chooses a measure of progress, usually cost-to-cost. The arithmetic resembles the old method; the entry conditions and the unit of account do not.

That distinction is not pedantry. It changes which contracts qualify, what counts as one contract, and when a variation becomes revenue.

## Does percentage of completion still exist?

The phrase survives in practice and in most cost systems. As an accounting method it does not: IFRS 15 replaced the separate construction-contracts approach with one revenue model applied to every industry.

What replaced it is a two-stage test. First, does this performance obligation transfer control over time rather than at a point in time? Second, if it does, what measure of progress faithfully depicts the transfer?

Where a contractor answers the first question with yes and the second with cost-to-cost, the resulting numbers look like the old percentage-of-completion figures. The difference shows up at the edges: a contract that fails the over-time test now recognises nothing until handover, however much work has been done.

## When does IFRS 15 for construction allow revenue over time?

Described in the Institute's own words, an obligation transfers over time when any one of three conditions holds.

| Condition | Plain reading | Typical construction case |
|---|---|---|
| The customer receives and consumes the benefit as the work is performed | Another contractor would not have to redo what is done | Routine maintenance, facilities services, term contracts |
| The customer controls the asset as it is created or enhanced | You are building on their land or improving their asset | Most on-site civils, fit-out, refurbishment |
| The asset has no alternative use and there is an enforceable right to payment for work completed to date | It cannot be sold to anyone else, and you can bill for progress if terminated | Bespoke plant, custom EPC scope, one-off structures |

Most construction contracts satisfy the second condition, and heavy engineering usually relies on the third. The third is the one that fails on a technicality, because an enforceable right to payment means payment for work performed plus a reasonable margin, not merely recovery of cost, and the contract has to say so.

Read the termination clause before assuming over-time recognition. On some contracts a termination-for-convenience clause paying only cost incurred is enough to push the whole scope to point-in-time recognition.

## How many performance obligations does a construction contract have?

Usually one. A promise is separately accounted for only when the customer can benefit from it on its own and it is distinct within the context of the contract.

On an integrated build the contractor is providing a significant service of combining inputs into a single output. Piling, frame, cladding and services are not distinct promises in that context; the promise is the building.

Separate obligations do appear. A design package sold and usable on its own, a 24-month operations and maintenance tail, or a standalone equipment supply that the customer could have bought elsewhere each stand on their own feet. Where they exist, the transaction price is split between them by relative standalone selling price, not by the contract's schedule of rates.

The reverse also happens. Two or more contracts signed at or near the same time with the same customer are combined and treated as one where they are negotiated as a package, or where the consideration on one depends on the other, or where the goods and services promised are a single obligation.

## Cost-to-cost, worked, including the materials adjustment

A fixed-price contract of **£120.0m** with a forecast cost of **£96.0m**, so an expected margin of **£24.0m**. Costs incurred to date are **£48.0m**, of which **£9.0m** is switchgear delivered to site and not installed.

**The naive calculation.** Progress = 48.0 / 96.0 = **50.00%**. Revenue = 0.50 × 120.0 = **£60.00m**. Cost £48.0m. Margin **£12.00m**.

That answer takes margin on a delivery note. Where a cost incurred is not proportionate to progress, it is stripped out of the measure and the material is recognised at cost with no margin until it is installed.

**The correct calculation.** Adjusted cost incurred = 48.0 − 9.0 = £39.0m. Adjusted forecast cost = 96.0 − 9.0 = £87.0m. Progress = 39.0 / 87.0 = **44.83%**.

Adjusted transaction price = 120.0 − 9.0 = £111.0m. Revenue from progress = 0.4483 × 111.0 = **£49.76m**. Add the switchgear at cost, £9.00m. Total revenue = **£58.76m**, cost £48.0m, margin **£10.76m**.

The cross-check: 44.83% of the £24.0m total expected margin is £10.76m. The two routes agree exactly, which is the test that the adjustment has been applied consistently to price and to cost.

The naive answer took **£1.24m** of margin a year early, and it would have unwound in the period the switchgear was installed.

## Variations, claims and cumulative catch-up

A contract modification is accounted for in one of three ways, and the choice is not discretionary.

| Situation | Treatment | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| The added scope is distinct and priced at its standalone selling price | A separate contract | No effect on the original numbers |
| The remaining goods and services are distinct from those already transferred | Termination of the old contract and a new one | Applied prospectively to remaining work |
| The added scope is not distinct and forms part of a single obligation | Adjustment to the existing contract | Cumulative catch-up in the current period |

On integrated construction work the third row is the normal case, and it produces results that surprise people.

Continue the example. A variation adds **£8.0m** to the price, taking it to £128.0m, and adds **£7.0m** of cost, taking the forecast to £103.0m. Adjusted cost incurred is unchanged at £39.0m; adjusted forecast cost becomes £94.0m. Progress = 39.0 / 94.0 = **41.49%**.

Total expected margin is now 128.0 − 103.0 = £25.0m, so cumulative margin = 0.4149 × 25.0 = **£10.37m**, against **£10.76m** before the variation. A catch-up of **−£0.39m** in the period the variation was agreed.

An £8.0m variation reduced reported profit, because it diluted the margin percentage and reset the measure of progress across the whole contract. That is arithmetic, not an error, and it is the single most useful thing to explain to a commercial team before they celebrate a variation.

Claims behave differently again. Variable consideration enters the transaction price only to the extent that a significant reversal of cumulative revenue is highly unlikely later. A £4m claim in the cost report as an expected recovery is not £4m of revenue, and booking it early creates a reversal in a future period.

## Costs to fulfil and the loss provision

Mobilisation costs, site establishment and other costs incurred to fulfil a contract are capitalised where they relate directly to the contract, create or enhance resources used to satisfy it, and are expected to be recovered. They are then amortised on the same basis as progress.

Tender and bid costs are normally expensed as incurred, because they arise whether or not the contract is won. The exception is where they are explicitly chargeable to the customer regardless of outcome.

One point is missed constantly: IFRS 15 contains no onerous contract provision. When a contract is expected to lose money overall, the provision arises under the provisions standard, IAS 37, and the whole expected loss is recognised as soon as it is expected rather than spread across remaining work. The arithmetic of that step is worked in [how the estimate at completion reaches the accounts](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/eac-accounting).

## What the cost engineer must produce each month

Cost incurred to the cut-off date, split between costs that measure progress and costs that do not, with uninstalled materials identified separately.

A forecast cost at completion on the same basis, so the numerator and denominator are consistent, and a change log explaining every movement in it. The status of each variation against the three-row table above, and the recoverability assessment for anything claimed but not agreed.

Get those three right and the revenue calculation is mechanical. Get them wrong and the accounts are wrong in a way no journal entry can detect.

All three are outputs of the close, which is why the cut-off discipline described in [month-end close for projects](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/month-end-close-for-projects) decides the quality of the revenue number more than the accounting policy does.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is cost-to-cost required under IFRS 15 for construction?**
No. Input methods and output methods are both permitted, and the entity picks the one that faithfully depicts transfer of control, then applies it consistently to similar contracts. Cost-to-cost dominates because the data already exists in the cost ledger and is auditable. Its weakness is that it treats money spent as work done, which is the assumption earned value management exists to challenge.

**Can a milestone schedule be used to measure progress?**
Only if the milestones genuinely represent output transferred, which most payment milestones do not. Payment milestones are negotiated for cash flow and are frequently front-loaded, so using them as a progress measure imports that front-loading into revenue. A survey of work performed or verified physical quantities is a defensible output method; the schedule of payments almost never is.

**What happens to retention under IFRS 15?**
Retention is a payment timing matter, not a measurement one, so it does not reduce revenue recognised. It sits as a receivable or a contract asset depending on whether the right to payment has become unconditional. The effect lands in working capital rather than in margin, which is why a contract can be profitable and still starve the business of cash.

**Does IFRS 15 change when a claim can be recognised?**
It sets the constraint explicitly: variable amounts are included only to the extent a significant reversal is highly unlikely. In practice that means an entitlement supported by contract terms and correspondence may qualify, while an optimistic quantum on a disputed head of claim will not. The judgement is disclosed, so it is visible to anyone reading the accounts.

**Who in a project team needs to know this?**
The cost engineer and the commercial manager, not only the accountant. The forecast, the variation status and the split of costs that do and do not measure progress all originate on the project, and each is an accounting input.
