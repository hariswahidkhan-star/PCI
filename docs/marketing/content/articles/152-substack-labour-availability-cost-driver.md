# Construction labour cost driver: how to price scarcity

Labour availability is the construction labour cost driver you cannot hedge, and it costs you twice: scarcity raises the rate you pay and lowers the output you get for each paid hour. The rate rise appears in any re-price. The productivity loss appears only if you are measuring your own hours against installed quantities.

*Written first for this newsletter. The figures below are a worked example built from assumptions, not published market data.*

## Why is labour scarcity the construction labour cost driver you cannot hedge?

Because it is the one input that cannot be bought forward. Steel can be ordered early and stored; a fitter cannot be bought in March and kept in a container until October.

Every other escalation risk has some hedge available: fixed-price supply agreements, early procurement, currency forwards, index-linked fluctuation clauses. Labour has crew retention and a higher rate, and both of those are ways of paying more, not ways of paying less.

| Cost driver | Can you hedge it? | With what |
|---|---|---|
| Material prices | Mostly | Early order, fixed-price supply, fluctuation clause |
| Currency | Yes | Forward contracts matched to payment dates |
| Plant and equipment | Partly | Long-term hire, owned fleet, standby rates |
| Energy and fuel | Partly | Fixed tariffs, index clauses |
| Labour availability | No | Only rate, retention and programme change |

## How much does scarcity actually cost? The arithmetic

Take a mechanical package with 420,000 budgeted direct labour hours at £34 per hour, so £14.28m of labour on a £38m package. All figures are illustrative.

Scarcity arrives as two numbers. The market rate rises 10% to £37.40, and your own timesheets show a productivity factor of 1.22, meaning actual hours are running at 1.22 times budgeted hours for the same installed quantity.

Hours become 420,000 × 1.22 = 512,400. Cost becomes 512,400 × £37.40 = **£19.16m**, against a budget of £14.28m. That is £4.88m, or 34.2%.

Now split it, because the split is what tells you where to act.

| Effect | Calculation | Labour cost | Increase |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Budget | 420,000 × £34.00 | £14.28m | — |
| Rate only | 420,000 × £37.40 | £15.71m | £1.43m |
| Productivity only | 512,400 × £34.00 | £17.42m | £3.14m |
| Both | 512,400 × £37.40 | £19.16m | £4.88m |

On these assumptions the productivity effect is 2.2 times the rate effect, and the remaining £0.31m is the cross term where the two multiply. Your own factor decides the ratio on your own job; the point is that the split exists and can be worked out. Teams negotiate hard on the rate and accept the productivity factor as weather. It is the wrong way round.

The productivity factor has to come from your own measured hours against installed quantities, not from a published table. A factor you cannot trace to a timesheet is a plug.

## What does overtime actually buy?

Overtime is the standard answer to a labour shortage, and it works for a few weeks. Sustained, it converts a shortage into a cost problem with the shortage still in place.

Assume a 50-hour week, and assume your own records show output per paid hour falling to 0.90 of the 40-hour baseline once that has run for two months.

Productive output = 50 × 0.90 = 45 hours' worth, against 40 at baseline. That is 12.5% more output.

Paid cost = 40 hours at standard plus 10 at time and a half = 55 standard-hour equivalents. Cost per productive hour = 55 ÷ 45 = **1.222**, so you are paying 22% more for each unit of work to get 12.5% more of it per week.

That trade is worth making when the constraint is the date and the date is worth money. It is not worth making to recover a cost variance, because it deepens the variance you were trying to close.

## What labour scarcity does to the critical path

A crew shortage does not show up as a cost variance first. It shows up as duration, because most construction activities are resource-limited rather than duration-limited.

An activity with 320 fitter-days of content is planned at 40 days with a crew of eight. Resource the same activity with six and it takes 320 ÷ 6 = 53 days.

If the activity carried five days of total float, thirteen days of extension absorbs the float and pushes the completion date out by eight. The critical path has moved onto an activity that was never critical in the baseline, and it moved because of a resourcing decision rather than a logic change.

This is why resource levelling belongs in the schedule review, not after it. Levelling a plan without re-identifying the critical path produces a programme with a defensible-looking float report and no relationship to how the site will actually be manned.

## What it does to cash

Labour is the fastest-paying cost on a construction project and the slowest to be reimbursed. That combination is a working capital problem, and it scales with how much you self-perform.

The cash conversion cycle is days sales outstanding plus days inventory outstanding minus days payables outstanding. On a project, read "inventory" as work done but not yet certified.

Take a £38m annual turnover, 62 days from certification to cash in, 21 days of uncertified work in progress, and payment terms of 45 days on subcontractors and suppliers.

Fully subcontracted: CCC = 62 + 21 − 45 = **38 days**. Working capital tied up = 38 ÷ 365 × £38m = **£3.96m**.

Self-performed with 38% direct labour paid weekly, so labour effectively sits at seven days: blended payables = (0.38 × 7) + (0.62 × 45) = 2.66 + 27.90 = 30.6 days. CCC = 62 + 21 − 30.6 = **52.4 days**, and working capital rises to 52.4 ÷ 365 × £38m = **£5.46m**.

A £1.50m funding swing, with no change to the contract, the scope or the margin. When a shortage pushes you to self-perform work you had planned to sublet, that is the number to put in front of the finance director, and it belongs on the [S-curve the project draws its cash from](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/project-cash-flow-forecasting) the same week the decision is made.

## What to do about it, in order

Measure the productivity factor weekly by area and trade, against installed quantities. A monthly, project-wide factor averages the problem into invisibility.

Re-resource the programme before you re-price it. If the crew you can actually recruit is six rather than eight, the schedule built on eight is not a target, it is a fiction that will produce a claim.

Price retention as a cost line. Travel, accommodation, completion bonuses and rotation cycles are cheaper than recruiting the same trade twice at the new market rate, and they are far cheaper than the productivity loss of a crew that never reaches a learning curve.

Then re-forecast, and say plainly which part of the movement is rate and which part is productivity. A forecast that reports only the total invites a negotiation about the rate, which on the numbers above was the smaller half of the problem.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a productivity factor the same as a CPI?**
No, and mixing them causes real errors. A productivity factor compares actual hours to budgeted hours for the same physical quantity, so it is a labour measure. Cost performance index compares earned value to actual cost across everything in the control account, including materials and plant. A package can have a poor productivity factor and an acceptable CPI if material prices came in under budget.

**Where should a labour scarcity allowance sit?**
In the base estimate if you expect it, in risk if it is contingent on a market event you can describe. What it must not be is a single uplift percentage applied to the whole estimate, because scarcity is trade-specific and applying it evenly overprices the trades you can recruit and underprices the one you cannot.

**Does paying above market rate fix availability?**
It fixes your availability and worsens everyone else's, briefly. In a regional shortage the rate rise moves crews between projects rather than adding crews to the market, so the effect decays as competitors match it. Budget for the rate to be matched within a quarter, and treat retention and sequencing as the durable measures.

**How do you evidence a productivity claim?**
Contemporaneously, or not at all. Daily allocation sheets tying hours to areas, quantity records signed at the same frequency, and a measured baseline period before the disruption began. A retrospective global claim built from the final cost against the tender is the weakest form of the argument and the one most often rejected.

**Can AI forecast labour demand usefully?**
It is genuinely good at the tedious part: aggregating crew demand across a portfolio, spotting where two projects need the same scarce trade in the same month, and testing whether a levelled programme is achievable against the recruitment you have actually made. That is the useful edge of [what a model can and cannot do with a resourced programme](https://pciai.org/ai-for-construction-scheduling). It cannot tell you whether the local market has those people. That answer comes from your supply chain, and it should be written down with a date.
