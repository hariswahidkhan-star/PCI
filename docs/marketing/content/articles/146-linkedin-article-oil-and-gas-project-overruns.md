# Oil and gas project overruns: the causes and the data

Oil and gas project overruns are mostly settled before construction starts. A number is fixed at sanction against a design that is not finished, and the gap to outturn is then filled by escalation, brownfield interfaces, fabrication productivity and currency. Published overrun percentages disagree because each one measures from a different baseline.

Written for LinkedIn as an original. It sits under the Institute's project controls fundamentals pillar.

## Why do published oil and gas project overruns disagree with each other?

Because "the budget" is not one number, and the studies rarely say which one they used. PCI does not publish an industry overrun percentage, because there is no single defined population and no agreed denominator behind the figures in circulation.

The honest version is to state your baseline every time you quote a variance. Four different, defensible numbers exist for the same project on the same day.

| The number people call "the budget" | What it is | Why it moves |
|---|---|---|
| Sanction (FID) estimate | The board-approved control estimate | Set at a stated price base, scope and exchange rate, all of which age |
| Contract award total | Executed contracts plus owner's costs | Market conditions at award differ from those assumed at sanction |
| Current control budget | Sanction plus approved changes | Rises legitimately every time scope is added and approved |
| Outturn | What was actually spent | Includes escalation, interest during construction and owner's costs, depending on the accounting boundary |

Comparing an outturn that includes owner's costs and interest with a sanction estimate that excluded both produces an "overrun" that is partly a boundary error. Check the boundary before you check the variance.

## What does the sanction estimate actually contain?

It contains a quantity take-off, a set of rates, a price base, an exchange rate, a schedule, and a contingency sized against the risks known at the time. The accuracy of the whole thing follows the definition level of the design, not the effort put into the spreadsheet.

Estimate classification systems used across cost engineering, including AACE International's, all say the same thing: as engineering definition rises, the expected range around the estimate narrows. Sanctioning at a low definition level is a decision to accept a wide range, and the range is not a forecasting failure when it materialises.

The failure is presenting a single number to a board without the range, and then treating the single number as a promise. A sanction paper should carry the P50 and the P80, and say which one funded the project.

## How much of an overrun is simply escalation?

More than most reports admit, and it is calculable. Escalation is the movement in prices between the estimate's price base and the date money is actually spent.

Take a sanction estimate of **US$4.0bn** at a Q1 price base, with **55%** of the value exposed to commodity, fabrication and labour escalation, spent across four years so the average exposure runs about **2.5 years**.

The estimate carried **3% a year**. Suppose the market runs at **7% a year**. The excess is 4 points compounding: 1.04^2.5 = **1.1030**, so the exposed value grows by 10.3%.

Exposed value is 4.0 × 0.55 = **US$2.2bn**. The escalation shortfall is 2.2 × 0.1030 = **US$227m**, or **5.7% of the sanction estimate**, before a single hour of rework.

That is illustrative arithmetic, not project data. The mechanism is the point: an escalation assumption is a forecast, usually the least examined line in the estimate, and on a four-year spend curve a four-point error is worth hundreds of millions.

## How does productivity turn into money?

Through the productivity factor, which is realised hours divided by estimated hours for the same scope. It is the earliest reliable warning on a site, months before the cost report shows the damage.

Take **3.2 million** direct construction man-hours in the estimate. At a realised productivity factor of **1.30**, the job needs 3.2m × 1.30 = 4.16m hours, so **960,000** hours are added.

At an all-in charge-out of **US$85 an hour**, that is **US$81.6m** of direct cost. It rarely arrives alone: if the same loss extends the site by three months and site running costs are **US$4.2m a month**, add **US$12.6m** of time-related cost.

Brownfield work bites hardest. Tie-ins to a live facility bring permits, isolations, shutdown windows and access queues that no greenfield benchmark rate includes.

## Which forecast method should you believe?

The four estimate at completion methods answer the same question with four different assumptions about whether today's performance continues. Show the board all four, then say which one you believe and why.

Take **BAC = US$4.0bn**. At month 30: **PV = 1.80bn**, **EV = 1.62bn**, **AC = 1.85bn**.

Cost variance is EV − AC = **−US$230m**. Schedule variance is EV − PV = **−US$180m**. CPI = 1.62 ÷ 1.85 = **0.876**. SPI = 1.62 ÷ 1.80 = **0.900**.

| Method | Formula | What it assumes | When it is the right one |
|---|---|---|---|
| Remaining work at plan | EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) | The overspend was a one-off event now closed | A discrete, priced, finished event such as a single vessel replacement |
| Remaining work at the current cost performance | EAC = BAC ÷ CPI | The cause of the variance is systemic and continues | Productivity or rate problems that are not fixed |
| Remaining work under cost and schedule pressure | EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI) | Being late is itself making the work more expensive | Delay-driven jobs paying acceleration, overtime and extended preliminaries |
| Bottom-up re-estimate | EAC = AC + a re-estimated ETC | The past tells you little because the remaining scope differs | After a major scope change, or when commissioning dominates the remainder |

Running the numbers: method one gives 1.85 + 2.38 = **US$4.23bn**. Method two gives 4.00 ÷ 0.876 = **US$4.57bn**. Method three gives 1.85 + 2.38 ÷ (0.876 × 0.900) = 1.85 + 3.02 = **US$4.87bn**.

The spread between the cheapest and dearest method is **US$640m**, about **16% of the sanction estimate**, on identical data. Anyone quoting one EAC without saying which method produced it is hiding that spread.

The reality check is TCPI. To land on BAC you need (4.00 − 1.62) ÷ (4.00 − 1.85) = 2.38 ÷ 2.15 = **1.107**, so the remaining work must run 11% better than plan while it has been running 12% worse than plan. That is a claim requiring evidence, not optimism.

## What the finance side sees when a project slips

A changed forecast is an accounting event, not only a reporting one, and this is where delivery and finance stop agreeing.

Where a contractor recognises revenue over time using an input measure — cost incurred divided by total forecast cost — raising the total forecast cost lowers the percentage complete. Revenue already recognised is corrected in the month the forecast changes, so a delivery forecast update becomes a profit movement.

On the owner's side, borrowing costs attributable to an asset under construction are capitalised while construction is active, and capitalisation stops when the asset is ready for use. A slip moves cost between the balance sheet and the income statement, on a date someone has to defend.

An engineer is examined on float, progress and productivity, and almost never on cut-off or capitalisation. An accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised, and almost never on a productivity factor. Overruns live in that gap, which is why the PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI) credential, at 16 domains and 61 knowledge areas, examines both sides.

## Which indicators move before the cost does?

Cost reports are lagging by construction. The indicators below move first, cost almost nothing to collect, and are usually already in someone's system.

| Indicator | What it is telling you | Why it leads |
|---|---|---|
| Deliverables issued for construction against plan | Engineering is behind the field | Drawings late means either the field waits or it builds from incomplete information |
| Material take-off growth between estimate revisions | The quantity basis was optimistic | Quantities settle long before rates are challenged |
| Vendor data returned on time | Fabrication and installation logic is at risk | Late vendor data delays foundations, layouts and structural steel |
| Commodity index against the estimate's price base | The escalation assumption is stale | The index is public and updates monthly, unlike your estimate |

Report those four against a stated data date, alongside the [four EAC methods](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/four-eac-formulas), and the forecast conversation changes from opinion to evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a cost increase the same as an overrun?**
No. An approved scope addition raises the control budget legitimately and is not an overrun, though it does raise the outturn. An overrun is spending more than the approved budget for the same scope. Most disputed "overrun" figures mix the two, which is why the scope basis has to be stated alongside every variance you publish.

**Should contingency and escalation sit inside the sanction estimate?**
Both belong in the funded amount, as separate lines with separate owners. Contingency covers identified risk within the defined scope, and escalation covers the movement between the price base and the spend date. Burying either inside rates removes the ability to explain a variance later, and makes drawdown impossible to monitor.

**Which EAC method do auditors usually expect?**
There is no universally required method, and the answer depends on the contract and the accounting policy. What auditors do consistently test is consistency and evidence: the same method applied period to period, a documented reason when it changes, and support for the total forecast cost when revenue is recognised on an input measure. Switching method to improve a reported margin is the finding nobody wants.

**When does a forecast change have to be reported?**
As soon as it is a better estimate than the one already published, not at the next quarter gate. Holding a known deterioration closes the decision window, and where the forecast feeds revenue recognition it affects a reported number. Set a materiality threshold in the reporting procedure and follow it.
