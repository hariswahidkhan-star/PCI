# AACE CCP vs PMP: which certification fits your role

AACE CCP vs PMP is not a choice between levels. CCP is AACE International's cost engineering credential, examining estimating, cost control, planning, economics and the total cost picture. PMP is PMI's general credential for managing projects. Take CCP if you own the numbers, PMP if you own the project.

Both are respected. Neither substitutes for the other, and holding one does not make the other redundant.

## AACE CCP vs PMP: what each one actually examines

| | CCP (AACE International) | PMP (PMI) |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Cost engineering across the asset life cycle: estimating, cost control, planning, economic analysis, earned value | Managing projects: people, process and business environment, across predictive and adaptive approaches |
| Typical holder | Cost engineer, estimator, project controls manager, commercial lead | Project manager, programme manager, delivery lead |
| Experience gate | Substantial documented experience, with academic study substituting for part of it | Documented months leading projects, plus formal project management education hours |
| Assessment shape | Written examination, with a written technical component in addition to objective questions | Situational multiple choice against a published exam content outline |
| Numeric depth | Central; the arithmetic is the examination | Present, but secondary to judgement and process |
| Renewal | Recertification cycle with continuing education | Professional development units on a fixed cycle |
| Travels well in | Engineering, construction, energy, mining, government cost communities | Almost every sector, and most HR filters |

Requirements and fees are revised, so confirm both against the current AACE and PMI handbooks before you plan a study calendar.

## The difference in one question each

A PMP-flavoured item gives you a situation and asks what you do next: a sponsor changes scope mid-sprint, a supplier misses a gate, a stakeholder escalates. The answer is a decision and a process.

A CCP-flavoured item gives you numbers and asks what they mean.

Take a package with a budget at completion of **£9.00m**. Planned value is **£3.90m**, earned value is **£3.60m**, actual cost is **£4.05m**.

Cost performance index = 3.60 ÷ 4.05 = **0.889**. Schedule performance index = 3.60 ÷ 3.90 = **0.923**.

Forecast one way: EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) = 4.05 + 5.40 = **£9.45m**, which assumes the overrun is behind you.
Forecast the other way: EAC = BAC ÷ CPI = 9.00 ÷ 0.889 = **£10.13m**, which assumes the run rate continues.

The gap is **£680,000** on one dataset. Then the question that separates a cost professional from an interested observer: what performance would the remaining work need to still land on £9.00m?

To-complete performance index = (BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC) = 5.40 ÷ 4.95 = **1.091**.

The team has been running at 0.889 and would have to run at 1.091, a **23% improvement** sustained to the end. Anyone can say "we will recover". The index says what recovery would have to mean, and that is the CCP examination in miniature.

## Money, not just cost: what a cost credential should make you fluent in

Cost control is about spend. Commercial survival is about when the cash arrives.

Take a contractor turning over **£18.0m** a year on a project, which is £18,000,000 ÷ 365 = **£49,315 a day**.

Money owed by the client after certification runs at 74 days. Work done but not yet certified sits at 21 days. Money owed to the supply chain runs at 46 days.

Cash conversion cycle = 74 + 21 − 46 = **49 days**.

Working capital tied up = 49 × 49,315 = **£2.42m**. At a 9% cost of money that is roughly **£218,000 a year** funding the gap between doing the work and being paid for it.

Cut the certification lag by ten days and you release 10 × 49,315 = **£493,000** without winning a single new pound of work. Neither the PMP nor the CCP syllabus is built around treasury, but a cost professional who cannot follow that arithmetic will keep optimising the wrong number.

## The subject neither examination covers

Here is the boundary that decides whether a project reports a profit.

Revenue on a long-term contract is recognised under a five-step model, described here in plain terms rather than reproduced from the standard. Identify the contract with the customer. Identify the distinct promises in it.

Determine the transaction price, including any variable amounts and the limit on recognising them. Allocate that price across the promises. Recognise revenue as each promise is satisfied, over time or at a point in time.

Step three is where project people get caught. Suppose you have incurred **£520,000** of cost on a variation you have claimed at **£600,000**, and the client has not yet agreed it.

The cost is real and it is in your cost report this month. The revenue is variable consideration, and it may only be recognised to the extent that a significant reversal is not expected later. If your evidence does not support that, the month shows **£520,000** of cost against nothing, and the package reports a loss it will later reverse.

A chartered accountant is examined on that constraint and almost never on a critical path. An engineer is examined on float and almost never on cut-off.

The project lives in the overlap, and the overlap is where the money is lost. PCI's credentials examine both sides deliberately, which is a different proposition from CCP or PMP rather than a better version of either.

## Which one first?

| Your situation | Take first | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| You build estimates and defend forecasts | CCP | Your daily work is its syllabus |
| You are accountable for delivery, scope and the team | PMP | Its situational items match your decisions |
| You are a planner heading for controls management | CCP, after a scheduling credential | Cost control is the gap most planners have |
| Your employer gates promotion on PMP | PMP | The gate is the reason; be honest about that |
| You sign forecasts that a finance function then books | A credential covering both sides | Neither CCP nor PMP examines the accounting treatment |
| You are early in your career | Neither yet | Both set experience gates for good reasons |

Two credentials in one year rarely changes anything an employer can see. One credential plus a worked example from your own project changes an interview.

## Where PCI fits

The Project Controls Institute is an independent certifying body with three AI-era credentials.

| Credential | Full name | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| PCL-AI | PCI AI Project Controls Leader | 13 domains, 61 knowledge areas |
| PFL-AI | PCI AI Project Finance Leader | 16 domains, 61 knowledge areas |
| PML-AI | PCI Project Management Leader – AI | 16 domains, 63 knowledge areas |

Each Body of Knowledge is proportioned 40% finance and reporting, 40% project management and 20% governed AI. Behind them sit 113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements, and 92 sector case studies across the three volumes (26 + 33 + 33). The calculation content of the PFL-AI and PML-AI volumes is verified by 15,613 machine calculation checks, all passing; PCL-AI has no equivalent suite.

Entry is around three years of professional experience in any field, with no degree requirement. The examination is scenario-based multiple choice, single best answer, sat under remote proctoring, and the fee opens a 12-month scheduling window from payment.

PCI is not accredited by ANAB, UKAS, IAS or any other ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body and does not claim to be. The scheme is designed with reference to ISO/IEC 17024 principles, including a criterion-referenced cut score and separation of preparation from the certification decision. PCI claims no recognition, endorsement, affiliation or partnership with AACE, PMI or any other body.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is CCP harder than PMP?**
They are hard in different ways. CCP demands numerical fluency and, for many candidates, a written component that cannot be crammed. PMP demands breadth across predictive and adaptive delivery and a feel for the way its situational items are written. Cost engineers usually find CCP the more natural paper and PMP the more unfamiliar vocabulary.

**Which is more recognised by employers?**
PMP passes more HR filters because it spans every sector. CCP carries more weight with people who read cost reports for a living, particularly in engineering, energy and heavy construction. If a specific employer matters, read their live job advertisements and count which acronym appears in the requirements rather than the nice-to-haves.

**Can I hold both CCP and PMP?**
Yes, and plenty of controls managers do, since the pair covers the numbers and the delivery. Sequence them rather than stacking them: certify the work you already do, use it for a year, then take the second when the questions you cannot answer have moved into that territory. Both carry independent renewal obligations.

**Does either one examine AI use in project controls?**
Not as a central subject. Both are revised periodically and technology appears in context, but neither is built around governing model output, and no examination should be assumed to cover it. If your organisation is deploying forecasting or schedule-review tools, look for a syllabus that names governed AI explicitly and says what it examines.

**How long does preparation take?**
Plan in hours, not weeks. Eighty hours over five or six months is realistic for a working practitioner on either credential, and more if a written technical component is required. The efficient route is a timed mock early, then study only the domains it exposes, rather than reading the syllabus front to back.

**Is an unaccredited credential a red flag?**
Not automatically, but it shifts the burden of proof onto the syllabus. Ask any body how its pass mark is set, whether preparation is separated from the certification decision, and what an appeal process looks like. A published Body of Knowledge you can read before paying tells you more than a logo you cannot verify.
