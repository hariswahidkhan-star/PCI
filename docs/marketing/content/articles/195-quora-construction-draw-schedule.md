# What is a construction draw schedule and how does it work?

A construction draw schedule is the agreed timetable and set of conditions under which a lender or owner releases development funds in instalments as work is completed and verified. Each draw is a request supported by evidence, checked by a monitoring surveyor or inspector, and paid against verified progress rather than against a promise.

It answers a different question from the schedule of values. The valuation says what the work is worth; the draw schedule says when the money actually arrives, and what has to be true first.

## What is in a draw schedule?

The document sets out draw periods, usually monthly, and what each draw is funded against — a valued application, a milestone, or a cost-to-complete test. It also fixes the order in which sources of funds are used.

It lists the evidence required with each request: the application and supporting valuation, invoices or subcontractor certificates, lien waivers or their local equivalent, updated cost-to-complete, insurance and permit confirmations, and the surveyor's certificate.

It states the timing between each step. That timing, not the total facility, is what determines whether a project runs out of cash while remaining fully funded on paper.

## How does the funding sequence work in practice?

Take a 48-unit residential scheme with a total development cost of £6.00m over twelve months. Funding is £1.50m of equity and £4.50m of senior debt, with equity drawn first — the most common structure, and the one that concentrates risk in the early months for the developer.

Cumulative net certified value at the end of month three is £980,000, all funded by equity.

| Month | Gross work valued | Retention at 5% | Net certified | Funded from equity | Funded from debt |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | £520,000 | £26,000 | £494,000 | £494,000 | £0 |
| 5 | £640,000 | £32,000 | £608,000 | £26,000 | £582,000 |
| 6 | £740,000 | £37,000 | £703,000 | £0 | £703,000 |

The arithmetic: equity of £1,500,000 less £980,000 already used leaves £520,000, which covers month four's £494,000 and £26,000 of month five. Debt drawn to the end of month six is 582,000 + 703,000 = **£1,285,000**, and total funding drawn is £2,785,000.

Retention held to date across these three months is 26,000 + 32,000 + 37,000 = £95,000. It is not funded, because it has not been certified for payment, but the contractor has already paid the labour and materials that produced it.

## Construction draw schedule vs the documents it sits beside

| Document | Produced by | What it governs | Trigger for money moving | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Draw schedule | Lender, agreed at financial close | When and on what evidence funds release | Certified draw request | Funds are available but late |
| Schedule of values | Contractor, agreed with client | How work is valued each period | Agreed percentage complete | Front-end loading distorts progress |
| Cash flow forecast | Project controls team | What the project expects to need | Nothing — it is a forecast | Built on the valuation date, not the payment date |
| Payment application | Contractor | What is claimed this period | Certification | Claimed early, certified late |

The third row is where most project teams lose money without noticing. A cash flow forecast built from the valuation curve shows cash leaving on the day work is done and arriving on the day it is valued, and neither is true.

## Why does a fully funded project still run short of cash?

Because the draw cycle takes time, and payroll does not. The contractor pays labour weekly, pays some suppliers on delivery, and waits for certification and then for the lender's funding period.

The cash conversion cycle puts a number on that wait. It is days inventory outstanding plus days sales outstanding minus days payables outstanding: how long money is tied up between paying for inputs and being paid for outputs.

| Component | Days | What it represents |
|---|---:|---|
| Days inventory outstanding | 25 | Materials and work in progress held before certification |
| Days sales outstanding | 52 | Application, certification and the lender's funding period |
| Days payables outstanding | (55) | Supplier terms actually taken |
| **Cash conversion cycle** | **22** | Days of spend funded by the contractor |

At an average spend of £500,000 a month, that is 500,000 ÷ 30 = £16,667 a day, so 22 days ties up **£366,700** of working capital.

Now let a monitoring visit slip and the funding period stretch by fifteen days. Days sales outstanding becomes 67, the cycle becomes 37 days, and the requirement becomes 37 × 16,667 = **£616,700**.

That is £250,000 of extra working capital demanded by a change in paperwork timing, with no change whatsoever to the work on site. It is the clearest argument there is for treating the draw schedule as a controls document rather than a finance formality.

## What else does the draw schedule cost you?

Interest during construction accrues on the drawn balance, so the shape of the draw curve, not just its total, drives the finance cost. Drawing early costs more; drawing late risks a funding gap.

On the scheme above, if debt is drawn from month five to month twelve to a peak of £4.50m, the average drawn balance is roughly £2.00m over eight months. At an illustrative all-in rate of 9%, interest during construction is 2.00m × 9% × 8 ÷ 12 = **£120,000**.

That figure belongs in the cost plan as a line, because it is a real cost driven by decisions the project team makes about sequencing and valuation timing.

Retention release matters for the same reason. On a build contract of £4.80m within the £6.00m, retention at 5% is £240,000, commonly released half at practical completion and half at the end of the defects period. A contractor forecasting that £120,000 tail into the wrong quarter has a cash problem that has nothing to do with construction.

## Frequently asked questions

**Who approves a construction draw?**
The lender approves it, usually on the recommendation of an independent monitoring surveyor or construction consultant who visits site, tests the claimed progress against the schedule of values, and confirms that the remaining facility is still sufficient to complete the works. The surveyor's certificate is the document the money actually moves against.

**What is a cost-to-complete test?**
A condition that the undrawn facility plus remaining equity must be at least equal to the forecast cost of completing the works. Failing it is usually an event of default, and it can be failed by a forecast alone. That makes the estimate at completion a covenant input, not just a management number, and it should be prepared with that in mind.

**How is a draw schedule different from a payment schedule?**
A payment schedule governs what the client owes the contractor under the building contract. A draw schedule governs what the lender releases to the borrower under the facility agreement. They involve different parties and different documents, and a contractor can be properly certified for payment while the borrower is still waiting on a draw.

**Can a draw be made for materials not yet installed?**
Sometimes, and always subject to conditions. Lenders that permit it typically require the materials to be on site, protected, insured, properly identified and free of supplier title retention. Off-site materials are funded much more rarely, because the lender's security over them is weaker and harder to enforce.

**What happens if a draw is refused?**
The work does not stop, which is the problem. The contractor keeps spending while the request is re-worked, so the cost of a refusal is measured in the working capital calculated above rather than in the delay itself. Most refusals are documentary, so the fix is a pre-submission checklist agreed with the surveyor rather than a renegotiation.

**How should project controls support the draw process?**
By owning the evidence pack and its timing: valuation, cost-to-complete, updated programme and change log, prepared before the cut-off rather than after it. Forecasting cash against the funding dates instead of the valuation dates is the other half. A forecast built on valuation dates will be wrong by roughly the whole cash conversion cycle, every month.
