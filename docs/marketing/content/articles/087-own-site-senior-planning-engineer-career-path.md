# Senior planning engineer career path

The senior planning engineer career path runs planner, planning engineer, senior planning engineer, then planning manager or project controls manager. The step to senior is not granted for years served. It is granted at the point you can forecast rather than report, put a price on a delay, and hold that position when the answer is unwelcome.

The gap between a planning engineer and a senior one is not more schedule. It is judgement that survives challenge.

## What changes at the step up

**You stop describing and start deciding.** A planning engineer reports that the driving path has moved. A senior one arrives with two recovery options and the cost of each.

**Your number gets used.** Somebody's commercial position, cash forecast or board report is built on the date you signed, which is why the questioning gets harder rather than easier.

**You own the assumption, not the output.** Anyone can produce an estimate at completion. The senior role is being able to say why that method rather than the other three, out loud, in a room that would prefer a different answer.

**You start managing inputs you do not control.** Subcontractor programmes, engineering deliverables, client approvals. Most senior schedule failures are interface failures, not logic failures.

## The senior planning engineer career path, rung by rung

| Rung | What you own | The gate to the next rung | Evidence that clears it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planner | Updating and maintaining the programme | Can you produce a status that needs no correction? | A full cycle statused clean, on time |
| Planning engineer | Logic, durations and the date on a package | Can you derive, not just record? | A duration built from quantity, output and calendar that held |
| Senior planning engineer | The programme on a project, and the story it tells | Can you price a slip and defend a forecast? | A recovery option chosen on your numbers |
| Planning manager | Method, systems and planners across projects | Can you make it repeatable without you? | A standard other projects adopted |
| Project controls manager | Cost, schedule, reporting and the team | Can you answer to a board for the whole number? | A decision taken on your analysis |

The gate column is the useful one: each rung asks a different question, and excelling at the previous one is not an answer.

## The arithmetic: pricing a four-week slip

Here is the calculation that separates the two grades. A driving path has slipped 20 working days — four weeks. Time-related site costs run at £42,000 per week and liquidated damages at £25,000 per week beyond contract completion.

**Option A — accept the slip.** Four weeks of prolongation plus four weeks of damages: 4 × (42,000 + 25,000) = **£268,000**.

**Option B — accelerate.** A second shift on the steel package costs £31,000 per week and is assumed to recover half a week for every week it runs. Six weeks of second shift costs 6 × 31,000 = £186,000 and recovers three weeks, leaving one week to absorb at £67,000. Total **£253,000**.

Option B is £15,000 cheaper. A planning engineer stops there. A senior planning engineer says the next sentence: that £15,000 is 5.6% of the exposure and it rests entirely on the recovery rate.

Test it. If the second shift returns 0.4 of a week per week instead of 0.5, six weeks recovers 2.4 weeks, the residual is 1.6 weeks at £67,000, and Option B costs 186,000 + 107,200 = **£293,200** — now £25,200 worse than doing nothing.

The honest recommendation is therefore Option B with a review gate at week three, measured against actual recovery rather than assumed recovery. That sentence is the whole promotion.

## The four forecasts, and the one you sign

The other half of the senior role is the outturn cost, because the programme drives it. Take a project with a budget at completion (BAC) of £48.0m. At the month-14 data date, planned value (PV) is £21.6m, earned value (EV) is £19.2m and actual cost (AC) is £22.5m.

- Cost variance: CV = EV − AC = 19.2 − 22.5 = **−£3.3m**
- Schedule variance: SV = EV − PV = 19.2 − 21.6 = **−£2.4m**
- Cost performance index: CPI = EV ÷ AC = 19.2 ÷ 22.5 = **0.853**
- Schedule performance index: SPI = EV ÷ PV = 19.2 ÷ 21.6 = **0.889**

| EAC method | Calculation on these inputs | Result | What it assumes |
|---|---|---:|---|
| AC + (BAC − EV) | 22.5 + (48.0 − 19.2) | **£51.3m** | The overspend was a one-off; the remaining work runs at plan |
| BAC ÷ CPI | 48.0 ÷ 0.853 | **£56.25m** | Cost efficiency to date is the best available predictor of the rest |
| AC + (BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI) | 22.5 + 28.8 ÷ 0.759 | **£60.47m** | Schedule pressure keeps driving cost, and both continue |
| AC + bottom-up estimate to complete | 22.5 + a re-estimate of the remaining scope | Whatever the re-estimate says | The people doing the work have re-priced it; the only method that can honestly come in lower |

The spread is £51.3m to £60.47m — **£9.17m** of difference produced by nothing but which assumption is signed. Naming the assumption in the same sentence as the number is the job.

One more measure earns its place. To-complete performance index: TCPI = (BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC) = 28.8 ÷ 25.5 = **1.129**. The remaining work must run 13% more efficiently than the plan, against the 0.853 achieved so far. That is the arithmetic way of saying a recovery narrative is not credible.

The methods, and where each of them fails, are set out in [the four EAC formulas](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/four-eac-formulas).

## What P80 means, and why you will be asked

A deterministic programme gives one date. A schedule risk analysis gives a distribution, and senior planners are asked for the number from the distribution.

Say the deterministic remaining duration is 320 days, the P50 comes back at 338 days and the P80 at 366 days. The P80 is the duration you would expect to meet or beat in four runs out of five.

The gap between deterministic and P80 — 46 days here — is the schedule contingency conversation. Presenting the 320 as a commitment and the 366 as a risk is the mistake; the deterministic date usually has a far lower confidence than anyone in the room assumes.

How to build that model, and what makes it worthless, is covered in [quantitative schedule risk analysis](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/quantitative-schedule-risk-analysis).

## Six moves that get you the step up

**1. Take the forecast, not just the update.** Volunteer to produce the estimate at completion and to be the person who explains it.

**2. Learn to read the cost report line by line.** Commitment, accrual, actual, forecast. Planners who can speak in money get promoted; planners who speak only in days stay where they are.

**3. Own an interface nobody wants.** Engineering deliverables or subcontract programmes. Interfaces are where senior scheduling is really done.

**4. Run one delay analysis properly.** A time impact analysis on a real event, with the baseline, the event, the impacted programme and a written narrative.

**5. Build the review gate habit.** Every recommendation you give gets a date and a measure attached. This is the single most visible behaviour difference between the two grades.

**6. Close the finance gap deliberately.** It is the gap on almost every planner's record, and it is the one that decides the manager step later.

## What stalls the career path

**Tool depth with no method depth.** Being the fastest in Primavera is entry-level competence, not seniority.

**A forecast with no named method.** If you cannot say which estimate at completion you used and why, the number is an opinion in a spreadsheet.

**No cost or contract vocabulary.** Prolongation, accrual, cut-off, contract asset. A planner who cannot use these words hands the commercial argument to someone else.

## Where a credential fits

The senior planning role sits exactly on the boundary between delivery and money. The four-week slip above is a float question and a £268,000 question at the same time, and the EAC you sign becomes an accounting estimate downstream.

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised, almost never on a driving path. An engineer is examined on float, almost never on cut-off. The project lives in the overlap, and so does the loss.

PCI examines that overlap deliberately. The PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) credential runs to 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas, with a Body of Knowledge proportioned **40 / 40 / 20** across finance and reporting, project management and governed AI, and resting on **113 mandatory PCI Standards carrying 532 process requirements**. For what a planning credential can and cannot evidence, see [certified planning engineer, honestly assessed](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/certified-planning-engineer).

## Frequently asked questions

**How many years does it take to reach senior planning engineer?**
Commonly five to eight years from a first construction or project role, but the range is wide and the driver is exposure rather than time. A planner who has statused one contract type for six years is often behind someone who has built a baseline, defended a slip and run a delay analysis within four.

**Do I need a certification to be promoted to senior planner?**
Not usually to be promoted internally. It matters most at the point you move employers or markets, where a client-approved CV needs something external to point at. Certify against the gap on your record rather than the title you want, and the gap is usually cost and contract rather than more scheduling.

**Is senior planning engineer a better path than project controls manager?**
They are the same path at different depths. Senior planning goes deeper into schedule, risk and delay, and stays technical. Project controls management widens into cost, reporting and people, and gives up some depth. Choose by whether you want to be the person who answers the technical question or the person who answers for the whole number.

**What is the hardest interview question at this grade?**
"Which estimate at completion did you sign, and why not one of the others?" It cannot be answered without understanding the four methods and their assumptions, and it exposes anyone who has only ever produced the number a system generated.
