# Earned value management training: what actually works

Earned value management training is worth paying for when it teaches progress measurement and forecast judgement, and worth skipping when it teaches formulas. The arithmetic takes an afternoon. Choosing an earning rule, defending an estimate at completion, and spotting a manufactured index take practice on real data, with someone correcting you.

Most courses spend their time on the afternoon's worth.

## What should earned value management training cover?

Six modules, in this order. Anything that starts at module three is teaching the easy part first.

| Module | You should leave able to | How to test it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Baseline and control accounts | Map a WBS to control accounts and a time-phased budget | Build one from a live project's own breakdown |
| 2. Earning rules | Pick a rule per work type and say how it could be gamed | Re-earn one month under two different rules |
| 3. Cut-off and data integrity | Measure EV and AC to the same date, accruals included | Trace one month's actuals from ledger to report |
| 4. The measures | CV, SV, CPI, SPI, VAC, TCPI, computed by hand | Twenty positions, no spreadsheet |
| 5. Forecasting | Produce four EAC values and defend one in a paragraph | The paragraph, marked by someone who will argue |
| 6. Thresholds and reporting | Set variance thresholds that trigger action, not commentary | Write the threshold rule into a procedure |

Module two is the one to interrogate before booking. It decides every number the rest of the course produces.

## The exercise that exposes weak training

The same month, earned three ways. This takes fifteen minutes and it ends most arguments about whether a course is serious.

A pipe spool fabrication package. Budget at completion is £2.40m for 200 spools. At the cut-off, 96 spools are complete, 34 are started but incomplete, and actual cost is £1.42m.

**Units complete (0/100 per spool).** EV = 96 / 200 × 2.40 = **£1.152m**. CPI = 1.152 / 1.42 = **0.811**.

**50/50 rule.** EV = (96 + 17) / 200 × 2.40 = **£1.356m**. CPI = 1.356 / 1.42 = **0.955**.

**Assessed percent complete, supervisor claims 65%.** EV = 0.65 × 2.40 = **£1.56m**. CPI = 1.56 / 1.42 = **1.099**.

Three legitimate-looking rules, one month, one set of spools. The first says the package returns 81 pence of budgeted work per pound spent, the second says 96 pence, the third says 110 pence.

Now forecast each at the rate achieved, EAC = BAC / CPI: **£2.96m**, **£2.51m** and **£2.18m**. A spread of **£0.77m** on a £2.4m package, produced entirely by a decision taken before the first spool was cut.

That is why the rule belongs in the procedure, agreed before work starts, and why a course that hands you the formulas without the rules has taught you nothing you can defend.

## Level of effort, and the training that never mentions it

Level of effort earns value with the passage of time rather than with output. Applied to genuine support functions it is reasonable; applied to discrete work it manufactures performance.

Take a £10.0m budget with £4.0m sitting on level of effort and £6.0m of discrete work. At the halfway point, level of effort has earned £2.0m against £2.0m spent, so it reports a CPI of exactly 1.00 whatever happened on site.

The discrete work has earned £2.4m against £3.0m spent, a CPI of **0.80**.

Blended, the report shows EV 4.4 / AC 5.0 = **0.88**. The pack returns 88 pence per pound while the work that actually builds the asset returns 80 pence, and the gap is pure arithmetic from the level-of-effort share.

The forecast follows the deception. BAC / blended CPI = 10.0 / 0.88 = **£11.36m**, while forecasting the two streams separately gives 6.0 / 0.80 + 4.0 = **£11.50m**, and that assumes the support cost holds.

Training that never asks what share of the baseline sits on level of effort has skipped the first question a reviewer asks.

## Which training format fixes which problem

| Format | Fixes | Evidence it worked | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-house workshop using your own project data | Rules, cut-off and reporting that suit your contracts | A rewritten cost control procedure | The room defends existing habits |
| Open online cohort with marked exercises | Method, and exposure to how others do it | Marked work you kept | Sessions are recordings with a chat window |
| Self-paced modules | Vocabulary and formulas, cheaply | Honestly, very little | Nobody challenges your answers |
| Vendor tool training | Getting the system to produce the numbers | A working EVM set-up | It becomes a menu tour with an EVM title |
| Coaching through two live month-ends | Judgement, under real pressure | A forecast you defended and were right about | The coach does the work instead of marking it |

The strongest combination for a working practitioner is self-paced theory, marked exercises, then one coached month-end. The coached month-end is the part almost nobody buys and the part that changes behaviour.

## A six-week plan that fits around a job

Week 1. Write down the earning rule applied to every work type on your current project, and next to each one, how a supervisor could inflate it.

Week 2. Trace one month's actual cost from ledger to cost report. Find the accrual that is missing, late or double-counted, because there is always one.

Week 3. Compute all six measures by hand on twenty positions until each takes under two minutes. Speed matters because hesitation reads as uncertainty in a review.

Week 4. Take three real packages, compute four EAC values for each, and write one paragraph defending your choice. Give the paragraphs to someone who will argue with them.

Week 5. Decompose a cost variance into rate, quantity and scope until the explanation would satisfy an auditor rather than a sponsor in a hurry.

Week 6. Set variance thresholds and write them into a procedure, then run a full timed mock of whatever assessment you are heading for.

Roughly 40 hours of work. It is more effective than most paid courses because every exercise runs on data you have to live with.

## How to judge an EVM trainer in ten minutes

Ask them to explain why SPI returns to 1.00 on a project finishing a year late. If earned schedule does not come up, the syllabus stops before the interesting part.

Ask what share of a baseline they would allow on level of effort, and what they do when they find discrete work sitting on it.

Ask which EAC method they teach as the default. The right answer is that there is no default, and the choice follows the cause of the variance.

Ask who marks the exercises and what they write on them. A trainer who cannot answer this is selling content, not training.

## What training cannot fix

Bad source data. If progress is claimed by the people whose bonus depends on it, and nobody surveys the quantity, no amount of training will make the indices honest.

Rules set by contract. On some programmes the earning rules, thresholds and reporting formats are contractually fixed, and your job is to operate them accurately, not to redesign them.

The credential question. Training produces a certificate of attendance from the organisation that took your money. Certification is an independent body examining you against a published standard, and the difference is set out in [what an EVM certification examines](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/earned-value-management-certification).

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does it take to learn earned value properly?**
The measures take an afternoon and the judgement takes about two live month-ends. A structured 40-hour effort spread over six weeks, run on your own project data, gets most practitioners to the point where they can defend a forecast. Anyone promising competence in a one-day workshop is selling awareness of the vocabulary.

**Is free earned value training good enough?**
For the formulas, yes. Published standards and the [PCL-AI Body of Knowledge](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/body-of-knowledge) give you the method free, and PCI publishes the syllabus so you can study from it without buying anything. What free material cannot give you is someone marking your reasoning, which is the part that changes how you forecast.

**Should training use my company's data or a case study?**
Both, in that order of importance. Case studies teach the pattern; your own data teaches you what your organisation gets wrong, which is usually cut-off discipline or an earning rule inherited from a project that ended years ago. If a provider refuses to work with your data under an NDA, ask why.

**Does earned value training cover earned schedule?**
It should, and many courses skip it. SPI converges on 1.00 as a project completes, so it reports on time at the moment it is most wrong; earned schedule restates performance in time units instead of money. A trainer who has not heard of it is teaching a syllabus that stopped being current a long time ago.

**Do I need training before a certification examination?**
No. PCI keeps preparation and the certification decision separate on purpose, so no provider can influence a result, and the entry requirement is three years of professional experience in any field. Choose training to close named gaps, not as a ticket to sit, and study the published standard first to find out what those gaps actually are.
