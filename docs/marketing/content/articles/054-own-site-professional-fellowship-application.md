# Professional fellowship application: what assessors look for

A professional fellowship application is scored on four things: the scope of responsibility you carried, what you contributed to the discipline beyond your own job, whether independent people corroborate it, and whether your conduct record is clear. Adjectives score nothing. Artefacts, dates, named referees and arithmetic score everything.

Assessors are not looking for the best career. They are looking for the case they can defend in the minutes when someone asks why this application succeeded and a similar one did not.

## How a professional fellowship application is assessed

Marking against a published document, and recording reasons. That is the whole job, and understanding it changes how you write.

A panel member reads your case with the criteria beside it, decides whether each criterion is met, and notes why. Anything in your case that maps to no criterion is unscoreable, however impressive it is.

So the structure that works is the boring one: one section per published criterion, in the institution's own order, using the institution's own words as headings. Make it possible to mark you without hunting.

## The four things being scored

**Scope carried.** Roles held, budgets and contract values owned, headcount led, dates. This is the easiest criterion to evidence and the one most applications over-invest in.

**Contribution made.** What changed in the discipline because of you, outside your own responsibilities. A method others adopted, a standard written, teaching that raised other people's capability, published work.

**Corroboration.** Whether people independent of you confirm the claim in specific terms. A panel weighs a referee who received the contribution far above one who admires you.

**Conduct standing.** A clean record, and declarations made honestly. This criterion is rarely the reason an application succeeds and often the reason one fails.

## Claims and the evidence that carries them

| Claim | Evidence that carries it | Evidence that does not |
|---|---|---|
| "Led project controls on major programmes" | Programme names or anonymised values, dates, headcount, what you personally signed | "Extensive senior experience in the sector" |
| "Improved forecasting accuracy" | The measure used, before and after figures, the method changed, who else adopted it | "Introduced best-practice forecasting" |
| "Developed the next generation" | Named individuals who progressed, the scheme you built, hours taught, materials still in use | "Passionate about mentoring" |
| "Contributed to the body of knowledge" | The publication, the standard, the committee minutes, the syllabus you wrote | Conference attendance, memberships held |
| "Led ethically under pressure" | A specific reported position and what it cost, corroborated by a referee | "Committed to the highest standards of integrity" |

The right-hand column is what most applications are made of. It is not dishonest, it is simply unscoreable, and an assessor who cannot score it has to leave the criterion unmet.

## Rewriting a weak paragraph: a worked example

Here is the most common pattern of weak claim, and the version that scores.

**Before.** "I introduced an AI-based early-warning capability that significantly improved our ability to identify at-risk packages, driving a step change in performance."

Nothing there can be verified, and nothing states how good the model was. Now the same contribution, with the arithmetic.

**After.** "I introduced a classifier that flags packages likely to overrun at the next reporting cycle, and defined how the flags are used. Over twelve months it was run across 240 packages, of which 56 overran."

The model flagged 60 packages. Of those, 42 did overrun and 18 did not, and 14 overruns were not flagged.

- Precision = true positives ÷ all flagged = 42 ÷ 60 = **0.70**
- Recall = true positives ÷ all actual overruns = 42 ÷ 56 = **0.75**
- F1 = 2 × (precision × recall) ÷ (precision + recall) = 2 × 0.525 ÷ 1.45 = **0.72**

"Precision of 0.70 means three in ten flagged packages were reviewed unnecessarily, at a cost of one meeting each. Recall of 0.75 means a quarter of overruns still arrived unannounced. I set the threshold deliberately towards recall, because the cost of a missed overrun exceeded the cost of an unnecessary review, and I documented that trade-off in the governance note so that my successor could revisit it."

The second version is longer, and every clause is checkable. It also does the thing panels rate most highly, which is showing judgement about a method's limits rather than enthusiasm for it. The same discipline applied to cost forecasting is set out in [the four EAC formulas](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/four-eac-formulas).

## The five errors that sink applications

**Writing a CV instead of a case.** The panel already has your CV. The case explains what the CV means against each criterion.

**Claiming the team's work as your own.** Senior people are known to other senior people. Say what you did and what the team did, and the whole case becomes more credible, not less.

**Leaving a criterion silent.** An unaddressed criterion is an unmet criterion. If your evidence is thin there, say so and say what you are doing about it. Panels respect a stated gap and distrust an unexplained one.

**Confusing recognition with certification.** Listing an unexamined recognition under qualifications will be noticed, and it damages everything else you claim. The distinction is set out in [what an honorary fellowship in engineering recognises](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/honorary-fellowship-engineering).

**Non-disclosure.** A disciplinary matter you did not declare, found by the panel, ends an application that the matter itself might not have. Declare it, explain it, and let the assessors judge it.

## Referees: how to brief them properly

A weak reference is almost always the applicant's fault. Referees are busy, and an unbriefed one writes generalities.

Send each referee three things: the published criteria, your case, and a short note naming the two or three specific items you would like them to speak to from direct knowledge. Ask them to write about what they saw rather than what they think of you.

Choose for proximity to the work, not seniority. A project director who watched you hold a forecast position against commercial pressure is worth more than a chief executive who knows your name.

Tell them the deadline a week before the real one, and thank them properly afterwards. Most people are asked to be a referee several times by the same person over a career.

## Declarations, conduct and identity

Every credible fellowship process asks you to confirm that what you submitted is true, complete and your own. Treat that as the load-bearing sentence in the whole application.

PCI's published terms for its honorary route state that misrepresentation may lead to refusal or withdrawal of recognition, and that recognition may be withdrawn where it was obtained through inaccurate information. Most institutions say something similar, and they act on it.

On identity documents, a well-designed process asks for the minimum, as late as possible. PCI publishes that identity documents are requested only if the board shortlists you, through a secure, single-use link valid for fourteen days, and that those documents are seen only by the board and deleted after the decision.

That pattern is worth knowing as a consumer test. A body that asks for passport scans at first contact, before any assessment, is collecting data it does not yet need.

## What happens after you submit

Panels sit on a cycle rather than continuously, so a decision typically takes weeks to months. Published review estimates are estimates rather than commitments, and PCI says so explicitly for its own route.

Three outcomes are normal: confirmed, deferred with a named gap, or declined. Deferral is the most common and the most useful, because it tells you exactly what to build.

Discretion is real, and honest bodies say so. PCI's published position on Honorary Fellow (PCI) is that meeting the criteria entitles nobody to recognition, that conferral is at the board's sole discretion, and that an application may be declined without reasons being given.

That is normal governance for a discretionary honour. It is different from an examined credential, where passing the published standard produces the credential every time.

PCI claims no accreditation, recognition, endorsement, affiliation or partnership beyond what is true today, and nothing here is legal, tax or accounting advice.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a fellowship application be?**
As long as the criteria need and no longer. One page per published criterion, with three or four specific evidenced items under each, suits most institutions. Panels read many cases in a sitting, and a tight, well-headed twelve-page submission is read properly while a forty-page narrative is skimmed.

**Do I need published work to become a fellow?**
No, though it helps because it is easy to verify. Contribution can equally be a method others adopted, a training scheme still running, standards or committee service, or a mentoring record with named people who progressed. What every route has in common is that somebody independent can confirm the contribution happened.

**What if my best evidence is confidential?**
Anonymise and quantify. "A £180m water framework" carries the same weight as the client's name, and referees can confirm the detail privately if the panel asks. Never submit material you are not entitled to share; an application that breaches confidence has already failed the conduct criterion it was meant to support.

**Does paying a membership fee improve my chances?**
No, and any body where it does is not worth joining. Membership fees buy membership services. PCI publishes that it charges no nomination, assessment or credential fee for honorary recognition, and that optional charges for printed certificates, delivery or ceremonies do not influence the decision or create any entitlement.

**Should I apply for fellowship or sit a certification first?**
Certification first, in most careers. An examined credential evidences current competence to people who have never met you and is portable across employers and countries. Fellowship evidences accumulated contribution, and the work that builds a strong fellowship case is usually done while holding a credential rather than instead of one.

**Can I reapply after a rejection?**
Yes, and reapplication with a named gap closed succeeds far more often than an immediate resubmission of the same case. Ask for the reason in writing, spend a year building the specific evidence that was missing, and return with new artefacts rather than better adjectives.
