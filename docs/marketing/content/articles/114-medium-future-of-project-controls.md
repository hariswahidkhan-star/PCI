# The future of project controls: four changes that count

The future of project controls is not a new tool set. Four things change: measurement becomes continuous rather than monthly, forecasting becomes a portfolio of methods with a named owner, the boundary with finance closes so controls numbers drive revenue and cash directly, and provenance becomes part of every deliverable.

Almost everything else predicted about the discipline is downstream of those four. This guide takes each in turn, says what it means on a Monday morning, and shows the arithmetic where there is arithmetic worth showing.

## What does the future of project controls change first?

The useful way to read a forecast about a profession is column by column. The middle column is where the effort goes; the right-hand column is what still needs a person at the end of it.

| Function | Today's normal | Where it is going | What does not change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Progress measurement | Monthly, assessed, partly negotiated | Continuous, sensor- and image-derived, reconciled monthly | Someone must agree the earning rule |
| Cost reporting | A monthly pack, two weeks after the data date | A standing position, refreshed daily, with a monthly cut | The cut-off still has to be clean |
| Forecasting | One EAC, one method, rarely stated | Several methods run together, one published with its assumption | The choice of method is a claim about cause |
| Risk | A register reviewed quarterly | Continuous quantification against live cost and schedule data | Ranges are judgement, not output |
| Schedule quality | Manual checks by an experienced planner | Automated rule checking on every update | Whether the logic reflects how the work is built |
| Reporting narrative | Written by hand over three days | Drafted in minutes, edited by the author | Ownership of the number |
| Finance interface | A hand-off at month end | A continuous feed into revenue, WIP and cash | The judgements stay judgements |
| Assurance | Sampled after the event | Provenance attached where the number is made | An auditor still asks who decided |

The middle column has one pattern: the interval between an event and its measurement collapses. The right-hand column has one pattern too: the decisions that were always hard are now the entire job.

## Change one: measurement stops being monthly

The monthly cycle exists because measurement used to be expensive. Sending a quantity surveyor round a site to assess progress cost days, so it happened once a period and every management decision inherited that rhythm.

When progress can be derived from imagery, scans, sensors and system data, the cost of measuring falls close to zero and the interval collapses with it. The monthly report survives as a formal cut rather than as the moment anyone first learns what happened.

The failure mode shows up quickly. Continuous measurement produces continuous small variances, and teams that have not agreed thresholds in advance either chase noise or stop reading the feed altogether. Set the threshold before switching the feed on.

## Change two: forecasting becomes a portfolio with a named owner

Running one estimate at completion by hand is slow, so most projects historically ran one. Running four is now free, which turns the question from "what is the EAC" into "which EAC are we publishing, and why".

The four standard methods disagree by design. Either efficiency to date continues, or the variance was a one-off, or schedule pressure keeps costing money, or a fresh bottom-up re-estimate replaces the lot. On a poorly performing package the spread between them is routinely millions.

That makes the forecaster's job explicit instead of hidden. Publish the method, publish the assumption underneath it, and put a name against it, because the alternative is a number that came out of a tool and belongs to nobody.

## Change three: the finance boundary closes

This is the change that attracts least attention and moves the most money. Project controls has always fed finance; what is different is that the feed is becoming direct enough that a measurement error becomes a reporting error inside the same period.

Look at the cash side, where the discipline's work shows up fastest. The cash conversion cycle for a contracting business is days of unbilled work in progress, plus days of receivables, minus days of payables.

Take a contractor with £180,000,000 of annual revenue and £160,000,000 of cost of sales. Daily revenue is 180,000,000 ÷ 365 = **£493,151**, and daily cost of sales is 160,000,000 ÷ 365 = **£438,356**.

Now the balances. Unbilled work in progress of £22,000,000 is 22,000,000 ÷ 493,151 = **44.6 days**, and receivables of £30,000,000 are **60.8 days**.

Payables of £26,000,000 are measured against cost of sales: 26,000,000 ÷ 438,356 = **59.3 days**.

The cash conversion cycle is 44.6 + 60.8 − 59.3 = **46.1 days**. That is the working capital the business funds before a pound of the work comes back.

Here is the part that belongs to project controls rather than to the finance function. Unbilled work in progress is created by the gap between work done and work valued, and that gap is measurement, documentation and notice — exactly the material a controls team produces.

Close ten days of that gap by measuring earlier and substantiating better, and the business releases roughly 10 × 493,151 = **£4,931,510** of cash, having won no new work and cut no cost. There is no software purchase in that sentence and no financing cost either. [How the cash curve and the funding peak are modelled](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/project-cash-flow-forecasting) shows where those days sit on a single contract.

This is why the future of the discipline is a finance conversation as much as a delivery one. The overlap between the two is where the money sits, and it is precisely the overlap that neither profession is traditionally examined on.

## Change four: provenance becomes a deliverable

When a number is produced by a person, its provenance is the person. When it is produced by a model over data of mixed quality, provenance has to be recorded deliberately or it does not exist.

The working standard is unglamorous: inputs, method, model version, date, reviewer, owner, attached to the output rather than filed somewhere else. A team that does this answers an auditor in a minute; a team that does not reconstructs history under pressure.

Expect this to be demanded contractually before it is demanded by regulation. Clients handed one confident AI-generated forecast that turned out to be wrong tend to ask for the audit trail on the next one.

## The skills that hold their value

Knowing which question a number answers. Physical progress, earned progress and cost-to-cost progress are three different measures that a report will happily label "percent complete".

Statistical literacy at a working level. Enough to ask what a model's error rate is on your own data, and enough to explain why a P80 is not a promise.

The finance vocabulary of a project: work in progress, contract assets and liabilities, cut-off, provisions, revenue over time. Not to do the accountant's job, but to see the consequence of a measurement before it reaches the accounts.

Writing a defensible narrative. When numbers arrive automatically, the scarce skill is the paragraph explaining what moved, why, and what will be done about it. [Keeping the audit trail when a model drafts the commentary](https://pciai.org/generative-ai-project-reporting) is the governance half of the same skill.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will project controls still exist as a separate function?**
Yes, for the reason it was separated in the first place: the person who has to explain an overrun should not also be the person choosing the percentage complete. Automating the measurement does not remove that conflict, it only makes it faster.

**Is the monthly report dead?**
No, but it stops being the first time anyone learns the position. It becomes a formal cut with a clean cut-off, agreed variances and a signed narrative — closer to a set of accounts than to a status update, and rather shorter than it used to be.

**What happens to entry-level controls roles?**
The data-assembly work those roles were built on is going, and it was also how people learned the discipline. Teams that want senior staff in five years will have to buy that learning deliberately, rotating juniors through measurement disputes, forecasting decisions and month-end instead of spreadsheet consolidation.

**Do we need new software to move in this direction?**
Usually less than expected. Most of the value comes from agreeing earning rules, tightening cut-off and closing the gap between work done and work valued. Those are process decisions, and a tool bought before them simply produces the same confusion faster.

**How do we start on the cash side?**
Measure unbilled work in progress in days rather than pounds, then ask what created it: late measurement, missing substantiation, or a notice that was never served. Each of those has a named owner in a controls team and each is fixable within a period.
