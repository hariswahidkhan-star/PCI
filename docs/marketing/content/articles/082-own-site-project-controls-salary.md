# Project controls salary: a free tool and how to use it

A project controls salary is a price for the decisions you are trusted to own, not for the title on your contract or the years on your CV. Work out which of five scope bands you are genuinely in, and the range you should be asking for follows. The ladder and the self-score below take twenty minutes.

Project controls is not one job. Planner, cost engineer, cost controller, estimator, risk analyst and controls lead sit in the same function and are paid on different curves, so the band matters more than the discipline.

## What is a project controls salary actually a price for?

It is a price for risk transferred to you. An employer pays more when your judgement is the thing standing between them and a wrong number in front of a client, a board or an auditor.

That is why two people with the same title and the same decade of experience are paid differently. One produces the report; the other signs the forecast inside it.

Everything in this tool sorts on that single question: if this figure turns out to be wrong, whose name is on it?

## The tool: a five-band scope ladder

Read the middle column, not the title column. Titles are inconsistent between companies; scope is not.

| Band | What you own | What you produce | What an employer accepts as evidence | Titles you may see |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Data | Nothing. You maintain what others define | Updated progress, populated templates, distributed reports | Accuracy and turnaround | Assistant planner, controls administrator, document controller |
| 2 — Analysis | The explanation, not the number | Variance analysis, trend curves, a commentary somebody else signs | A variance you spotted before the client did | Planner, cost engineer, controls analyst |
| 3 — Ownership | The baseline or the forecast for a package or contract | A defended baseline, a forecast with a stated method, a change position | You held a number under pressure and it stood | Senior planner, senior cost engineer, cost controller |
| 4 — Integration | Cost and schedule together, across contracts, into the accounts | One integrated position, thresholds, the accrual, the cash view | The commercial and finance leads call you first | Project controls lead, project controls manager |
| 5 — Function | The method, the assurance and the people | Programme-level reporting, the controls standard, audit-ready evidence | An external party relied on your reporting | Head of project controls, programme controls director |

Most careers stall between band 2 and band 3, and almost every large step in pay happens at the 3-to-4 boundary. That is where the job stops being about producing numbers and starts being about their consequences.

## The ten-question self-score

Score one point for each statement that is true of your current role. Not what you could do. What you are accountable for this month.

1. I have set a baseline that a client or sponsor formally accepted.
2. I choose the forecast method and can say aloud what it assumes.
3. I have refused to publish a number and made the refusal stick.
4. I own the cost breakdown structure and its mapping to the accounts.
5. I write the commentary that goes to the sponsor unedited.
6. I set the reporting thresholds rather than applying somebody else's.
7. I can explain what this month's progress does to revenue and cash.
8. I have run a change or claim position that was tested by the other side.
9. I brief the schedule and the cost position in the same meeting, on the same data.
10. Somebody outside my company has relied on reporting I signed.

Scoring: 0–2 is band 1, 3–4 is band 2, 5–6 is band 3, 7–8 is band 4, 9–10 is band 5.

If you score 6 and are paid at band 2, you have a documented case rather than a grievance. Take the six statements you scored and put a project name against each.

## The step most people are stuck below

The 3-to-4 boundary is crossed by one capability: being able to say what the delivery position does to the money.

A band 3 planner reports that the pipeline test slipped three weeks. A band 4 controls lead reports that the slip moves a certification milestone past the cut-off, which moves revenue recognition into the next period and pushes the receipt into the quarter after that.

Same fact. Two different conversations, and only one of them gets invited back.

## The cash arithmetic that moves you up a band

Cash conversion cycle is the number of days between paying for work and being paid for it. For a contractor it is the clearest single measure of how much working capital the delivery programme is consuming.

The formula is **CCC = DSO + days of work in progress − DPO**.

Take a business with revenue of 210.0m and cost of sales of 189.0m over the year. Receivables stand at 42.0m, unbilled work in progress at 18.9m and payables at 31.5m.

Days sales outstanding: (42.0 ÷ 210.0) × 365 = **73.0 days**.

Days of work in progress: (18.9 ÷ 189.0) × 365 = **36.5 days**.

Days payable outstanding: (31.5 ÷ 189.0) × 365 = **60.8 days**.

Cash conversion cycle: 73.0 + 36.5 − 60.8 = **48.7 days**.

Revenue per day is 210.0 ÷ 365 = **0.575m**. So each day of cycle ties up roughly that much cash, and taking ten days out of the cycle releases in the order of **5.75m** without winning a single new contract.

Now connect it to your day job. Work in progress is unbilled because a milestone was not certified, and milestones are certified against the schedule you maintain.

A planner who moves a certification date by a week has moved cash by about four million in that business. Very few planners have ever been shown that arithmetic, and the ones who have stop being priced as planners.

## What it costs an employer to employ you

Knowing the other side's arithmetic changes how you negotiate.

On a gross salary of 60,000, add employer pension at 6% (3,600), employer social contributions at roughly 12% (7,200), and 3,000 for insurance, equipment and workspace. Employment cost is **73,800**, a multiplier of **1.23** on your gross.

If you sit in a consultancy charged to a client at 95 an hour for 1,600 chargeable hours, you generate **152,000**. Against employment cost of 73,800, that is a recovery of **2.06 times**.

Consultancies target a multiplier in that region to cover non-chargeable time, overhead and margin, which is why utilisation dominates every pay conversation there. A contractor-side role has no such multiplier and prices you against the cost of the alternative, which is why the two markets pay differently for identical work.

Use the right arithmetic for the employer you are talking to. Asking a contractor to pay you a consultancy multiple gets you nowhere.

## What we do not publish

No salary table, and no figure for what a credential does to pay. There is no honest way to isolate that effect without a control group, and any percentage you are shown was produced by a provider with something to sell.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which project controls discipline pays best?**
The band matters more than the discipline. A band 4 cost controller who owns the integrated position out-earns a band 2 planner and a band 2 estimator by a wide margin, and the same is true in reverse. Within a band, the disciplines closest to the money and to contractual exposure tend to sit at the upper end, because the consequence of being wrong is larger.

**Should I move to a contractor, a client or a consultancy?**
Each teaches a different half of the job. Contractors teach production, cost and the reality of records; clients teach assurance, funding and dealing with a board; consultancies teach method and breadth quickly, priced against utilisation. Band 4 is much easier to reach if you have worked at least two of the three, because integration is exactly what one side alone cannot teach.

**Does a certification move me up a band?**
Not on its own. A band is scope, and scope is granted by an employer who believes you can carry it. What examined knowledge does is remove the objection that you have never been tested on the half of the job your degree skipped, which is usually the finance half for engineers and the delivery half for accountants.

**How do I ask for a band 4 salary while doing a band 3 job?**
You do not. You ask for the band 4 scope first, in writing, with the specific accountabilities named, then the pay conversation follows the scope by a review cycle. Asking for the money before the scope invites the answer that you are already paid correctly for what you do, which will be true.

**Are these bands the same everywhere?**
The ladder is stable across markets because it is built on accountability rather than pay. The figures attached to each rung are not, and they move with sector, contract type and local employment cost. Rebuild your own figures using the normalisation worksheet before any negotiation.
