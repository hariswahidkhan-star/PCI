# How to apply for an extension of time in construction

You apply for an extension of time by giving notice inside the period the contract sets, naming the delaying event and the clause that entitles you, then submitting an analysis showing the event's effect on the critical path, supported by contemporaneous records. Time is awarded for delay to the completion date, not to the activity.

Most claims that fail do not fail on the analysis. They fail on the notice, and they fail early.

## How to apply for an extension of time, step by step

**1. Give notice.** Within the stated period, in the stated form, to the stated person. Contracts vary from a few days to a month, and some make notice a condition precedent, meaning entitlement is lost if the notice is late.

**2. Identify the event and the clause.** An extension is granted for a defined category of event — an instructed variation, late information, an employer's act of prevention, exceptional weather where the contract provides for it. Delay with no clause behind it is delay at your own risk.

**3. Fix the programme you will analyse against.** Normally the accepted baseline programme or the last agreed update. Arguing about which programme applies after the analysis is written is a fight you have already lost.

**4. Prove the effect on the critical path.** Show the delay flowing through the logic to the completion date, not simply that an activity started late.

**5. Choose and state your analysis method.** Name it, say why it suits the facts, and set out its assumptions openly. An analysis whose method is unstated reads as a method chosen after the answer.

**6. Attach the records.** Progress records, site diaries, instructions, requests for information, weather data, labour and plant returns, photographs, correspondence.

**7. Submit, then answer the questions.** Expect requests for further particulars, respond within the contractual period, and keep the assessment and its reasoning on file.

## What does the notice actually have to do?

It has to identify the event, state when it occurred or became apparent, and say that an extension will be sought. It does not have to quantify the delay, and waiting until you can quantify it is the usual reason a notice arrives late.

Send it as soon as the event is apparent, even if the effect is unclear. A notice saying "we cannot yet assess the effect and will report by the end of the month" preserves position; silence does not.

Keep notices separate and specific. One letter covering six events over three months invites a response that deals with none of them.

## How do you show the delay hit the critical path?

Float is the test. An event that consumes float delays an activity without delaying the project, and the completion date is what an extension moves.

Take a steel delivery activity with 8 days of total float. A late design release delays it by 22 days.

The net effect on completion is 22 − 8 = **14 days**. That is the claim. Claiming 22 days because the activity moved 22 days is the single most common overreach in a first submission, and it damages the credibility of the rest.

Two conditions have to hold for that arithmetic to survive scrutiny. The 8 days of float must be genuine float in an accepted programme, not float created by missing logic or by an artificially early planned finish. And the path must remain the driving path after the event — compress or delay enough and a different path becomes critical, at which point the answer changes.

Concurrency complicates it further. If the contractor caused 6 days of critical delay in the same window, the treatment of that overlap depends on the contract and the jurisdiction, and it is one of the most contested areas in construction law.

The common outcome is that time and money are treated differently: an award of time with no prolongation cost for the concurrent period. Take advice on the specific contract; nothing published here is legal advice.

## Which delay analysis method should you use?

| Method | What it needs | When it suits | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Impacted as-planned | A reliable baseline programme | Early, prospective assessment before the work is done | Ignores actual progress, so it can produce a delay that never happened |
| Time impact analysis | A contemporaneous programme update at the date of the event | Assessing entitlement as events occur, event by event | Needs disciplined updates; expensive if there are many events |
| As-planned versus as-built windows | Good as-built records across the whole period | Retrospective assessment of a long, multi-event delay | Judgement-heavy; two analysts can reach different windows |
| Collapsed as-built | A defensible as-built programme with logic | Retrospective, where the baseline is weak or was never agreed | Requires as-built logic that is reconstructed, and therefore arguable |

The choice is driven by the records available and by when the assessment is being made, not by which method gives the bigger number. Assessors know the methods, and a prospective method used retrospectively to avoid inconvenient as-built data is visible immediately.

## What records decide the outcome?

The claim is usually won or lost in month three, in the progress records, not in month thirty in the expert's report. By then the question is only what the records will support.

The records that carry weight are the ones created contemporaneously for an operational purpose: daily allocation sheets, the weekly progress report submitted at the time, signed instructions, the RFI register with dates in and out, the delivery log. Documents assembled later for the claim carry very little.

One habit is worth more than any of them. Update the programme every period, on time, whether or not there is a dispute, and keep every update. A contemporaneous set of updates makes a time impact analysis possible; without them, the method is not available.

## Does an extension of time bring money?

No, not automatically, and this is the point most often misunderstood on site. An extension of time changes the contractual completion date and relieves liquidated damages. Money is a separate entitlement under a separate clause, and some events carry time without cost.

Work both numbers on the example. Liquidated damages at £12,000 per day, over 14 days, means the extension relieves 14 × 12,000 = **£168,000** of damages.

Prolongation is different arithmetic. Time-related site overheads — supervision, site accommodation, welfare, standing plant — at £9,500 per day over 14 days come to 14 × 9,500 = **£133,000**, recoverable only if the event carries a money entitlement.

One event, two numbers, two clauses, and either can be granted without the other. A team that assumes an extension carries its prolongation costs plans a cash position that does not arrive.

There is a reporting consequence as well. Prolongation cost that is incurred but not yet certain of recovery increases expected costs, and where progress towards a performance obligation is measured by a cost-based input method, higher expected costs reduce the reported percentage complete. The forecast moves before the claim settles.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long do you have to apply for an extension of time?**
It depends entirely on the contract, and the period is usually short — commonly somewhere between a few days and a month from the event becoming apparent. Some contracts make the notice a condition precedent to entitlement, so a late notice ends the claim regardless of its merits. Read the clause at mobilisation, not at the point of the first delay.

**Can you claim an extension of time after the works are complete?**
Sometimes, if the contract permits a retrospective assessment and notices were given at the time. It is much harder. Records have dispersed, the people involved have moved on, and any analysis has to be reconstructed rather than contemporaneous, which weakens it considerably.

**What is the difference between an extension of time and acceleration?**
An extension moves the completion date later, so the contractor has more time and liquidated damages are relieved. Acceleration keeps or brings forward the date by adding resources or changing sequence, at a cost. Instructed acceleration is normally paid for as a variation. Recovering at your own cost after a client-caused delay, without notice, tends to lose you both the time and the money.

**Does a variation automatically give an extension of time?**
No. A variation gives entitlement only if it actually delays the completion date. A large variation on an activity with substantial float may cost a great deal of money and add no time at all, while a small one on the critical path may add weeks. Cost and time are assessed separately.

**Who assesses the claim?**
Usually the contract administrator, engineer, architect or project manager named in the contract, acting to the standard the contract requires and within a stated period. If they fail to assess in time, the contract normally provides a route — often adjudication or the stated dispute procedure — and that route is worth understanding before you need it.

**What is the most common reason a claim is rejected?**
Failure to demonstrate critical path effect. A submission that shows an activity was late, attaches correspondence, and asks for the same number of days is not an analysis. The assessor needs to see the delay flow through the logic to the completion date, with the float position shown before and after.
