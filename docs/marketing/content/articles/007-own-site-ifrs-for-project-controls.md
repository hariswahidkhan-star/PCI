# IFRS for project controls: what cost engineers must know

IFRS for project controls comes down to five standards. IFRS 15 sets when revenue may be recognised, IAS 37 forces the loss on an onerous contract to be booked in full and immediately, IAS 16 and IAS 23 govern what may be capitalised, and IFRS 16 covers site plant held under lease. Your cost forecast feeds four of them.

Nothing here is accounting advice. It describes, in the Institute's own words, how these standards work so that a project controls professional can understand what their numbers are being used for.

## Which standards does IFRS for project controls actually cover?

| Standard | What it governs | What project controls supplies | Where it bites |
|---|---|---|---|
| IFRS 15 | Revenue from contracts with customers | Cost to date, estimate at completion, progress measurement | Revenue moves whenever the forecast moves |
| IAS 37 | Provisions, onerous contracts, contingent items | Forecast final cost against contract value | An expected loss is booked in full, at once |
| IAS 16 | Property, plant and equipment | Cost breakdown of what is directly attributable | What sits on the balance sheet versus the income statement |
| IAS 23 | Borrowing costs | Construction period, spend profile | Interest capitalised only while the asset is being readied |
| IFRS 16 | Leases | Plant hire, accommodation, long-term equipment | Short hire is cost; a long hire may be an asset and a liability |
| IAS 21 | Effects of foreign exchange rates | Currency of committed purchase orders | Rate movement between commitment and payment |
| IAS 8 | Accounting policies and changes in estimates | Any revision to the estimate at completion | A forecast change is a change in estimate, not an error |

Two of these govern the contractor's side, two the owner's side, and the rest apply to both. Every one of them is fed by numbers a cost engineer produces.

IFRS 18 changes how an income statement is presented for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027. It alters presentation rather than measurement, so the arithmetic below is unaffected.

## The IFRS 15 five-step model, in plain terms

IFRS 15 replaced the old construction-contract approach with a single model built on the promises inside a contract. Described in the Institute's own words, the five steps run as follows.

**Step one: identify the contract.** There must be an agreement with commercial substance, approved by both parties, with identifiable rights, payment terms, and collection that is probable.

**Step two: identify the performance obligations.** These are the distinct promises in the contract. On most construction and EPC work the promise is a single integrated outcome, so the whole contract is one obligation, but a design package sold separately, or a two-year maintenance tail, may well be its own.

**Step three: determine the transaction price.** This is what the entity expects to be entitled to, including variable amounts such as variations, incentives, milestone bonuses and liquidated damages.

**Step four: allocate the price to the obligations.** Where there is more than one, the price is split by relative standalone selling price rather than by whatever the contract schedule happens to say.

**Step five: recognise revenue as each obligation is satisfied.** Either over time, as the work progresses, or at a point in time when control passes.

Step five is the one that pulls project controls into financial reporting. Revenue is recognised over time when the customer receives and consumes the benefit as work is performed, or controls the asset as it is created, or when the asset has no alternative use to the contractor and there is an enforceable right to payment for work completed to date. Most construction contracts meet at least one of those tests.

Which ones qualify, what counts as a single contract, and when a variation becomes revenue are worked through in [the five steps applied to a construction contract](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/ifrs-15-for-construction).

## Measuring progress: input methods and output methods

Once over-time recognition applies, the entity must measure progress. IFRS 15 permits input methods and output methods, and the choice is a policy decision that must be applied consistently.

**Input methods** measure the effort put in. Cost-to-cost is the dominant one: progress equals costs incurred divided by total forecast costs.

**Output methods** measure what has come out. Surveys of work performed, units delivered, milestones achieved, physical progress against a rules-of-credit table.

Cost-to-cost is popular because it is auditable and because the inputs already exist in the cost system. Its weakness is that it treats money spent as work done, which is exactly the assumption earned value management exists to challenge.

There is a specific correction for that weakness. Where a cost is incurred that is not proportionate to progress, such as materials delivered to site but not yet installed, the cost is excluded from the progress calculation and revenue is recognised at cost, with no margin, until the materials are used.

## Worked example: what a forecast movement does to the income statement

Take a fixed-price contract of £100m with an original forecast cost of £80m, so an expected margin of £20m. Costs incurred to date are £40m.

**Position one, forecast £80m.** Progress is 40 / 80 = 50%. Revenue recognised is 50% × £100m = £50m. Cost recognised is £40m. Cumulative margin is £10m.

**Position two, the estimate at completion moves to £90m.** Nothing has been spent that was not spent before. The cost engineer has simply revised the forecast.

Progress is now 40 / 90 = 44.4%. Cumulative revenue becomes 44.4% × £100m = £44.4m. Cost recognised is still £40m, so cumulative margin falls to £4.4m.

That is a £5.6m reduction in profit in a single period, produced by a forecast, not by a transaction. The cross-check works: total expected margin is now £10m, and 44.4% of £10m is £4.4m.

This is the single most useful thing a cost engineer can understand about IFRS. The estimate at completion is not an internal management number. It is a direct input to reported revenue, and a £10m movement on this contract removed £5.6m of profit before anyone had signed a purchase order. The route that revision takes from spreadsheet to ledger is traced in [how a forecast becomes reported profit](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/eac-accounting).

## When the contract turns onerous

**Position three, the estimate at completion moves to £105m.** The contract is now expected to lose £5m over its life. IAS 37 applies to that expectation.

An onerous contract is one where the unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations exceed the benefits expected from it. When a contract becomes onerous, the entire expected loss is recognised immediately, not spread across the remaining work.

Work the arithmetic. Progress is 40 / 105 = 38.1%, so cumulative revenue is £38.1m against costs of £40m, giving a cumulative loss of £1.9m through the normal measurement.

The full expected loss is £5m. The provision required is therefore £5m − £1.9m = £3.1m, recognised in the period the forecast crossed the line.

That is why the month a forecast turns negative is a reporting event rather than a project event. A £15m movement in the estimate at completion, in a business with a 10% net margin, wipes out the profit of a substantial amount of other work in one journal entry.

## Contract assets, contract liabilities and the number people call WIP

Cumulative revenue recognised almost never equals cumulative amounts invoiced, and the difference has a name.

When cumulative revenue exceeds cumulative billings, the entity holds a **contract asset**: work performed for which it has not yet earned an unconditional right to payment. Take position two above, revenue of £44.4m against billings of £38m, and the contract asset is £6.4m.

When cumulative billings exceed cumulative revenue, the entity holds a **contract liability**: cash or a receivable taken for work not yet delivered. Same revenue of £44.4m against billings of £52m gives a contract liability of £7.6m.

The commercial reading is more interesting than the accounting one. A large contract liability means front-loaded billing, which is good for cash and means the reported margin is being funded by the client's money. A large contract asset means the opposite, and is usually a signal that applications for payment are being disputed or that the schedule of values no longer matches the work.

An unconditional right to payment makes it a receivable instead. That distinction is why a certified application and an uncertified one sit in different places on the balance sheet.

## Variations and claims are not revenue until the constraint is passed

Step three of the model includes variable consideration, and step three also constrains it. Variable amounts are included in the transaction price only to the extent that it is highly probable a significant reversal of cumulative revenue will not occur later.

The practical effect is blunt. A £4m claim sitting in the cost report as an expected recovery is not £4m of revenue, and a contractor who books it before the constraint is satisfied is creating a reversal for a future period.

The evidence that moves a claim across that line is contemporaneous: correspondence, an instruction, a substantiated entitlement, a record of the delay or disruption as it happened. That evidence is produced by project controls, not by finance.

Contract modifications have their own treatment. A variation that adds distinct scope at its standalone selling price is accounted for as a separate contract. One that does not, which is most of them, is folded into the existing contract with a cumulative catch-up adjustment on the revenue already recognised.

## Capitalisation, borrowing costs and leases

On the owner's side of the table, the question changes from when revenue may be recognised to what may be added to the asset.

IAS 16 allows the purchase price plus costs directly attributable to bringing the asset to the location and condition needed for its intended use. Site preparation, installation, testing and professional fees attributable to the work qualify. General administrative overhead, training and the cost of an idle period do not.

IAS 23 requires borrowing costs directly attributable to acquiring or constructing a qualifying asset to be capitalised while that asset is being readied. Capitalisation is suspended during extended periods of suspended activity and stops when the asset is substantially ready.

Depreciation begins when the asset is available for use, which is not the same date as practical completion or as the ribbon-cutting. A three-month commissioning delay is therefore both a schedule event and a depreciation event.

IFRS 16 brings most leases onto the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. A tower crane on a 30-month hire is treated very differently from the same crane taken for three weeks, and that decision needs the schedule to be right.

## Why this is the overlap where the money is lost

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy. They are almost never examined on a critical path or an earning rule.

An engineer is examined on float and progress measurement. They are almost never examined on cut-off or a contract asset.

A project lives in the overlap. The estimate at completion is produced by one profession and consumed, unaltered, by the other, and neither is trained to interrogate what the other did with it.

The failure pattern follows from that. The forecast is optimistic for three quarters because nobody wants to be the person who moved it, then it moves £15m in one month and the loss is recognised in full under IAS 37 in that same month. The engineering event happened over nine months. The financial event happened in one.

That gap is the reason [a credential that examines both sides of the handover](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/finance-and-project-management-certification) exists at all. ## What a project controls professional should be able to produce

Four things, without asking finance for help.

An estimate at completion they can defend line by line, with the basis of each movement written down. The percentage complete that estimate implies under cost-to-cost, and the revenue that follows from it.

The value of any uninstalled materials sitting inside costs incurred. The date on which the forecast final cost first exceeded the contract value.

Produce those four and the month-end conversation changes from a reconciliation into a decision.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does IFRS 15 still allow percentage of completion?**
Effectively yes, but not by that name and not as a free choice. Where an obligation is satisfied over time, progress must be measured by an input or output method, and cost-to-cost is the input method that most closely resembles the old percentage of completion. The change is that the entity must first prove over-time recognition applies.

**Why does my revenue fall when I increase the estimate at completion?**
Because cost-to-cost progress is costs incurred divided by total forecast costs, and increasing the denominator reduces the percentage. On the worked example above, moving the forecast from £80m to £90m cut progress from 50% to 44.4% and reversed £5.6m of revenue that had already been reported. No cash moved.

**What makes a contract onerous?**
The unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations exceed the economic benefits expected from it. In project terms, the forecast final cost exceeds the forecast final value, including any variable consideration that has passed the constraint. Once that is true, IAS 37 requires the whole expected loss to be recognised immediately rather than as it is incurred.

**Can we include a pending claim in the forecast revenue?**
Only to the extent it is highly probable that a significant reversal will not occur. That is a high bar, and an optimistic entitlement view does not clear it. The claim can and should sit in the commercial forecast used to manage the job, while the recognised transaction price stays at the constrained figure until the evidence supports more.

**Is this different under US GAAP?**
The revenue model is substantially converged, since IFRS 15 and ASC 606 were developed jointly. Differences remain elsewhere, including aspects of onerous contract treatment and the capitalisation of borrowing costs. Any group reporting under both should confirm its own policy rather than assuming the two answers match.

**Who owns the number in practice?**
Finance owns the entry, project controls owns the estimate behind it, and the disagreement usually starts because nobody wrote that down. A short, signed forecast basis stating scope, quantities, rates, risk allowance and the date of the underlying data resolves most of it before it starts.
