# Cost controller certification: what it covers, who it suits

A cost controller certification examines the monthly cycle the role actually runs: baseline, commitment, progress, accrual, forecast, change, cash and report. It suits people who own a cost report and have to defend it at cut-off. It is narrower and more operational than a cost engineering credential, and that is the point.

The test of any such certification is whether it examines all eight steps or only the four that are easy to write questions about.

## What does a cost controller do that a cost engineer does not?

A cost controller is accountable for the truth of the current project's numbers at a fixed date each month. A cost engineer is accountable for the estimate and the forecast across the asset's life.

The distinction matters because the roles fail differently. A cost engineer fails by mis-estimating. A cost controller fails by reporting a position that the ledger later contradicts.

| Role | Primary artefact | Time horizon | The question they answer | Where they sit at month-end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cost controller | The monthly cost report | This period, this project | What is the position, and is it true? | Reconciling the report to the ledger |
| Cost engineer | The estimate and the forecast | Sanction to handover, and beyond | What will this finish at, and why? | Defending the estimate to complete |
| Quantity surveyor | The valuation and the account | Contract to final account | What are we owed, and what do we owe? | Certifying and agreeing measurement |
| Project accountant | The ledger and the disclosure | Statutory reporting period | What may be recognised, and when? | Closing, accruing and testing cut-off |

Most organisations blur two of these into one job title. That is workable. What is not workable is nobody owning the reconciliation between them.

## The eight-step monthly cycle a certification should examine

**One, baseline integrity.** Every pound of budget mapped to a control account with an owner, no unallocated balance parked in a suspense line, and change applied to the baseline only through approved change control.

**Two, commitment capture.** Order value recorded against the budget it draws on, on the day the order is placed. An order above its budget is a variance immediately, not when the invoice lands.

**Three, progress under an earning rule.** The rule is published before the work starts, applies to every package, and never changes mid-project. Progress claimed by opinion is not progress.

**Four, accrual and cut-off.** Work performed and not invoiced comes in; anything invoiced in the period that belongs to the next comes out. This is where the report is usually wrong.

**Five, forecast.** Four methods, chosen by cause: remaining work at budget, performance continues, cost-and-schedule pressure, or a fresh bottom-up estimate to complete. The choice needs a stated reason.

**Six, change and trend.** Potential change logged when it is likely, not when it is approved, with a value range and an owner.

**Seven, cash.** Certification dates, payment terms and retention modelled, because a project can be profitable and still run out of money.

**Eight, report and escalation.** Thresholds with a percentage and an absolute limb, a named owner per breach and an action date.

## Worked: why step four decides whether the report is true

A package reports actual cost of £7.90m against earned value of £8.20m, so CPI reads 8.20 / 7.90 = **1.038** and the package looks 3.8% under.

Three cut-off items then surface: £1.05m of subcontract work performed and not yet invoiced, £0.30m invoiced in the period for plant delivered after cut-off, and £0.12m of insurance prepaid for the next period.

Corrected AC = 7.90 + 1.05 − 0.30 − 0.12 = **£8.53m**. Corrected CPI = 8.20 / 8.53 = **0.961**.

The package moved from 3.8% under to 3.9% over on accrual discipline alone, with no new work and no new transaction. A certification that never asks a candidate to do that correction is examining arithmetic, not the job.

## Why does a cost controller need to know IFRS 15?

Because the forecast produced at step five is usually the denominator of the revenue calculation, so moving it moves reported profit.

IFRS 15 sets out a five-step model for revenue from contracts with customers. In PCI's own words, and without reproducing the standard:

**Step one, identify the contract.** An agreement with enforceable rights and obligations, defined payment terms, and a real prospect of collection.

**Step two, identify the performance obligations.** The distinct promises inside it, which on a construction contract is often one integrated service rather than the packages in your work breakdown structure.

**Step three, determine the transaction price.** Including variable amounts such as incentives, liquidated damages and unapproved variations, constrained to the amount that is unlikely to reverse later.

**Step four, allocate the price** to those obligations, normally in proportion to their standalone selling prices.

**Step five, recognise revenue as each obligation is satisfied.** Where the criteria for transfer over time are met, progress is measured by an input or an output measure, and the common input measure is cost incurred over total forecast cost.

That last clause is the cost controller's stake in an accounting standard. The standard is the authority and nothing here is accounting advice, but the number in the denominator is yours.

**The arithmetic.** A contract priced at £48.0m, forecast cost £40.0m, cost incurred £18.0m. Progress = 18.0 / 40.0 = **45.00%**. Cumulative revenue = 0.45 × 48.0 = **£21.60m**, so cumulative margin is **£3.60m**.

The forecast then moves to £43.0m. Progress = 18.0 / 43.0 = **41.86%**. Cumulative revenue = **£20.09m**, and cumulative margin falls to **£2.09m**. The cross-check: expected margin is now 48.0 − 43.0 = £5.0m, and 41.86% of £5.0m is £2.09m.

A £3.0m movement in a cost forecast removed **£1.51m** of margin already reported, in the period the forecast moved. Nobody spent anything.

## What does the role do for cash?

The cash conversion cycle measures how long money is tied up between paying for work and being paid for it: days sales outstanding, plus days of unbilled work in progress, minus days payable outstanding.

Take DSO of 68 days, 24 days of work performed but not yet certified, and DPO of 41 days. The cycle is 68 + 24 − 41 = **51 days**.

On £40m of annual turnover, one day is 40,000,000 / 365 = **£109,589**. Fifty-one days is **£5.59m** of working capital funded by somebody.

Cut the certification lag by nine days and 9 × 109,589 = **£986,301** is released. That is a one-off release of working capital, not profit, and a cost controller who describes it as profit will be corrected once and remembered for it.

## Who does this suit, and who should sit something else?

It suits a cost controller or cost analyst with a report of their own, two years in, who keeps being asked questions that begin "but what does finance think".

It suits a project accountant moving towards projects who can close a ledger but has never tested an earning rule.

It suits nobody who wants a scheduling credential. If your work is network logic, delay and float, sit a scheduling examination instead, and read [schedule risk analysis](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/schedule-risk-analysis) before you choose.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a cost controller certification different from a cost engineer certification?**
Yes, in scope rather than difficulty. The cost controller examination should be dominated by the monthly cycle, cut-off and reporting. The cost engineer examination should be dominated by estimating, forecasting technique and claims. The routes are compared side by side in [cost engineer certification](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/cost-engineer-certification).

**How much accounting does a cost controller really need?**
Enough to test their own report before finance does: accruals and prepayments, cut-off, the difference between a commitment and a cost, how a forecast drives revenue under a cost-to-cost measure, and when margin erosion becomes a provision. Not enough to prepare statutory accounts, which is a different profession.

**Can I do the job without a certification?**
Plenty of people do. What certification changes is portability and the speed of trust: a stranger reading your CV learns that an independent body examined you against a published standard, rather than that an employer kept you. That is worth most at the point you change employer or country.

**Does the role disappear when reporting is automated?**
The reporting does. The judgement does not: which earning rule applies, whether an accrual belongs in this period, which forecasting method matches the cause of the variance. Automation moves the role from producing the report to defending it, which raises the standard rather than removing it.

**How long does preparation take?**
Budget by gap, not by hours. Someone who runs a monthly cycle already and needs the accounting half will spend most of their preparation on cut-off, revenue and provisions. Someone from finance will spend it on earning rules and forecasting. Work through the [earned value practice questions](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/earned-value-practice-questions) first to find out which of the two you are.
