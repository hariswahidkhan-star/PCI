# Fellowship for project professionals: routes and evidence

Fellowship for project professionals comes in three distinct forms: an elected fellow grade in a membership body, an honorary fellowship conferred by a board, and an examined credential that tests competence rather than contribution. Only the third proves what you can do. The first two recognise what you have given, and neither is interchangeable with a qualification.

Getting the three straight is not pedantry. It decides what you may write on a tender, a CV and an expert statement.

## Fellowship for project professionals: the three routes compared

| Route | How it is conferred | Examination | What it evidences | What it must never be claimed as | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fellow membership grade | Nomination, assessed against published criteria | No | Sustained contribution above competent practice | Proof of technical competence | A membership fee |
| Honorary fellowship | Board or council decision, at its discretion | No | Contribution the body chose to mark publicly | A qualification, licence or accreditation | Usually nothing |
| Examined credential | Passing a published examination | Yes | Assessed knowledge against a syllabus, on a date | A statement about your whole career | An examination fee |

The middle column is the one people misread. Two of the three involve no examination, so neither can carry a competence claim, however senior the holder.

The routes are sequential in practice rather than alternative. The work that earns a nomination is usually done by someone who already holds a credential and was trusted with scope because of it.

## What evidence a project professional actually holds

Most people in this discipline hold stronger fellowship evidence than they realise, in places they do not think to look.

**Baselines owned.** Named programmes, contract values, the change control you ran, the dates you signed. This is the easiest criterion to evidence and the one most applications lean on too heavily.

**Forecasts defended.** A position you took against pressure, the method behind it, and what happened next. Being proved wrong having documented the assumption is still evidence; being right by accident is not.

**Methods others adopted.** The reporting pack that became the template, the earning-rule policy that survived you leaving, the estimating basis another region copied.

**People developed.** Named individuals who progressed, the scheme you built, the material still being taught from.

**Work published.** Papers, standards contributions, committee service, conference material that others cite or reuse.

Write these down before you read any criteria; mapping from a full list is far easier than from a blank page. The method is in [how to become a fellow of a professional institution](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/how-to-become-a-fellow-of-a-professional-institution).

## The evidence that carries most weight

Contribution that sat across the boundary between finance and delivery, because almost nobody can do it and every programme needs it.

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, almost never on a critical path or an earning rule. An engineer is examined on float and progress measurement, almost never on cut-off or a contract asset. A project lives in that overlap, and the overlap is where the money goes missing.

Here is what that looks like as evidence, with the arithmetic.

### A worked cross-boundary case

Under IFRS 15, revenue from a customer contract is recognised through five steps, in PCI's own words: identify the contract; identify the distinct promises in it; determine the transaction price, including variable amounts and the constraint on them; allocate that price across the promises; recognise revenue as each promise is satisfied, over time or at a point in time.

Nothing here is accounting advice, and the standard itself governs.

Take a £48m design-and-build contract with a single performance obligation satisfied over time, measured by an input method: costs incurred against total expected costs.

Total expected costs are £40m. Costs incurred to date are £18m, so measured progress is 18 ÷ 40 = **45%**.

There is also a £2m completion bonus. It is variable consideration, and the judgement is whether including it risks a significant reversal later. Judged constrained, it stays out, so the transaction price is £48m and revenue to date is 0.45 × 48 = **£21.6m**.

Certified and billed to date is £19.4m, so the balance sheet carries a contract asset of 21.6 − 19.4 = **£2.2m** of revenue recognised ahead of billing.

Now the delivery side of the same number. £6m of the £18m incurred is materials delivered to site but not installed: the cost has been spent and no work has been done with it, so counting it as progress overstates what has been earned.

Take those materials out of both sides of the measure. Progress on installed work is 12 ÷ 34 = **35.3%**, applied to the price net of those materials, 48 − 6 = £42m, giving 0.353 × 42 = **£14.82m**, plus the £6m of materials recognised at cost with no margin. Revenue to date becomes **£20.82m**.

So the adjustment moves **£0.78m** of revenue out of the period and cuts the contract asset from £2.2m to £1.42m, a fall of more than a third. Anyone who resolved that argument, wrote the policy that stopped it recurring, and can explain it to an auditor and a project director has a fellowship case that no single-sided career can match.

The same logic drives the earned value number, since uninstalled materials inflate progress there too. The mechanics are covered in [earned value management explained](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/earned-value-management).

## Where PCI's routes sit today

PCI publishes two recognitions and three examined credentials, and keeps the categories separate deliberately.

**The Fellow membership grade** is the institute's most senior grade, awarded by nomination for a sustained record of contribution, carrying the FPCI post-nominal. PCI's published position is that the criteria and process are in development and will be published in full before any nominations open, so nobody is assessed against rules they could not read.

PCI publishes the individual membership fee as USD 99 for a three-year term, currently USD 49.50 with a 50% discount, at the same flat rate across the Student, Associate, Professional and Fellow grades. Check the current page before relying on it.

**Honorary Fellow (PCI)** is a board-conferred recognition of distinguished contribution, involving no examination and separate from the examined credentials. Published eligibility is at least eight years in project controls, cost control, finance, project management or a closely related field, of which at least three at managerial level, plus a demonstrable record of contribution.

PCI charges no nomination, assessment or credential fee for honorary recognition, and states that meeting the criteria entitles nobody to it. The detail is in [what an honorary fellowship in engineering recognises](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/honorary-fellowship-engineering).

PCI is explicit that neither is a qualification. A fellow who has not earned a PCI credential holds no certification, and a fellow who has must keep it current like everyone else.

## What to do this year

**If you are under ten years in.** Ignore fellowship. Take the examined credential that closes your evidenced gap, and keep a contribution file: methods you wrote, people you taught, positions you held. Ten minutes a month, and it is the raw material of a case later.

**If you are senior and have never applied.** Read the criteria of the one body that genuinely covers your work, and map your record to it one criterion at a time. Most people in this position find they have been eligible for years.

**If you are close but thin on contribution.** Pick one thing that will outlast a project, such as a standard, a training scheme or a committee seat, and do it properly for eighteen months. That is usually the whole gap.

**If someone offers you a fellowship for a fee.** Decline. Recognition that can be purchased evidences nothing, and listing it damages the claims around it. What separates credentials worth holding is covered in [what a project controls certification actually proves](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/project-controls-certification).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is fellowship worth it for a project professional?**
It is worth having and rarely worth chasing early. Fellowship signals standing to peers, clients and expert-witness instructing parties, and it opens committee and standards work. It does not evidence competence, will not substitute for a credential in a tender requiring one, and is best treated as a consequence of contribution rather than a target.

**Can I hold a fellowship and a certification at the same time?**
Yes, and that is the normal combination for senior practitioners. They answer different questions: the credential says you were assessed against a published syllabus, the fellowship says your contribution was recognised. List them separately on a CV, under qualifications and under honours respectively, and never merge the two into one line.

**Which comes first, the credential or the fellowship?**
The credential, for almost everyone. It is portable, verifiable by strangers, and it is what employers and clients filter on. Fellowship rests on years of contribution that are usually accumulated while working at the level a credential helps you reach in the first place.

**How do people verify a fellowship I hold?**
Through the conferring body's own register or verification page, which is why bodies that publish one are worth preferring. Give the exact designation as the body publishes it, and a link to where it can be checked. A recognition nobody can verify does very little work on your behalf.
