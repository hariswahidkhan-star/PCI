# How to get into project controls with no experience

You get into project controls with no experience by producing evidence instead of applications. Build one small baselined project, run it forward for three periods with real progress rules, and show the arithmetic. Then apply into an adjacent role where your existing sector knowledge already counts. Certification helps after you can do the arithmetic, not before.

Written for LinkedIn as an original. It sits under the Institute's certification and careers pillar.

## What does "no experience" actually block?

It blocks one thing: the employer cannot tell whether you will accept a progress claim that is wrong.

Everything else about the role can be taught in weeks. Reading a network, coding a cost, building a curve and writing a variance commentary are all learnable and all documented.

Judgement about whether a number is true is the part that normally comes from watching work get installed, and it is the part a hiring manager is worried about. Your job as an applicant is to remove that worry with something they can inspect.

## Who hires into project controls with no experience?

Employers who have a reporting backlog and a defined method. That means large contractors, programme delivery bodies, owner client teams and consultancies with a controls standard someone else already wrote.

It rarely means a small firm with one controls person, because there is nobody there to check you. It also rarely means a role advertised as senior, whatever the salary band suggests about desperation.

The realistic entry points are assistant planner, project controls analyst, cost assistant, document controller in a controls team, and graduate schemes at contractors and owner organisations.

## The five entry routes, compared honestly

| Route | Typical time to first role | What it costs you | What it proves | Where it stalls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Graduate scheme | One recruitment cycle | Requires a relevant degree and a specific window | That an employer will train you from zero | Closed to career changers and to anyone past two years out |
| Adjacent-role conversion (site engineer, QS, planner's assistant, project accountant) | 6–18 months inside a current employer | Time in a role you may not want long term | Sector knowledge, which is the hardest half | Stalls if the controls team never lets you own an output |
| Data or reporting role first | 3–9 months | You learn tools before you learn the work | That you can build the reporting layer | People get typed as the dashboard person and stay there |
| Contract or agency work on a delivery programme | Fast, sometimes weeks | Insecure, and the work is often data entry | That you can operate to a cut-off | Repetition without method exposure teaches little |
| Certification-first | Weeks to months of study | Fees and study time, with no role attached | Documented knowledge a stranger can check | On its own it does not answer the judgement question |

The two strongest routes are conversion and a hybrid: convert from an adjacent role, and use a credential to prove the parts your current job never showed.

Nothing on that list is a guarantee of a role, and anyone selling one is selling something else.

## What to learn, and in what order

Start with the work breakdown structure, because every other artefact hangs off it. If the WBS is wrong, the schedule, the cost accounts and the reporting are all wrong in the same way.

Then learn progress measurement, which is where beginners are weakest and employers are most exposed. Rules of credit, quantity-based progress and the difference between claimed, installed and accepted are worth more than any software certificate.

Then scheduling logic: predecessors, float, calendars and constraints. Then cost coding and commitment tracking. Then earned value, which is only meaningful once the four above are sound.

Software comes last and takes least time. Learning Primavera P6 or Microsoft Project before you understand progress measurement produces someone who can update a file and cannot tell when it is lying.

## The portfolio piece: one small project, worked

Invent a plausible small project, document it as if it were real, and run it for three periods. Do not invent a client, a testimonial or a real company; make it clearly a worked example.

### The network

Four activities. A, site set-up, 5 days. B, foundations, 8 days, after A. C, temporary power, 3 days, after A. D, frame erection, 4 days, after both B and C.

**Forward pass.** A runs day 0 to 5. B runs 5 to 13. C runs 5 to 8. D waits for both, so D runs 13 to 17.

**Backward pass.** Finish is day 17, so D must start by 13. B must finish by 13, late start 5. C must finish by 13, late start 10.

**Total float** is late start minus early start. A = 0, B = 0, C = 10 − 5 = **5 days**, D = 0.

The critical path is A, B, D at **17 days**. Temporary power has five days of float, which is exactly the sort of thing a site manager will not believe until you show the two passes.

### The cost position

Five work packages, budget at completion **£1,300,000**: foundations £200,000, steel £300,000, cladding £250,000, mechanical and electrical £400,000, commissioning £150,000.

At the data date, progress by rules of credit is foundations 100%, steel 60%, cladding 20%, M&E 10%, commissioning 0%.

**Earned value** = (200,000 × 1.00) + (300,000 × 0.60) + (250,000 × 0.20) + (400,000 × 0.10) + 0
= 200,000 + 180,000 + 50,000 + 40,000 = **£470,000**.

Planned value at that date is **£520,000** and actual cost is **£505,000**.

**SPI** = 470,000 ÷ 520,000 = **0.90**. **CPI** = 470,000 ÷ 505,000 = **0.93**.
**EAC at current performance** = BAC ÷ CPI = 1,300,000 ÷ 0.9307 = **£1,396,800**.

You are 10% behind in money-weighted progress and running about 7% over on what you have bought. On this data the job lands roughly £97,000 above budget unless something changes.

### The part that gets you hired

Write 200 words explaining why the M&E percentage is the weakest number in the table, and what you would ask to firm it up. A 10% claim on a £400,000 package that is mostly procurement is the classic place where progress is really payment.

Anyone can produce the arithmetic. Being able to name your own weakest input is the judgement signal, and it is rare in candidates at any level.

## Which adjacent experience converts fastest?

Site engineering converts fastest, because you already know what installed looks like. Quantity surveying converts nearly as fast, since measurement and change are half the cost picture.

Project accounting is undervalued as a route and converts well. If you have run accruals, commitments and cost coding, you already understand the ledger side that most planners never see.

That crossover is worth naming plainly. An accountant is examined on recognition and provisions, an engineer on float and progress, and a capital project loses money in the gap between the two. Anyone who arrives holding one side and learns the other is unusually employable.

## Where does certification fit?

After the arithmetic, not before it. A credential answers a stranger's question about what you know; it does not answer the question about whether you will hold a number under pressure.

Used in the right order it works well: build the worked example, learn the method properly, then take an examination that tests both the finance and the delivery side so the document says something a tool certificate does not. ## Frequently asked questions

**Can you get in with no degree as well as no track record?**
Yes, and it happens regularly through the trades and site-supervision route, particularly in construction and shutdown work. What replaces the degree is demonstrable site knowledge plus documented method study. The route is slower into owner client teams, which often filter on qualifications at application stage, and faster into contractors, who filter on whether you can run a cut-off.

**How long does it take to become useful?**
Roughly one full reporting cycle to be useful and three to four to be trusted, assuming you are on a project with a defined method. Useful means producing an accurate update without supervision. Trusted means someone will publish your variance commentary without rewriting it, which is a much later milestone.

**Should I learn Primavera P6 or Excel first?**
Excel, without much doubt. Most controls work still passes through a spreadsheet at some point, and the reasoning skills transfer directly. Learn P6 once you know what a progress claim should satisfy, because the software will happily let you build a schedule that is internally valid and factually untrue.

**Do I need to move to a construction site?**
Not permanently, but time near the work pays for itself. Controls people who have never seen the installation sequence tend to accept optimistic progress and miss the physical constraints that create half the schedule logic. Even a few weeks of site attachment changes how you read a progress claim.

**Is a free online course enough to get an interview?**
It can get you past a keyword filter and rarely past a technical interviewer. What gets you the interview is the worked example: a network with the two passes shown, a cost position with the earned value arithmetic, and a written note naming your weakest input. That artefact is unusual enough to be remembered.
