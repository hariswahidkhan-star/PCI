# PMO certification: what it covers and where it stops

A PMO certification examines how a project management office is set up and run: governance, portfolio prioritisation, methodology, assurance and reporting. It stops at the arithmetic. Most syllabuses do not examine earned value calculation in depth, forecast defence, revenue recognition or schedule risk mathematics — which is where the numbers a PMO reports are actually made.

That is a scope statement, not a criticism. The gap only matters when someone assumes the certificate covers it.

## What does a PMO certification examine?

Broadly five areas, and they are consistent across the main syllabuses.

Governance and operating model: what the PMO is for, who it reports to, and what decisions it supports. Portfolio management: intake, prioritisation, capacity and stage-gate discipline. Methodology and standards: the templates, lifecycles and tailoring rules the organisation runs on.

Then reporting and information management: dashboards, data quality, escalation thresholds. And assurance: health checks, reviews, and independent challenge of project reporting.

Several bodies publish credentials in this space. AXELOS publishes P3O, covering portfolio, programme and project office design. PMI publishes portfolio and programme credentials alongside the PMP.

The Association for Project Management publishes qualifications covering the same governance territory. Naming them is a statement of what exists, not an endorsement, an affiliation or a comparison of quality.

## The three PMO types, compared

Most syllabuses classify PMOs by how much authority they hold. The distinction decides what a certification is useful for, because the three types need different skills.

| Type | Authority | What it does | What it needs from its people |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supportive | Low — advisory | Templates, training, lessons, tool administration, a repository of good practice | Facilitation, methodology, patience |
| Controlling | Medium — compliance | Sets standards and checks conformance; reviews plans and reports before they go up | Assurance skill, and enough technical depth to challenge a number |
| Directive | High — delivery | Manages the projects directly; project managers report into the PMO | Delivery management and portfolio-level resource judgement |

A supportive PMO with a controlling mandate produces friction and no assurance. A controlling PMO whose staff cannot check the arithmetic behind a report produces the appearance of assurance, which is worse than none, because it lets the sponsor believe the number has been tested.

## Where a PMO certification stops

Five things a PMO syllabus generally does not examine to any depth.

Earned value calculation and its failure modes, beyond definitions. Estimate at completion, including which of the four standard methods applies and what each assumes. Schedule network analysis: float, driving path, constraints and out-of-sequence progress.

Quantified risk analysis and how contingency is derived from it. And the accounting consequences of a project number, from capitalisation cut-off to how a progress measure feeds revenue recognised over time.

A PMO can run without those skills if the projects supply them. Most PMOs assume they are being supplied and never check.

## The £2m that hangs on one measurement rule

Here is the gap made concrete. A PMO receives a monthly return: the package is 62 per cent complete, £4.3m has been spent, the budget at completion is £8.0m.

Read as earned value, that looks good. Earned value is 0.62 × 8,000,000 = £4,960,000. Cost performance index is EV ÷ AC = 4,960,000 ÷ 4,300,000 = **1.153**. Estimate at completion is BAC ÷ CPI = 8,000,000 ÷ 1.153 = **£6.94m**, an underrun of about £1.06m. The dashboard turns green.

Now ask where the 62 per cent came from. If it is a package manager's judgement rather than rules of credit applied to measurable quantities, it can be wrong by a lot. Measured properly — steel erected against tonnes planned, cable pulled against metres planned — the same package earns 48 per cent.

Earned value becomes 0.48 × 8,000,000 = £3,840,000. CPI is 3,840,000 ÷ 4,300,000 = **0.893**. EAC is 8,000,000 ÷ 0.893 = **£8.96m**, an overrun of about £0.96m.

The forecast moved from £6.94m to £8.96m — a swing of roughly **£2.0m** — with no change in the money spent and no change in the work done. The only thing that changed was the rule used to measure progress.

A PMO that cannot ask where the percentage came from will report the first number. That question is not on most PMO syllabuses.

## PMO certification against the alternatives

| | PMO certification | Project controls certification | Accounting qualification | PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Examines | Governance, portfolio, methodology, assurance, reporting | Estimating, scheduling, earned value, cost control, risk | Recognition, measurement, disclosure, audit | Delivery and governance together with the finance and reporting consequences |
| Assumes you already know | How the underlying numbers are produced | The organisational context the numbers land in | The operational detail behind the transaction | Neither side is assumed |
| Cannot tell you | Whether a reported forecast is credible | Whether the portfolio should have funded this at all | How progress was measured | — |
| Suits | PMO leads, portfolio analysts, assurance roles | Planners, cost engineers, controls leads | Finance business partners on capital work | Delivery leaders accountable for the reported position |

The pattern in that table is the point. Each of the first three is a competent answer to part of the problem, and each assumes somebody else has covered the rest.

## Does a PMO certification make you a project controls professional?

No, and treating it as one is a common and expensive career mistake.

The two disciplines share vocabulary and diverge on method. A PMO lead can define cost performance index and explain its use in reporting. Producing a defensible one requires knowing how the earned value was measured, whether accruals are in the actual cost, whether the cut-off is clean, and what the commitment position is.

The reverse also holds. A strong cost engineer is not automatically ready to run a PMO, because portfolio prioritisation, stakeholder governance and methodology design are genuine skills that the technical route never teaches.

Anyone moving between the two should expect to study, not to translate.

## What to check before paying for any certification

Six questions, and they apply to every credential including this Institute's.

Is there a published Body of Knowledge, and can you read its structure before you pay? Is there a real examination, or is attendance the qualification? Is the syllabus versioned and dated, so you can tell what changed?

Who owns and maintains the standard, and what happens if they stop? What does recertification require, and how much does maintaining it cost over five years? And what does the credential explicitly say it does *not* cover?

The last question is the most revealing. A credential whose marketing implies it covers everything is telling you something about the care taken with the rest of it.

## What PCI examines instead

The PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI) examines 16 domains across 63 knowledge areas, covering delivery, governance and the reporting consequences of both in one syllabus.

The Body of Knowledge runs in a 40 / 40 / 20 proportion across finance and reporting, project management, and governed AI. The 40 per cent devoted to finance and reporting is the deliberate answer to the £2m example above: the person reading the return should be able to interrogate how the earned value was measured and what it does to the reported position.

The calculation content is checked mechanically. Across PFL-AI and PML-AI, 15,613 machine calculation checks run over the worked material and all of them pass; PCL-AI has no equivalent suite, so the figure covers those two credentials only.

Certification requirements published by the Institute are certification requirements, not law, and nothing PCI publishes is legal, tax or accounting advice.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a PMO certification worth it?**
It is worth it if you work in or lead a PMO and want the governance, portfolio and assurance material organised properly. It is poor value if you expect it to teach the technical content behind project reporting, because it does not claim to and does not. Match the credential to the job you are actually doing, and be honest about which of the two problems you have.

**What is the difference between a PMO certification and a project management certification?**
A project management certification examines running a project: scope, plan, team, contract, delivery. A PMO certification examines the function that supports and governs many projects: intake, prioritisation, standards, assurance and reporting. They overlap in vocabulary and diverge in accountability, and holding one tells an employer very little about your competence in the other.

**Do I need project management experience before a PMO qualification?**
It helps considerably, and in a controlling or directive PMO it is close to essential. Assurance depends on knowing what a plausible plan looks like and where reports are usually softened. Someone who has never delivered a project can administer PMO processes competently but will struggle to challenge a project manager who is confident and wrong.

**Which PMO certification is most recognised?**
Recognition depends heavily on sector and country, and any single answer is likely to be wrong somewhere. The practical test is to look at ten current job adverts for the role you want and see what they list. That takes twenty minutes and beats any general ranking, including this one.

**Can a PMO certification replace project controls training?**
No. They examine different material, and the gap is technical rather than presentational. A PMO that has no access to project controls skill will report what projects tell it, which is the failure mode the £2m example describes. If your PMO has no controls capability behind it, the training gap is the more urgent of the two.
