# Is project controls in demand? How to check it yourself

Yes, wherever capital is actually being executed rather than announced. Demand tracks sanctioned capital, contract form and lender or audit scrutiny, not fashion. Nobody can quote you a credible global headcount, so *is project controls in demand* is best answered with a method you can run on your own market this week.

The confident nonsense online comes from answering it with a number nobody sourced. Below is where the numbers break down, and what to measure instead.

## Why nobody can give you a real headcount

The discipline has no clean occupational code and no single job title, so it is invisible to the statistics that count other professions.

Ask ten organisations who does project controls and you get planners, schedulers, cost engineers, cost controllers, estimators, risk analysts, PMO analysts, document controllers and quantity surveyors — several of whom would not call themselves project controls people at all.

| Role family | What it is measured on | Titles it hides behind |
|---|---|---|
| Planning and scheduling | Logic quality, forecast dates, critical path integrity | Planning engineer, P6 scheduler, delivery planner |
| Cost engineering | Budget integrity, commitment tracking, forecast at completion | Cost controller, project cost analyst |
| Estimating | Estimate class, basis of estimate, benchmark defensibility | Cost estimator, pre-construction lead |
| Risk | Register quality, quantified analysis, contingency derivation | QRA analyst, risk manager |
| Reporting and data | Data integrity, period reporting, dashboards | PMO analyst, performance analyst |
| Commercial | Valuation, change, final account | Quantity surveyor, commercial manager |
| Controls management | The whole function and its interface with finance | Head of project controls, PMO lead |

Any count that ignores that spread is measuring job adverts, not the profession. Anyone quoting you a precise worldwide figure has estimated it and then dropped the assumptions.

## The demand model you can run in twenty minutes

Controls headcount scales with capital executed per year, not with capital announced. Use your own organisation's ratio, and if you do not have one, measure it before you trust anyone else's.

Take a programme with £900m of capital to execute over five years. That is **£180m a year** of executed capex.

Suppose your organisation has historically run one controls head for every £9m of annual executed capex. Required headcount is 180 ÷ 9 = **20 people**.

The ratio is the whole model, so test it rather than accepting it.

| £m annual capex per controls head | Headcount on £180m a year | Reads as |
|---:|---:|---|
| 7 | 25.7 | Heavy measurement: reimbursable work, owner-side scrutiny |
| 8 | 22.5 | Complex multi-contract programme |
| 9 | 20.0 | The illustrative middle |
| 10 | 18.0 | Lean function, mature systems |
| 11 | 16.4 | Lump-sum delivery, controls pushed to contractors |

These figures are illustrative arithmetic on a stated assumption, not published data. The point is the shape: a ratio anywhere between £7m and £11m per head is defensible for the same portfolio, and it moves the answer from 25.7 people to 16.4, a difference of nine.

That is why sector-wide claims about demand should be read with suspicion. The sensitivity of the model is larger than most of the differences people argue about.

Then add replacement. If that team of 20 turns over at 18 per cent a year, it needs 3.6 hires annually before the portfolio grows at all. Demand is mostly replacement demand, which is exactly the part that never appears in an announcement.

## Is project controls in demand in every sector?

No, and the variation is larger than the variation between countries.

| Sector | What drives controls demand | What suppresses it |
|---|---|---|
| Energy and LNG | Long sanction-to-completion cycles, joint venture partners requiring auditable reporting | Delayed final investment decisions |
| Power transmission and grid | Regulated investment cycles with published allowances | Regulatory determinations landing late |
| Rail and metro | Public scrutiny, multi-year programmes, formal stage gates | Programme deferral after a spending review |
| Water and utilities | Fixed regulatory periods that force a delivery profile | End-of-period slowdown before the next cycle |
| Data centres and semiconductors | Speed, parallel construction, heavy procurement risk | Demand-led pauses in capacity plans |
| Pharmaceutical and process | Validation-driven scheduling, tight capitalisation cut-offs | Portfolio reprioritisation |
| Commercial property | Development finance requiring drawdown certification | Rate-sensitive stop-start |

The safest reading is that demand follows executed capital with a lag of roughly one project stage. Watch sanction announcements, not press releases about ambitions.

## Contract form changes the demand more than the sector does

Reimbursable and target-cost contracts require the client to measure everything, because the client is paying for it. Those forms create controls roles on both sides of the table.

Lump-sum contracts push measurement to the contractor and change what the owner needs: fewer cost engineers, more commercial and change-management capacity.

Alliance and integrated forms need the most, because a shared pain-gain mechanism only works if the cost and progress data is trusted by everyone in the arrangement.

## Where demand is hardest to satisfy

The scarce profile is not a planner or a cost engineer. It is somebody who can hold both sides of the same number.

A project's percentage complete is a delivery measure and a revenue measure at the same time. An engineer is examined on progress measurement and almost never on cut-off or a contract asset; an accountant is examined on the reverse. The projects that lose money quietly lose it in that gap.

That is the profile organisations struggle to hire, and it is why PCI's credentials are built across the seam: the PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) covers 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas, the PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI) 16 domains and 61 knowledge areas, and the PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI) 16 domains and 63 knowledge areas, each Body of Knowledge running 40 per cent finance and reporting, 40 per cent project management and 20 per cent governed AI.

## What to check before you conclude anything

Five signals, all of which you can read directly rather than taking on trust.

| Signal | Where to look | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Sanction announcements | Owner investor updates and regulator determinations | Executed capex 6–18 months ahead |
| Framework awards | Contractor and PMC news pages | Which firms are about to staff up |
| Contract form | Award notices and tender documents | Whether the roles land owner-side or contractor-side |
| Repeat postings | The same role re-advertised after 60 days | Genuine scarcity rather than churn |
| Requirements drift | Job adverts asking for forecasting, risk and finance literacy together | The overlap profile becoming the standard ask |

The fifth is the one to watch if you are planning a career rather than a hire. Adverts that once asked for a scheduling tool now ask for measurement, forecasting and an ability to explain the numbers to finance.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is project controls a growing field or a shrinking one?**
It grows where capital execution grows, and both parts of that sentence matter. Announcements do not create controls roles; sanctioned projects entering the define and execute stages do. Watch final investment decisions and regulator spending determinations in your region, because they lead hiring by roughly two to four quarters.

**Will automation reduce demand for project controls staff?**
It reduces demand for data assembly, which was a large share of junior work, and raises demand for people who can judge the output. Schedule checks, progress imports and report drafting automate well. Choosing a forecast method, defending a variance and resolving out-of-sequence progress do not, and those now occupy more of the role.

**Which project controls role is easiest to enter?**
Reporting and data analysis roles have the lowest entry barrier, because the tooling can be learned quickly and the work is visible. They are also the most exposed to automation. Planning and cost engineering take longer to enter and hold their value better, since both require judgement that is developed on live projects rather than in a course.

**Does demand differ between owner and contractor organisations?**
Substantially, and the difference follows the contract. Owners running reimbursable or target-cost work measure everything themselves and staff accordingly. Owners running lump-sum work carry a smaller team focused on assurance and change. Contractors mirror the opposite pattern, which is why the same regional capex can produce quite different hiring.

**Do certifications affect employability in this field?**
They matter most where an employer cannot easily verify what you have done, which covers most first interviews and nearly all overseas applications. A credential examined on scenario judgement rather than tool operation is worth more than a product badge. It supplements a record of work you can explain; it does not substitute for one.
