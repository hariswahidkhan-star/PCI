# Project financing for project controls professionals

Project financing funds an asset on the strength of its own forecast cash flows. Lenders take security over a single-purpose company and have limited recourse to the sponsors, so the cost report and the programme stop being management documents and become conditions of drawing money.

Project financing for project controls professionals is therefore not a finance topic to be handed upstairs. It is a set of tests your monthly outputs have to pass, in someone else's format, to a date you do not control.

This is a LinkedIn original under the Institute's cost control and estimating pillar.

## Project finance and corporate finance are not the same borrowing

| Axis | Corporate borrowing | Project financing |
|---|---|---|
| Who repays | The company, from all its activities | One asset, from its own cash flows |
| Security | The balance sheet | The shares in the vehicle, its contracts and its accounts |
| Recourse to sponsors | Full | Limited, and usually ending at completion |
| What is examined | Historic accounts and covenants | A financial model running twenty years forward |
| What stops the money | A missed covenant test | A failed condition precedent to the next drawdown |
| Who checks the project | Nobody, routinely | A lenders' technical adviser, every month |
| Cost of funds | Lower | Higher, plus fees, plus the cost of the diligence |

The last row deserves saying plainly. Project financing is expensive and slow. It is chosen when the sponsor cannot or will not carry the asset on its own balance sheet, not because it is cheaper.

## Where the money comes from, and in what order

A worked structure. Capital cost **£500m**: EPC contract £430m, owner's costs £30m, development costs £15m, contingency £25m.

Add the two items that are not in the estimate. **Interest during construction** and arrangement fees. Total funding requirement becomes **£535m**, geared roughly 70:30, so **£375m** of debt and **£160m** of equity.

Interest during construction is circular. The interest depends on the drawdown profile, the drawdown profile depends on the facility size, and the facility size includes the interest. Models solve it by iteration, and the answer is sensitive to the S-curve you supplied.

Work it once. Drawdowns follow construction spend over 30 months, so the average outstanding balance is around 45% of the facility: 0.45 × 375 = **£168.8m**. At 7% for 2.5 years, that is 168.8 × 0.07 × 2.5 = **£29.5m** of interest capitalised into the asset.

Equity is normally drawn first or pro rata, and the order is negotiated rather than standard. Where equity goes in first, the sponsor is exposed to the whole early overrun before the bank has lent a pound.

## The ratios that decide whether the project keeps its money

| Ratio | What it divides | Typical trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Gearing | Debt to total funding | Sized at financial close, tested at completion |
| Interest cover | Operating cash flow to interest | A warning ratio rather than a hard test |
| Debt service cover (DSCR) | Cash available for debt service to principal plus interest in the period | Distributions locked up below one level, default below another |
| Loan life cover (LLCR) | Net present value of cash available over the remaining loan life to debt outstanding | Tested at refinancing points and on major changes |

Take the first operating year. Cash available for debt service, after operating costs and tax but before distributions, is **£62m**. Debt service, principal plus interest, is **£46m**.

**DSCR** = 62 / 46 = **1.35 times**.

The lock-up covenant is 1.30 times. The cash flow that trips it is 46 × 1.30 = **£59.8m**, which is £2.2m below forecast, or **3.5%**.

The event of default sits at 1.15 times, needing 46 × 1.15 = **£52.9m**, a fall of **14.7%**.

Read those two percentages together and the discipline makes sense. A 3.5% miss stops the sponsors taking a dividend. That is how tight the structure is, and it is why lenders care about a two-week slip in commissioning.

**LLCR** on the same facility: the present value of cash available over the remaining loan life is £510m against debt outstanding of £375m, giving **1.36 times**. LLCR looks at the whole tail, so it survives one bad year where DSCR does not.

## What does project financing for project controls professionals change?

**The drawdown certificate.** Money is released against a certificate stating costs incurred, costs to complete and that the funding remains sufficient. That is a cost report with a signature on it and a consequence attached.

**Cost to complete, not cost at completion.** Lenders test whether committed funds cover the remaining cost. The estimate at completion is the input, but the number in the certificate is the remaining spend, split between committed and forecast.

**The lenders' technical adviser.** An engineer paid by the banks reads your programme, your progress measurement and your variation register every month, and writes an opinion the banks act on. Weak earning rules get found here.

**Completion tests.** Physical completion, performance tests and a reliability run convert construction debt into term debt and release the sponsors from most recourse. Missing them keeps the guarantee alive.

**Contingency drawing rules.** Contingency in a project-financed structure is usually not the sponsor's to spend. Drawing it needs a stated cause and, above a threshold, lender consent.

None of this works if the cost report and the programme disagree. The forecast that supports a drawdown, the forecast that supports revenue recognition and the forecast the project team believes have to be the same forecast, which is the point argued in [how the estimate at completion reaches the accounts](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/eac-accounting).

## Delay costs money twice, and only one part is obvious

A three-month delay to commercial operation on the structure above.

**Interest.** At the end of construction the facility is fully drawn at £375m. Three further months at 7% is 375 × 0.07 × 0.25 = **£6.6m**, added to the funding requirement and, if the facility is fully committed, funded by the sponsors.

**Revenue.** Three months of operating cash flow at £62m a year is 62 × 0.25 = **£15.5m** of cash that never arrives, pushed to the end of the concession where it is worth less.

**Cover.** If the first repayment date does not move, the debt service falls into a year with nine months of revenue. CFADS of 0.75 × 62 = £46.5m against debt service of £46m gives a DSCR of **1.01 times**, through both covenants.

The liquidated damages conversation happens after all three of those, and it recovers a fraction of them. A planner who can put £22.1m against a three-month slip gets a different hearing from one who reports negative float.

## The working capital the contractor still carries

Project financing pays the contractor; it does not pay the contractor quickly. The payment mechanism in the EPC contract sets your receivable days, and it was negotiated by the borrower's lawyers to suit the lenders.

A contractor with annual revenue of **£200m** and cost of sales of **£180m**, with 74 days sales outstanding, 21 days of work in progress and contract assets, and 48 days payable outstanding.

Receivables = 74 / 365 × 200 = **£40.5m**. Work in progress = 21 / 365 × 180 = **£10.4m**. Payables = 48 / 365 × 180 = **£23.7m**.

Net working capital funded = 40.5 + 10.4 − 23.7 = **£27.2m**, on a cash conversion cycle of 74 + 21 − 48 = **47 days**.

Ten days off receivable days releases 10 / 365 × 200 = **£5.5m**, permanently, with no change to revenue or cost. Almost all of it comes from applications that match the certification rules rather than from chasing.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to understand the financial model to do my job?**
You need to understand what it consumes from you and what it is sensitive to. The model takes a drawdown profile, a completion date, a capital cost and an operating cost, and it turns them into cover ratios. Two of those four are outputs of project controls, and a slip in either can change a covenant test without anyone touching the model's assumptions.

**Why is the contingency locked?**
Because in a limited-recourse structure the contingency is part of the lenders' protection against cost overrun, not a management reserve. Drawing it usually needs a stated cause, evidence that the remaining funds still cover the remaining cost, and consent above an agreed threshold. Budget-holders who treat it as available money find out at the worst moment.

**What is cash available for debt service?**
Operating revenue less operating costs, maintenance capital expenditure and tax, before any payment to shareholders. It is a cash measure, so it ignores depreciation and any accounting accrual. Cover ratios are calculated on it precisely because it cannot be improved by an accounting judgement.

**Does earned value have any role in a project-financed job?**
Yes, and a sharper one than usual. The drawdown certificate needs cost incurred and cost to complete, and earned value is the most defensible way to show that spend and progress are consistent. A cost performance index near 1.00 on a package that is visibly behind is exactly what a lenders' technical adviser is looking for.

**Where does a project controls professional learn the finance side properly?**
Not from a scheduling course, and not from an accounting qualification either, because neither examines the overlap. A chartered accountant is rarely examined on a drawdown certificate; an engineer is rarely examined on a cover ratio.
