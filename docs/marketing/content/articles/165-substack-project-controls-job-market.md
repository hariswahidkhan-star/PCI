# The project controls job market: three ways to size it

Nobody knows, and any single figure you have seen is a job-board scrape with the duplicates left in. The project controls job market has no occupational classification of its own, so it cannot be counted directly. It can be estimated three ways, and the spread between those three is the honest answer.

*Written first for this newsletter. Every number below is illustrative and exists to show the method, because a fabricated market size would be worse than no market size. Substitute your own region's inputs and the arithmetic holds.*

## Why the project controls job market has no published count

Statistical agencies count occupations by classification code, and there is no code for a planning engineer or a cost controller. The work is scattered across engineering, management and administrative codes, and the split differs by country.

Employers do not help. The same job is advertised as planner, scheduler, planning engineer, project controls engineer, cost engineer, PMO analyst and delivery analyst, and one of those titles will also be used for something else entirely.

So every number in circulation is one of three things: a live-vacancy count, a ratio estimate, or a guess with a decimal point on it. The first two are worth doing.

## Method one: counting postings properly

Start with a fixed collection window and a fixed title list, then remove what is not a job.

Illustrative inputs for one country over twelve months: 6,650 raw postings across four boards. Same-role duplication across boards and re-posts by agencies runs at 38%, so unique postings = 6,650 × 0.62 = **4,123**.

Not every posting becomes a hire. At a fill rate of 0.70, hires = 4,123 × 0.70 = **2,886**.

Postings measure flow. To get the stock of people doing the work, divide the annual hiring flow by the annual turnover rate: 2,886 ÷ 0.14 = **20,615**.

Now the honest part, which is the sensitivity:

| Fill rate | Turnover | Estimated stock |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.60 | 18% | 13,743 |
| 0.60 | 11% | 22,489 |
| 0.85 | 18% | 19,470 |
| 0.85 | 11% | 31,860 |

Two assumptions nobody can measure precisely move the answer by a factor of 2.3. Anyone quoting a posting-derived market size to three significant figures has not run this table.

## Method two: the capex ratio

Project controls headcount tracks capital expenditure being executed, not revenue and not company size. So take programmes where you know both numbers.

| Programme | Annual capex £m | Controls headcount | £m capex per head |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Rail upgrade | 620 | 71 | 8.73 |
| Water investment cycle | 240 | 34 | 7.06 |
| Offshore wind | 1,100 | 118 | 9.32 |
| Acute hospital | 95 | 9 | 10.56 |
| **Total** | **2,055** | **232** | **8.86** |

The weighted ratio is 2,055 ÷ 232 = **£8.86m of annual capex per controls professional**, all figures illustrative.

Apply it to a region executing £180bn of major-project capex a year: 180,000 ÷ 8.86 = **20,321 people**.

Using the extremes of the four programmes instead, £7.06m and £10.56m per head, gives **25,496** and **17,045**. The ratio is sensitive to project type, and a portfolio heavy in small building work needs a different ratio from one heavy in process plant.

This method is more stable than posting counts because it rests on one assumption rather than three, and that assumption is measurable inside your own business.

## Method three: occupational mapping

Take the adjacent classification code that captures most of the population, then apply a share.

Illustrative: 96,000 people sit in the adjacent code, and a sample of job descriptions suggests 21% are doing project controls work. That gives 96,000 × 0.21 = **20,160**.

The whole method rests on the share, and the share can only come from reading job descriptions. It is the crudest of the three and the only one anchored to a real population count.

## The three methods side by side

| Method | What it measures | Central estimate | Plausible range | Dominant error |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Posting count | Hiring flow converted to stock | 20,615 | 13,700–31,900 | Fill rate and turnover assumptions |
| Capex ratio | Staffing intensity of executed capex | 20,321 | 17,000–25,500 | Portfolio mix |
| Occupational mapping | Share of an adjacent population | 20,160 | Depends entirely on the share | Title ambiguity |

Three methods landing between 20,100 and 20,700 is not confirmation, because the inputs were chosen by the same person on the same afternoon. What it does show is that no method supports quoting a single number.

The defensible statement is a range with its assumptions attached: on these inputs, roughly 20,000 people, and anything between 15,000 and 30,000 is consistent with the evidence. That sentence is more useful in a board paper than a false precision, and it is the only version that survives being checked.

## What the count is made of

| Role family | What it is measured on | Where it hides in a search |
|---|---|---|
| Planner or scheduler | Logic quality, forecast dates, critical path | "Planning engineer", "P6 scheduler", "delivery planner" |
| Cost engineer | Budget integrity, commitment tracking, forecast | "Cost controller", "project cost analyst" |
| Estimator | Estimate class, basis, benchmark defensibility | "Cost estimator", "pre-construction" |
| Risk analyst | Register quality, quantified analysis, contingency | "QRA analyst", "risk manager" |
| Reporting and data analyst | Data integrity, dashboards, period reporting | "PMO analyst", "performance analyst" |
| Document control | Revision integrity, transmittal discipline | "Information manager", "EDMS administrator" |
| Commercial and QS | Valuation, change, final account | "Quantity surveyor", "commercial manager" |
| Controls manager | The whole function and its interface with finance | "Head of project controls", "PMO lead" |

Any market size depends on which rows you counted. Include document control and commercial and the number roughly doubles; count only planners and cost engineers and it halves.

State the rows before stating the total. Most published disagreements about the size of this profession are disagreements about that list rather than about the data.

## What moves demand, by region

Demand follows sanctioned capital, with a lag of roughly two to four quarters between a programme reaching financial close and the controls roles appearing.

| Region | What to watch | Where the roles are advertised |
|---|---|---|
| Gulf | Giga-programme packages reaching award | Owner and PMC careers pages before job boards |
| North America | Data centre, semiconductor and LNG sanction announcements | EPC contractor sites and specialist agencies |
| UK and Europe | Regulated investment cycles: water, grid, rail | Framework contractor portals |
| Asia-Pacific | Transmission, metro and industrial capacity | Local agencies, often not in English |
| Africa | Mining and power projects reaching close | Owner and lender-side advisory roles |

Two structural points hold everywhere. Owner-side roles are advertised least and paid most, because they are filled from contractor-side people who have already done the work, which is [the gate most planning careers actually turn on](https://pciworld.org/senior-planning-engineer-career-path). And the sharpest demand sits where finance and delivery meet, on programmes where the controls function has to produce a forecast the reporting team can use directly.

That overlap is what the three PCI credentials examine: PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) across 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas, PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI) across 16 domains and 61 knowledge areas, and PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI) across 16 domains and 63 knowledge areas. Anyone weighing those against the established alternatives needs [which controls certification fits which role](https://credentialfinder.org/best-project-controls-certification), not a job count.

## What a job count cannot tell you

It cannot tell you whether those jobs are good. Stock and flow say nothing about seniority mix, contract length, or how many of the postings are the same understaffed programme reappearing.

It cannot tell you what will happen next year, because postings respond to sanction decisions already made and tell you about the past two quarters.

And it cannot tell you your own odds, which depend on your sector, your region and whether you can produce a defensible forecast. A market of twenty thousand is irrelevant if the eleven roles you can actually do are all in one city.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do published estimates of this market differ so widely?**
Because they count different role lists over different geographies with different methods, and rarely say which. One study counting planners and cost engineers in one country will produce a fraction of another counting the full controls function across a continent. Read the method note before the headline, and if there is no method note the figure is not evidence.

**Is the number of project controls jobs growing?**
Sanctioned capital in energy transition, grid, water and digital infrastructure has been rising in most regions, and controls headcount follows capital with a lag. That is a directional statement from the shape of the market, not a measured growth rate, and I am not going to invent one. Track sanction announcements in your sector; they are the leading indicator you can actually see.

**Does AI reduce the number of roles?**
It changes what the roles do before it changes how many there are. Schedule assembly, data cleaning and report production are the parts a model does well, and they are the junior parts. The likely effect is fewer people producing reports and more people being asked to explain a forecast, which raises the entry bar rather than removing the job.

**Which method should I use for a business case?**
The capex ratio, calibrated on your own programmes, because you can defend every input from your own records. Use a posting count as a cross-check on the direction and never as the primary basis. Present both with their assumptions, and let the range be the answer rather than picking whichever number supports the argument.

**How do I count roles that are not advertised?**
You cannot count them directly, which is why every posting-based estimate understates owner-side and internal-transfer hiring. Apply an uplift derived from your own hiring records: if a third of your controls hires came through internal moves and referrals, a posting count is measuring two thirds of the market at best. State the uplift rather than burying it.
