# IFRS 15 vs IAS 11: what really changed for construction

IFRS 15 replaced IAS 11 for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2018. The arithmetic of cost-to-cost survived; almost nothing around it did. The unit of account moved from the contract to the performance obligation, claims became harder to recognise, and the loss rule left the revenue standard altogether.

So IFRS 15 vs IAS 11 is not a change of formula. It is a change in what is being measured, and in how much has to be true before a number may be booked.

This piece is written for LinkedIn and is not on the PCI site. It sits under the Institute's cost control and estimating pillar, which is where contract accounting and cost engineering meet.

## What did IFRS 15 vs IAS 11 change, line by line?

IAS 11 was a construction-specific standard. IFRS 15 is a single revenue model applied to software, telecoms, pharmaceuticals and civils alike, with no industry carve-out for contractors.

| Axis | IAS 11 (withdrawn) | IFRS 15 (in force) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Construction contracts only | Every contract with a customer, all industries |
| Unit of account | The contract, with segmentation and combination rules | The performance obligation inside the contract |
| Entry test | Contract type: fixed price or cost plus | Does control transfer over time? Three conditions, any one |
| Named method | Percentage of completion | No named method. Measure progress faithfully; input or output |
| Variations and claims | Included when probable and reliably measurable | Included only to the extent a significant reversal is highly unlikely |
| Loss-making contracts | Expected loss recognised immediately, inside the standard | No loss rule. The provision arises under IAS 37 |
| Balance sheet label | Gross amount due from or to customers | Contract asset, contract liability, receivable |
| Uninstalled materials | Little explicit guidance | Excluded from the progress measure, recognised at cost with no margin |

Two rows in that table cause almost all of the practical difficulty: the unit of account and the constraint on variable consideration.

## The unit of account moved from the contract to the promise

Under IAS 11 the contract was the thing you accounted for, and separate rules told you when to split one contract or merge two.

Under IFRS 15 you start inside the contract and ask what distinct promises it contains. A promise is distinct when the customer can benefit from it on its own and it is separable from the rest of the contract.

On an integrated build the answer is usually one obligation, because the contractor is providing a significant service of combining inputs into a single output. Piling and cladding are not separate promises when the promise is a building.

Where a genuinely separate promise exists, such as a design package the customer could have bought elsewhere or a five-year maintenance tail, the price is split by relative standalone selling price. Not by the schedule of rates, which was negotiated for cash flow.

## Claims: from probable to highly unlikely to reverse

This is the change that moves the most money, and the one most cost reports have never been adjusted for.

**The old test.** A claim entered contract revenue when negotiations had reached a stage where it was probable the customer would accept it, and the amount could be measured reliably.

**The new test.** A variable amount enters the transaction price only to the extent that a significant reversal of cumulative revenue is highly unlikely once the uncertainty resolves. Probable is not enough.

Work it. A fixed-price contract of **£75.0m**, forecast cost **£60.0m**, costs incurred to date **£30.0m**. Cost-to-cost progress is 30.0 / 60.0 = **50.00%**.

The contractor has a **£6.0m** disruption claim in front of the client. The commercial team assesses **£4.0m** as probable of recovery. The constraint assessment, tested head by head against contract entitlement and correspondence, supports **£1.5m** as highly unlikely to reverse.

**Old basis.** Transaction price 75.0 + 4.0 = £79.0m. Cumulative revenue = 0.50 × 79.0 = **£39.50m**. Cost £30.0m. Cumulative margin **£9.50m**.

**IFRS 15 basis.** Transaction price 75.0 + 1.5 = £76.5m. Cumulative revenue = 0.50 × 76.5 = **£38.25m**. Cost £30.0m. Cumulative margin **£8.25m**.

| Line | Old basis | IFRS 15 basis |
|---|---:|---:|
| Transaction price | £79.00m | £76.50m |
| Progress (cost-to-cost) | 50.00% | 50.00% |
| Cumulative revenue | £39.50m | £38.25m |
| Cumulative cost | £30.00m | £30.00m |
| Cumulative margin | **£9.50m** | **£8.25m** |

The cross-check ties both ways: expected margin of 79.0 − 60.0 = £19.0m at 50% gives £9.50m, and 76.5 − 60.0 = £16.5m at 50% gives £8.25m.

A **£1.25m** difference in reported profit, from the same claim, the same costs and the same progress. The only thing that changed is the evidential bar.

## Combining two contracts can erase a loss provision

Contracts entered into at or near the same time with the same customer are combined and treated as one where they were negotiated as a package, where the consideration on one depends on the other, or where the promises are a single obligation.

That rule is dry until you price an enabling works package at cost to win the main job.

**Enabling works.** Price £8.0m, forecast cost £8.4m. Standing alone, it is an onerous contract: a **£0.4m** provision on signature.

**Main works.** Price £20.0m, forecast cost £16.0m, margin £4.0m.

**Combined.** Price £28.0m, forecast cost £24.4m, margin **£3.6m**. No provision arises, because no separate loss-making contract exists. The measure of progress now runs across the whole £24.4m of cost, so the enabling works earn at the blended 12.86% margin rather than at a loss.

The reported profit over the life of the two jobs is £3.6m either way. The timing and the balance sheet are different, and so is the conversation with the auditor about why a signed contract carries no provision.

## The loss rule left the revenue standard

IAS 11 carried its own requirement: when total contract costs were expected to exceed total contract revenue, the expected loss went straight to the income statement.

IFRS 15 contains no onerous contract provision. When a contract is expected to lose money, the provision arises under IAS 37, and that standard defines the unavoidable costs as the lower of the cost of fulfilling and any penalty for failing to fulfil, with directly related costs included.

The practical effect on a project is unchanged in one respect. The whole expected loss is still recognised as soon as it is expected, rather than bled across the remaining programme.

What did change is which team owns the judgement and which cost base feeds it. That is a live question the month a forecast crosses the line, and it is worked in full in [how the estimate at completion reaches the accounts](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/eac-accounting).

## What the cost engineer has to produce that IAS 11 never asked for

Three deliverables became accounting inputs rather than management information.

**A split of costs incurred** between those that measure progress and those that do not, with uninstalled materials identified separately by value and location.

**A constraint assessment per claim**, head by head, saying what evidence supports the amount and what would have to happen for it to reverse. A single expected recovery figure in the cost report is no longer sufficient.

**A contract map** showing which agreements with the same customer were negotiated together, because that determines whether you are reporting on one contract or three.

None of those come from the finance function. They come from the people who run the cost report and the commercial file, which is exactly the overlap the [PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI)](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/finance-and-project-management-certification) credential examines across 16 domains and 61 knowledge areas.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is percentage of completion still allowed under IFRS 15?**
The phrase is not in the standard. What survives is the underlying arithmetic: once an obligation is satisfied over time, the entity picks a measure of progress, and cost-to-cost is the most common. The difference is the entry test. Under IAS 11 the contract type decided the treatment; under IFRS 15 an obligation that fails the over-time test recognises nothing until control passes, however much work has been done.

**Did IFRS 15 change how much profit a contract makes?**
No. Over the life of a contract, profit is price minus cost under any standard. IFRS 15 changed when that profit appears, which contracts it is measured across, and how much evidence a claim needs before it counts. On a multi-year job those timing differences are large enough to move a reporting period from profit to loss.

**What replaced gross amount due from customers?**
Contract asset and contract liability. A contract asset arises when cumulative revenue exceeds cumulative billings, and it is not yet an unconditional right to payment. A contract liability is the reverse: billed ahead of the work. Once the right to payment becomes unconditional, the balance moves to receivables, which is why a certified application and an uncertified one sit in different places.

**Does the over-time test ever fail on construction work?**
Yes, and it is worth checking rather than assuming. The third condition requires an asset with no alternative use and an enforceable right to payment for work completed to date, including a reasonable margin. A termination-for-convenience clause that pays only cost incurred can break that right, pushing the whole scope to point-in-time recognition and deferring every pound of revenue to handover.

**Who in a project team needs to understand this comparison?**
The cost engineer, the commercial manager and the planner, not only the accountant. Progress, forecast cost and claim status all originate on the project and all three are accounting inputs. A chartered accountant is rarely examined on an earning rule; an engineer is rarely examined on cut-off. The money is lost in the gap.
