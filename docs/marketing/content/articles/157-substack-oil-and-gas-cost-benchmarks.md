# Oil and gas cost benchmarks: what a rate must carry

Oil and gas cost benchmarks are unit rates — cost per daily barrel, per tonne, per kilometre, per installed hour — carrying four fields with them: scope boundary, price base date, currency basis and the denominator's definition. A rate without those four fields is an anecdote, and most rates in circulation are missing at least two.

*Written first for this newsletter. No published benchmark data set is reproduced here and no operator's figures are quoted. Every number below is invented to show the method, which is the part that transfers.*

## Which oil and gas cost benchmarks are worth keeping?

A benchmark is a measured outcome from a completed scope, normalised so it can be compared with a different scope. Four families do different jobs, and teams that keep only the first family cannot explain anything.

| Family | Example metrics | What it answers | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit capital cost | Cost per daily barrel of capacity, per tonne of annual capacity, per kilometre of pipeline at a stated diameter, per installed kW | Is this estimate the right size? | Says nothing about why, and is the most sensitive to boundary error |
| Input intensity | Engineering hours per tag, fabrication hours per tonne of steel, direct field hours per tonne erected, metres of pipe per welder-day | Where is the money going, and is it going faster than planned? | Needs consistent quantity take-off rules across projects |
| Schedule | Months from sanction to first oil, engineering duration to 80% issued for construction, commissioning duration per system | Is the programme plausible? | Meaningless without stating the scope boundary and the completion definition |
| Predictability | Cost growth from sanction to outturn, contingency drawn against progress, change value as a share of contract value | Can this organisation forecast? | Requires a stable definition of the baseline being measured from |

Unit capital cost is what gets quoted. Input intensity is what predicts. The second family is where an in-house benchmark set earns its keep, because it produces a forecast eighteen months before the first family produces a verdict.

## How do you normalise a unit rate so it actually compares?

Climb the ladder in a fixed order and show every step. A rate that arrives as a single number, with no ladder underneath it, will not survive a challenge from anyone who has priced the work.

Take a completed facility: **US$2.4bn** for **85,000 barrels per day** of capacity, at a 2019 price base, excluding owner's costs and excluding interest during construction.

Raw: 2,400m ÷ 85,000 = **US$28,235 per daily barrel**.

Add owner's costs, which your target estimate includes at 8% of installed cost: 2,400 × 1.08 = 2,592m, so 2,592m ÷ 85,000 = **US$30,494**.

Escalate to today's price base. Using your own composite index, not a published one, assume the cumulative factor over the period is 1.34: 30,494 × 1.34 = **US$40,862**.

Apply a location factor of 1.15 for the target region: 40,862 × 1.15 = **US$46,991**.

| Step | Adjustment | Rate (US$/bpd) |
|---|---|---:|
| 1 | As published | 28,235 |
| 2 | Owner's costs added at 8% | 30,494 |
| 3 | Escalated by a factor of 1.34 | 40,862 |
| 4 | Location factor 1.15 | 46,991 |

The comparable rate is 66% above the published one, and nothing about the facility changed. Every point of that 66% came from an adjustment made after the number was read.

Interest during construction is deliberately still outside this rate. Financing cost belongs in the funding case, not in a capital cost benchmark, and mixing the two is the fastest way to make two projects incomparable.

## Which metric tells you about an overrun before the cost report does?

Input intensity, measured weekly, against the same quantity basis the estimate used.

A fabrication scope of **12,400 tonnes** was budgeted at **28 hours per tonne**, so 12,400 × 28 = **347,200 hours**.

At the cut-off, **6,200 tonnes** are complete and **210,000 hours** have been booked. Achieved intensity is 210,000 ÷ 6,200 = **33.87 hours per tonne**.

Productivity factor = achieved ÷ budget = 33.87 ÷ 28 = **1.21**. Every budgeted hour is taking 1.21 hours.

Forecast the rest at the same factor: remaining 6,200 tonnes at 28 hours = 173,600 budget hours, × 1.21 = **210,056 hours**. Total forecast = 210,000 + 210,056 = **420,056 hours** against 347,200 budgeted, an overrun of **72,856 hours**, or 21%.

At a loaded rate of US$65 an hour that is 72,856 × 65 = **US$4.7m** on one fabrication scope, visible from a tonnage return and a timesheet long before it appears in a committed cost.

The same arithmetic runs on any physical unit: metres of pipe welded, cubic metres placed, cable pulled, instruments loop-checked. Pick units the site already counts for its own purposes, because a benchmark that needs a new data collection process will be abandoned by month four.

## What does contingency drawdown tell you?

More than any single cost variance, because it measures the estimate rather than the execution.

Plot contingency drawn as a share of the original contingency against physical progress, and read the ratio.

| Physical progress | Contingency drawn | Ratio | Reading |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 20% | 18% | 0.90 | Cover is holding, so far |
| 45% | 72% | 1.60 | Consumed faster than work is being done; the remainder is unfunded |
| 70% | 95% | 1.36 | Late risks are now uncovered, and commissioning has not started |

At the 45% row, 28% of the contingency is left to cover 55% of the work. If the drawdown rate continues, cover runs out at roughly 45 + (28 ÷ 1.60) = **62.5%** complete.

That single division is worth putting on the monthly report. It converts a comfortable-looking balance into a date.

## What does an in-house benchmark set need to record?

Five fields per entry. If any is blank, the entry is excluded from comparison rather than used with a caveat, because caveats do not survive being copied into a slide.

| Field | Why the comparison breaks without it |
|---|---|
| Scope boundary | Owner's costs, pre-sanction engineering, spares, first fill and land are in or out, and each is material |
| Price base date and index | An unstated price base makes escalation guesswork in both directions |
| Currency and conversion basis | A spot conversion and a purchasing-power conversion answer different questions |
| Denominator definition | Nameplate, debottlenecked or achieved capacity; gross or net; installed or erected tonnes |
| Delivery model and location | A lump-sum turnkey outcome and a reimbursable outcome are not the same measurement |

Build the set from projects your organisation delivered or priced. Two comparables you can reconstruct beat twenty published rates whose basis nobody can recover.

Record every entry at elemental level, not as a single total. A total rate cannot be adjusted honestly, because you cannot tell whether the difference sits in the substructure, the mechanical scope or the commissioning.

Sector benchmarking of this kind is taught through worked case material rather than rules of thumb. The PCI Bodies of Knowledge carry 92 sector case studies across the three volumes, split 26, 33 and 33.

## Where do these metrics meet the balance sheet?

On a contractor's working capital, which is the number the group treasurer feels first and the project team rarely sees.

The cash conversion cycle is days sales outstanding plus days inventory outstanding minus days payables outstanding. It measures how long cash is locked between paying for work and being paid for it.

Take a services contractor with **US$840m** of annual revenue, DSO of **74 days** including unbilled work, DIO of **41 days** across materials and work in progress, and DPO of **58 days**.

CCC = 74 + 41 − 58 = **57 days**. Daily revenue is 840 ÷ 365 = **US$2.30m**, so the cycle ties up 57 × 2.30 = **US$131m** of cash permanently.

Now let approvals slip and DSO rise by 10 days: 10 × 2.30 = **US$23m** of additional funding, from an administrative delay that appears in no cost report and moves no CPI.

This is the overlap the discipline is bad at. A project can be on budget by every metric above and still be the reason the group draws on its revolving facility, because progress measurement and cash collection are governed by different documents and different people.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many comparables do you need before a benchmark is usable?**
Fewer than people assume, if they are properly analysed. Three projects you can reconstruct to elemental level, with the five fields complete, support a defensible range. Twenty rates of unknown basis support nothing, because you cannot tell whether their spread is real variation or inconsistent measurement.

**Should a benchmark be a point or a range?**
A range, with the point you have chosen named inside it. Report the low, the chosen figure and the high, and state which comparables set each bound. A single benchmark number invites a negotiation about the number, where a range invites a discussion about the comparables, which is the conversation you want.

**Is cost per daily barrel comparable between onshore and offshore?**
No, and it is barely comparable within either. Water depth, host facility, sour service, arctic or desert conditions, local content rules and brownfield tie-ins each move the rate by more than the difference you are usually trying to measure. Compare within a class, and state the class.

**How often should the benchmark set be re-based?**
Re-base the price base annually and re-test the location factors whenever a market moves sharply, which in fabrication and specialist labour can be within a single quarter. Keep the original entries untouched and hold the adjustments separately, so a re-base never destroys the source measurement.

**Can a model normalise benchmarks automatically?**
It can propose the adjustments and it can find candidate comparables quickly, which is the tedious part. Measure it before trusting it: run a validation set a cost manager has already reviewed, and report precision and recall for the proposals. A tool described only as AI-assisted, with no measured error rate, cannot be cited in an estimate basis document.
