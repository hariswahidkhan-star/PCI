# Is project controls a good career? An honest answer

Is project controls a good career? Yes, for someone willing to be the person whose number gets tested in public, and no for almost everyone else. Pay tracks accountability rather than title, demand follows capital spending and is cyclical, and the ceiling is high only once you own a forecast rather than produce a report.

Written for LinkedIn as an original. It sits under the Institute's certification and careers pillar.

## What is the job on a normal month?

Project controls is the function that keeps one consistent statement of where a project is on time and money, and makes that statement defensible.

The month has a shape. Data date, progress cut, cost cut, reconcile the two, produce variance, explain variance, forecast, then defend the forecast to people who would prefer a different one.

Everything else in the role hangs off that cycle. The software is incidental; the cycle is the job. A fuller task-level account is in [what a project controls engineer does](https://pciworld.org/what-does-a-project-controls-engineer-do).

## The arithmetic that decides your month

Take a package with a budget at completion (**BAC**) of **£10,000,000**. At the data date, the work scheduled should have cost **£4,000,000** (**planned value, PV**). The work actually done is worth **£3,600,000** of budget (**earned value, EV**). You have spent **£4,200,000** (**actual cost, AC**).

**Cost variance** = EV − AC = 3,600,000 − 4,200,000 = **−£600,000**.
**Schedule variance** = EV − PV = 3,600,000 − 4,000,000 = **−£400,000**.
**Cost performance index (CPI)** = EV ÷ AC = 3,600,000 ÷ 4,200,000 = **0.857**.
**Schedule performance index (SPI)** = EV ÷ PV = 3,600,000 ÷ 4,000,000 = **0.90**.

You are getting 85.7 pence of budgeted work for every pound spent, and you are 10% behind the plan measured in money. That much is arithmetic and nobody argues with it.

Then somebody asks what the job will cost when it finishes, and the argument starts.

## Which estimate at completion do you sign?

| Method | Formula, on the numbers above | What it assumes | When it misleads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Remaining work at budget | AC + (BAC − EV) = 4,200,000 + 6,400,000 = **£10,600,000** | The overrun so far was a one-off and the rest runs to plan | Almost always optimistic when the cause is productivity |
| Performance continues | BAC ÷ CPI = 10,000,000 ÷ 0.857 = **£11,666,667** | Today's cost performance holds to the end | Wrong early, when a few large commitments distort CPI |
| Cost and schedule pressure both continue | AC + (BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI) = 4,200,000 + 6,400,000 ÷ 0.771 = **£12,496,296** | Being late will cost money as well as time | Double-counts when the delay is external and the site is demobilised |
| Bottom-up re-estimate | AC + a re-priced estimate to complete | Nothing about the past predicts the rest; you re-cost the remaining scope | Slow, and only as good as the people who priced it |

Same data, four defensible answers, spanning **£10.6m to £12.5m**. Choosing between them and being able to say aloud what your choice assumes is the career. The [four EAC formulas and what each assumes](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/four-eac-formulas) sets out the full treatment.

That £1.9m spread is also the point at which a project controls person becomes expensive to replace.

## How does it compare with the neighbouring careers?

| Career | What you own | What you are examined on | Where the ceiling is | Exposure to automation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Project controls | The integrated cost and schedule position, and the forecast method | Progress measurement, earned value, forecasting, risk quantification | Head of function, programme controls director | Data assembly automates; method choice does not |
| Project management | Delivery of the outcome, the team and the client relationship | Scope, stakeholders, delivery decisions | Programme and portfolio director | Low on people work, high on reporting admin |
| Quantity surveying | The commercial position: valuations, change, final account | Measurement, contract mechanisms, entitlement | Commercial director | Measurement automates; entitlement judgement does not |
| Management accounting | Recognition, provisions, accruals, statutory reporting | Accounting standards and controls | Finance director | Transaction processing automates heavily |
| Data analysis in delivery | The pipeline and the dashboard | Tooling, modelling, data quality | Head of data | Rapidly changing; the tools are the job |

The interesting column is the second one. An accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, almost never on a critical path. An engineer is examined on float and progress measurement, almost never on cut-off.

A capital project lives in that overlap, and project controls is the only role in the list asked about both sides in the same meeting.

That overlap is the strongest argument for the career and the reason it is hard to fill.

## Where does the money genuinely step up?

Not at a title change. It steps up when the organisation stops treating your output as information and starts treating it as a position it will defend externally.

Before that line you produce numbers and somebody else signs them. After it, the sentence "we are forecasting £11.7m and here is why" has your name attached when a lender, a client or an auditor tests it.

The bands people describe as seniority are really bands of scope, and the short version is that most careers stall one band below the signature.

## Is project controls a good career if you dislike conflict?

Honestly, no. The role exists because different parties want different numbers, and your value is that you produce the same number regardless of who is asking.

Expect to be asked to change a forecast. Expect the request to be reasonable in tone and unreasonable in substance. Expect to be right about a slippage four weeks before anyone thanks you for it, and sometimes to never be thanked at all.

People who thrive here are comfortable with being unpopular for a fortnight and vindicated in the quarter. People who need agreement in the room find it corrosive.

## What are the real costs of the career?

Cyclicality is the first. The function is funded from capital budgets, and capital budgets move with commodity prices, interest rates and government programmes. Sector concentration is a risk you should manage deliberately rather than discover.

Location is the second. The best-paid work follows large capital programmes, which are frequently not where you live. Site postings, rotations and relocation are normal rather than exceptional.

The month-end cycle is the third. The reporting calendar does not move for your diary, and the two or three days around cut-off are intense in a way that does not average out.

Tool churn is a minor one, and it is often overstated. Scheduling and cost systems change; progress measurement, float and the definition of an accrual do not.

## What makes it durable?

The parts that automate are the parts that were never the job. Extraction, reconciliation, schedule quality checking and first-draft commentary are all moving to software, and quickly.

What does not move is the ability to say why the number is the number, under questioning, with the contract and the accounting treatment both in view. A model can produce a forecast. It cannot be accountable for one.

The practical consequence for a new entrant is that the old training route through years of manual updating is closing, so the skill has to be built deliberately. Reviewing machine output well is harder than producing it, because a wrong answer that is internally consistent looks exactly like a right one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a graduate with no site experience do well in this profession?**
Yes, provided you get to a project where you see the physical work. Controls people who have never watched the work being installed tend to accept progress claims that a site-experienced colleague would refuse. Take the first role that puts you near delivery, even if the title is weaker, and move to the analysis work once you can tell claimed from installed.

**Is project controls better paid than project management?**
The two overlap heavily and comparing titles is unreliable, because the same title covers very different scope between companies. What is consistent is the driver: both are paid for the consequences of the decisions they own. PCI publishes no salary figures, and any single national average for either role is averaging incompatible jobs together.

**How long does it take to reach the point where you own a forecast?**
It depends on exposure rather than years. People who have run a full reporting cycle end to end, including the accrual and the change position, get there considerably faster than people who have only maintained a schedule. The fastest route is a smaller project where you do all of it, rather than a large one where you do one slice.

**Does the career survive a downturn?**
Partly, and not evenly. When capital spending falls, new-build controls roles fall with it, while claims, delay analysis and forensic work usually rise because troubled projects need evidence. People with contract and delay skills alongside the reporting skills move between those two states; people with only tool skills find the transition hard.

**Is certification necessary to progress?**
It is not a gate in most markets, and no credential guarantees a role. What a credential does is give a hiring manager something documentary to check when your experience is difficult to verify, which matters most when you are moving country, sector or employer. Judge one by whether its examination covers what the job actually tests.
