# Women in project controls: measuring the profession

There is no credible published figure for the share of women in project controls, because national statistics contain no such occupation and the profession has never run a census. What can be measured, honestly and quickly, is progression inside a single function. The method below takes an afternoon and produces numbers you can defend.

Written for LinkedIn as an original. It sits under the Institute's certification and careers pillar.

## Why is there no reliable figure for women in project controls?

Because the profession is invisible to the systems that count professions.

Occupational classifications used by national statistics offices do not carry a project controls category. The same people are recorded as cost estimators, project management specialists, construction managers, engineers or business analysts depending on how their employer filed them.

Job titles compound it. Planner, cost engineer, cost controller, scheduler, controls analyst and controls manager describe overlapping work that different firms slice differently, so no title-based sample captures the same population twice.

Any figure you see for the profession's gender composition has therefore been assembled from a survey of unknown coverage, a job-board scrape, or one company's headcount. None of those is the profession, and PCI will not publish a number it cannot source.

## What can a single organisation measure instead?

Progression, gate by gate. A controls function has four gates, and each one is decided by something you can look up.

| Gate | What actually decides it | Is the decision recorded? | Where representation leaks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entry to the function | Which feeder discipline you arrived from | Yes, in recruitment records | The function inherits the composition of engineering, surveying, accounting and site supervision |
| Analysis to ownership | Whether you have been handed a baseline or forecast to own | Rarely — usually an informal allocation | The single biggest leak, because the opportunity is given, not applied for |
| Ownership to integration | Whether you have run cost and schedule together across contracts | Partly, through role definitions | Depends on which projects you were staffed to, and on rotation and site postings |
| Integration to function lead | Whether an external party has relied on reporting you signed | Yes, in the sign-off record | Small numbers make this gate look random when it is not |

The second row is the one worth staring at. In most functions, nobody applies to own a forecast. Somebody decides to give it to you, and that decision leaves no paper trail unless you create one.

## The progression funnel, with the arithmetic

The figures below are illustrative. They are not PCI data and they are not a claim about the profession; they show what the calculation looks like when you run it on your own function.

A controls function of **120 people**, mapped to the [five-band project controls scope ladder](https://pciworld.org/project-controls-salary).

| Band | People | Women | Share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 — Data | 30 | 12 | 40% |
| 2 — Analysis | 45 | 15 | 33% |
| 3 — Ownership | 30 | 7 | 23% |
| 4 — Integration | 12 | 2 | 17% |
| 5 — Function | 3 | 0 | 0% |
| **Total** | **120** | **36** | **30%** |

The headline is 36 ÷ 120 = **30%**. On its own that number is close to useless, because it is an average across five populations with different shapes.

The shape is the finding. Representation falls **10 percentage points** between band 2 and band 3, from 33% to 23%, which is exactly the gate where number ownership starts and where the opportunity is allocated rather than requested.

Report the shape, not the average. A function quoting one percentage for itself is doing the same thing to its own data that the profession does to itself.

## Why small samples mislead, and what to do about it

At band 5 there are three people. One appointment moves the reported share from 0% to 33%, a swing of **33 percentage points**. At band 4 there are twelve, so one person is worth **8.3 points**.

Percentages at those sample sizes are theatre. Report counts: "none of three" and "two of twelve" are honest, and they stop anyone building a trend out of one hire.

The same discipline applies to conversion rates. Suppose that over 24 months, **2 of the 15 women in band 2 (13%)** moved up to band 3, against **6 of the 30 men (20%)**.

The gap looks like nearly seven percentage points. In people it is **one**: promote a third woman and 3 of 15 is 20%, exactly the same rate as the men.

That is not a finding, and presenting it as one damages the case. It is a question, and the answer comes from the allocation records behind the second gate, not from the percentage.

## Compare like with like, or compare nothing

The most common error in this analysis is a missing denominator.

Suppose the same function hired **40 people** in the last three years, **14 of them women (35%)**, while the population with eight or more years of tenure is **45 people, 9 of them women (20%)**.

The 30% headline sits between those two figures and explains neither. Intake and long-tenure population are different cohorts with different histories, and a change in intake will take years to appear in the senior bands.

Match cohorts by tenure before you compare anything. Then compare the function against its own feeder disciplines rather than against a national figure that does not exist.

## What in the job's structure decides progression?

Entry route is first. Project controls has no standard degree, so a function's composition is largely inherited from the disciplines it recruits from. A team that hires only from site engineering will look like site engineering.

Staffing is second, and it is the mechanism that changes the outcome fastest. Bands 3 and 4 are reached by being staffed to work that has a forecast attached, and staffing decisions are usually made quickly, verbally and without a record.

Mobility is third. The work with the widest scope tends to sit on large capital programmes that involve relocation, rotation or extended site postings, and any constraint on mobility becomes a constraint on scope.

The signature is fourth. The evidence that moves a career in this profession is documentary: a baseline someone accepted, a forecast you defended, reporting an external party relied on. That is unusually auditable compared with most professions, which makes it fixable.

## What a certifying body can and cannot do about it

It cannot promise an outcome, and any body that does is misleading you.

What an examination can do is produce documentary evidence of capability that does not depend on who staffed you to which project. PCI's credentials are examined against published Bodies of Knowledge, with the PCI AI Project Controls Leader (PCL-AI) at 13 domains and 61 knowledge areas, the PCI AI Project Finance Leader (PFL-AI) at 16 domains and 61 knowledge areas, and the PCI Project Management Leader – AI (PML-AI) at 16 domains and 63 knowledge areas.

A credential is one documentary input among several, and a third party can [verify a PCI credential](https://credentialfinder.org/verify) independently. It does not replace the record of what you have owned, and it is not a substitute for an organisation fixing its own second gate.

For anyone entering the profession, the practical version of all this is to make the invisible decisions visible. Ask which artefact you will own this year, get the answer written down, and keep the record of what you signed. [How to become a planning engineer](https://pciworld.org/how-to-become-a-planning-engineer) sets out the same evidence trail for the scheduling route.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there any trustworthy data on the profession's composition?**
Not at profession level. Individual employers, some national engineering bodies and some regional industry associations publish their own figures, and those are usable for the population they actually cover. What does not exist is a defined occupational category and a sampling frame for the profession as a whole, so any global percentage is an estimate built on an undefined denominator.

**Why not just use construction industry figures as a proxy?**
Because project controls sits across construction, energy, pharmaceuticals, defence, rail and technology, and the office-based analytical work has a different composition from site trades. Using an industry-wide figure imports the composition of a much larger population that includes occupations the profession does not draw from.

**What single measure should an employer start with?**
The band 2 to band 3 conversion rate, reported as counts and split by cohort tenure. That gate is where number ownership begins, it is where the funnel usually narrows, and the decision behind it is normally undocumented. Measuring it forces the allocation of forecast ownership to become an explicit decision rather than a habit.

**Does a credential help where the record is thin?**
It helps most when your experience is hard for a stranger to verify: moving country, moving sector, returning after a break, or coming from an employer nobody in the room recognises. It gives a hiring manager something checkable. It makes no claim about the role you will get, and PCI does not make one.

**Is the profession getting more balanced?**
There is no reliable series to answer that, which is itself the honest answer. Anyone claiming a trend needs two comparable measurements taken the same way at two points in time, and no such series exists for project controls. Organisations that start measuring their own gates now will be able to answer this question in five years.
