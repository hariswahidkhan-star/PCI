# Highest paying project controls industries, and why

The highest paying project controls industries are the ones where being wrong is most expensive: capital-intensive work, heavily financed, with contractual damages attached to dates and external auditors attached to numbers. PCI publishes no salary figures. What follows is the arithmetic that sets the band, so you can rank any sector for yourself.

Written for LinkedIn as an original. It sits under the Institute's certification and careers pillar.

## Why does pay track the cost of being wrong?

Because a controls role is an insurance position. The organisation is paying for a lower chance of a bad number reaching a client, a lender or a board.

That premium scales with what a bad number costs. On a fit-out, a wrong forecast is embarrassing. On a process plant with a financing package attached, a wrong forecast moves covenants.

So the sector question is really a question about exposure per day and per pound, and it can be computed rather than guessed.

## What does a day of delay actually cost?

Two costs run in parallel, and most people only count the first.

**Contractual damages.** Delay damages are commonly expressed as a percentage of contract value per day, subject to a cap. On a **£400,000,000** contract with a rate of **0.1% per day capped at 10%**, that is **£400,000 a day** to a maximum of **£40,000,000**.

On a **£4,000,000** fit-out at the same rate it is **£4,000 a day**. The rates and caps are illustrative; the ratio between the two is the whole point.

**Financing.** Money drawn during construction earns nothing until the asset operates. With **£400,000,000** drawn at a **7%** nominal rate, the annual carrying cost is **£28,000,000**, which is **£28,000,000 ÷ 365 = £76,712 a day**.

Together, a day on that programme is worth roughly **£477,000** before anyone counts prolongation, preliminaries or lost revenue from a delayed start of operations.

Now compare that with the fully loaded annual cost of a senior controls professional. A function that reliably recovers a handful of days a year has paid for itself several times over, and that arithmetic is why some sectors bid the same skills at a different level.

## Which are the highest paying project controls industries?

Rank the drivers rather than the job titles. The sectors where the drivers stack up together are where the top of the range sits.

| Sector | Capital intensity | Cost of a day | Contractual exposure | External scrutiny of your numbers | What controls is asked to own |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LNG, upstream oil and gas | Very high | Very high, tied to first production | Heavy damages, tight interface obligations | Joint venture partners and lenders | Integrated cost, schedule and contingency across contractors |
| Nuclear and major rail | Very high | Very high, with regulatory sequencing | Long programmes, heavy change regimes | Regulators and public accountability | Baseline integrity over many years, evidence quality |
| Pharmaceutical and semiconductor plant | High | Very high, tied to a product window | Compressed programmes, validation gates | Corporate audit and regulators | Schedule certainty against a market date |
| Power, renewables and transmission | High | High, tied to grid connection and offtake dates | Damages plus availability obligations | Lenders under project finance | Cost to connect, contingency, cash profile |
| Metro, highways and utilities | High | Moderate to high | Public procurement, change-heavy | Public audit bodies | Change control and reporting to funders |
| Commercial building | Moderate | Moderate | Standard forms, capped damages | Developer and funder | Cost plan discipline and valuation control |
| Fit-out and SME works | Low | Low | Small caps | Internal only | Reporting and margin protection |

The pattern is consistent. Pay rises where the controls function is on the critical path of the money, not merely reporting on it.

Geography stacks on top of this. The same sector pays differently where a market is short of experienced people and where the work carries rotation, mobilisation or remote-site conditions, which is what makes the Saudi giga-programme roles their own case.

## Which sectors put your numbers in front of an auditor?

The ones where your progress measurement drives reported revenue. That is the mechanism most people entering the profession have never had explained to them.

Under IFRS 15, described here in PCI's own words rather than reproduced, revenue is determined in five steps: identify the contract with the customer; identify the distinct performance obligations within it; determine the transaction price, including variable consideration and the constraint on recognising it; allocate that price across the obligations; and recognise revenue as each obligation is satisfied, over time where the criteria for transfer over time are met, otherwise at a point in time.

When revenue is recognised over time using an input measure such as cost incurred against total forecast cost, your estimate at completion is directly inside the revenue calculation. Change the EAC and you change the percentage complete, and therefore the revenue and the margin in the accounts.

The full treatment is in [IFRS 15 for construction](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/ifrs-15-for-construction). The short version is that heavily audited sectors pay for controls people who can be questioned by finance without needing a translator. A person who can hold both the progress position and its accounting consequence in one conversation is scarce, and the scarcity is priced.

An accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, almost never on float. An engineer is examined on float and progress measurement, almost never on cut-off. The money is lost in between, and that gap is where the best-paid controls work sits.

## What about the cash side?

Sectors with long cash cycles pay well because a controls person can move the cycle, and the saving is measurable.

The cash conversion cycle is days inventory outstanding plus days sales outstanding minus days payables outstanding. For a contractor, read the first term as unbilled work in progress: work done and not yet certified.

Take a business with **£120,000,000** of annual turnover. Work sits uncertified for **45 days**, certified applications are paid in **60 days**, and suppliers are paid in **30 days**.

**Cash conversion cycle** = 45 + 60 − 30 = **75 days**.
Daily turnover = 120,000,000 ÷ 365 = **£328,767**.
Working capital tied up = 328,767 × 75 = **£24,657,500**.
At a **7%** cost of money, carrying that costs **£1,726,000 a year**.

Now improve the front end. Better substantiation of applications pulls days sales outstanding from 60 to 45. Fifteen days released is 328,767 × 15 = **£4,931,500** of cash, worth about **£345,000 a year** in financing cost alone.

That improvement comes from measurement discipline, not from finance. It is one of the few things a controls team can do that shows up directly in a treasury report, and it is a clean argument to make in a pay conversation.

## Why do national average salary figures mislead?

Three structural reasons, and all of them push the same way.

Occupational statistics generally have no category called project controls. The people doing the work are counted as cost estimators, project management specialists, construction managers or engineers, so any figure attributed to the profession has been assembled from incompatible groups.

Titles are inconsistent between employers. A project controls manager at one contractor owns a function; at another it is a senior reporting role. Averaging them produces a number that describes nobody.

Package structure differs by sector and location. Rotation allowances, site uplifts, tax treatment and completion bonuses form a large part of the difference between two roles that show the same base figure.

Use the drivers above to work out where a role sits, and settle [which of the five scope bands your role genuinely occupies](https://pciworld.org/project-controls-salary) before you compare anything.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does oil and gas always pay more than construction?**
Not always, and treating it as a rule leads people into the wrong move. What travels with the money is the driver set: capital intensity, damages exposure, financing cost and audit scrutiny. A heavily financed data centre or grid connection programme can carry more of those drivers than a small upstream brownfield job, and it will price accordingly.

**Is it worth changing sector for pay alone?**
Only with the cycle in view. Sectors funded by commodity prices or a single government programme hire hard and stop suddenly, and people who arrive at the peak often leave at the trough with one narrow reference. Moving for a genuine step up in scope is durable; moving for a rate at the same scope usually is not.

**Which skills raise the band inside any sector?**
The ones that connect the schedule to the accounts. Forecasting with a stated method, quantified risk and contingency, change and entitlement, and the ability to explain what this month's progress does to revenue and cash. Tool skills move you between employers at the same band; those four move you up one.

**Does contracting or consulting pay more than a permanent role?**
The headline rate is usually higher and the comparison is not like for like. A day rate carries no notice period, no paid downtime between assignments, no employer pension contribution and no training budget. Compare on expected annual earnings across a realistic utilisation, then subtract the benefits you are replacing yourself.

**Does PCI publish salary data?**
No. PCI publishes certification requirements and the Bodies of Knowledge behind its examinations, and it makes no claim about pay, uplift or employment outcomes. Where you see a figure attached to a credential anywhere, ask who collected it, from how many people, in which year and in which market, before you act on it.
