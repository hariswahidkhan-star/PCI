# LNG cost benchmarks: which metrics actually compare

LNG cost benchmarks compare plants on cost per tonne of annual capacity, and that rate only means something once four things are fixed: the capacity definition, the scope boundary, the price base and the share of work executed in fabrication yards. Left unadjusted, two plants performing identically can differ by a third.

*Written first for this newsletter. No project data is quoted and no published cost figure is reproduced. Every number below is invented so the normalisation can be followed step by step.*

## Which LNG cost benchmarks are worth tracking?

Six, and only the first one gets quoted in public. The other five are the ones that let you explain the first.

| Metric | Definition | What it is good for |
|---|---|---|
| Cost per tonne of annual capacity | Total installed cost ÷ nameplate Mtpa | Sizing an estimate against a class of plant |
| Module content | Share of total direct construction hours executed in fabrication yards | Explaining the labour cost and the site risk |
| Direct field hours per tonne of capacity | Site hours ÷ Mtpa | Testing whether the construction plan is physically achievable |
| Marine and storage share | Jetty, tanks and loading as a share of installed cost | Making brownfield and greenfield totals comparable |
| Months from sanction to first cargo | Stated per train, with the completion definition named | Testing the programme against the class |
| Contingency drawn against progress | Cover consumed ÷ physical progress | The earliest honest signal on the estimate |

Two plants can share a cost per tonne and differ completely on every other row. That is when you know the headline rate was a coincidence.

## Why is cost per tonne not comparable as published?

Because the numerator and the denominator are both defined locally, and neither definition travels.

On the denominator, nameplate capacity, debottlenecked capacity and achieved production are three different numbers for the same plant, and a total that rose because a third train was added is not an overrun.

On the numerator, the boundary moves: upstream wells, the feed pipeline, the jetty, storage tanks, the accommodation camp, owner's costs and interest during construction are each in or out, and each is material.

Worked comparison, on invented figures. Greenfield plant G cost **US$16.8bn** for **10.5 Mtpa**, including jetty, storage, camp, owner's costs and financing. Brownfield expansion B cost **US$6.3bn** for **5.2 Mtpa**, excluding owner's costs and financing, on an existing site using the existing jetty and tanks.

Raw: G = 16,800 ÷ 10.5 = **US$1,600 per tonne**. B = 6,300 ÷ 5.2 = **US$1,212 per tonne**. G looks 32% more expensive.

Now put B on G's basis. Add owner's costs at 9%: 6.3 × 1.09 = **6.867bn**. Add interest during construction at 6%: 6.867 × 1.06 = **7.279bn**, so 7,279 ÷ 5.2 = **US$1,400 per tonne**.

Then add the marine and storage scope B did not have to build. On G, marine and storage is 14% of installed cost: 16.8 × 0.14 = 2.352bn, or 2,352 ÷ 10.5 = **US$224 per tonne**.

| Basis | G (US$/t) | B (US$/t) |
|---|---:|---:|
| As published | 1,600 | 1,212 |
| B with owner's costs and financing added | 1,600 | 1,400 |
| B with marine and storage allocated | 1,600 | 1,624 |

The 32% gap becomes 1.5% the other way. Nothing about either plant changed, and the entire difference was a boundary.

## What does module content actually change?

It moves hours from a site labour market to a yard labour market, and those two markets do not price alike.

Two plants of the same capacity class, each with **24.0m** direct construction hours. Plant M is 62% modular, Plant S is 35%.

Plant M: 24.0 × 0.62 = **14.88m yard hours**, leaving **9.12m site hours**. Plant S: 24.0 × 0.35 = **8.40m yard hours**, leaving **15.60m site hours**.

At an all-in yard rate of US$38 an hour and an all-in site rate of US$95 an hour, which carries camp, rotation, travel, supervision and the site's lower achieved productivity:

| | Yard cost | Site cost | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Plant M (62% modular) | 14.88m × 38 = **US$565m** | 9.12m × 95 = **US$866m** | **US$1,432m** |
| Plant S (35% modular) | 8.40m × 38 = **US$319m** | 15.60m × 95 = **US$1,482m** | **US$1,801m** |

A gap of US$369m from one ratio. That is the argument for modularisation, and it is incomplete.

Modules carry their own costs: heavier steel in the module frames, sea fastening, heavy transport, marine spreads and a heavy-lift campaign on site. Add 12% to M's yard scope for frame steel, 565 × 0.12 = **US$68m**, plus **US$180m** of transport and lift, and the advantage falls to 369 − 248 = **US$122m**.

Report the ratio and its consequences together. A benchmark that records module content without recording transport and heavy lift will flatter every modular plant it touches.

## How do you read the spend curve against the benchmark?

Compare spend to date against the sanction curve, then let earned value tell you which of the two possible readings is true.

At month 30 of a 60-month programme, the sanction curve says 46% of installed cost should be spent. Actual spend is 51%. On its own that says the project is ahead, or it says money has gone out without work coming in, and the curve cannot distinguish them.

Add earned value at 45%. On a BAC of US$16.8bn: PV = 0.46 × 16.8 = **US$7.73bn**, AC = 0.51 × 16.8 = **US$8.57bn**, EV = 0.45 × 16.8 = **US$7.56bn**.

CPI = EV ÷ AC = 7.56 ÷ 8.57 = **0.882**. SPI = EV ÷ PV = 7.56 ÷ 7.73 = **0.978**. Cost variance = 7.56 − 8.57 = −**US$1.01bn**.

EAC = BAC ÷ CPI = 16.8 ÷ 0.882 = **US$19.0bn**, a variance at completion of −US$2.2bn if current performance holds. The plant is close to programme and losing money, which is the signature of acceleration, of a rate assumption that was wrong at sanction, or of both.

Report by train and by area as well as in total. A blended index on a two-train job hides the train that is in trouble, and a two-train job usually has one.

## How does the cost forecast turn into reported revenue?

Through the five steps the applicable revenue standard sets out, described here in PCI's own words rather than reproduced.

First, identify the contract with the customer and satisfy yourself it is enforceable and that collection is probable. Second, identify the performance obligations in it, which for an LNG EPC contract usually means deciding whether integrated engineering, procurement and construction form one combined obligation or several distinct ones.

Third, determine the transaction price, including variable amounts such as incentives, milestone bonuses and liquidated damages, constrained to the amount that is highly probable not to reverse later. Fourth, allocate that price across the obligations identified.

Fifth, recognise revenue as each obligation is satisfied, over time where the standard's criteria are met, measured by an input or an output method that faithfully depicts progress.

Where the input method is cost-based, the arithmetic runs straight off the forecast. On a **US$4.2bn** contract with **US$1.47bn** of cost incurred and **US$3.9bn** of total expected cost: 1.47 ÷ 3.9 = **37.7%**, so revenue recognised is 0.377 × 4.2 = **US$1.583bn**.

Raise the total expected cost to **US$4.15bn** and the same costs incurred now measure 1.47 ÷ 4.15 = **35.4%**, so revenue becomes **US$1.488bn**. Around **US$95m** of revenue already reported reverses in the period the forecast moves.

If expected costs reach US$4.35bn against a price of US$4.2bn, the contract is loss-making, and an expected loss of that kind is generally recognised in full as soon as it becomes apparent rather than spread across the remaining programme. Nothing here is accounting advice; the point is that a cost engineer's forecast movement is a reporting event.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is cost per tonne comparable between regions?**
Only within a class. Labour rates, module content, ambient conditions, seismic and cryogenic code requirements, marine scope and local content rules all move the rate, and a brownfield expansion starts from a different base than a greenfield plant with its own jetty and camp. Compare within the class and state the class, or you are ranking geographies rather than projects.

**Should interest during construction sit inside the benchmark?**
Keep it outside the capital cost benchmark and inside the funding case, then state clearly which you are quoting. Financing cost depends on the capital structure and the drawdown profile, neither of which says anything about how well the plant was built. A benchmark that includes it measures the deal as much as the project.

**What is a useful early warning on an LNG build?**
Contingency drawn against physical progress, tracked monthly from the first quarter. If 60% of the cover has gone at 35% complete, the ratio is 1.7 and the cover runs out around 35 + (40 ÷ 1.7) = 58% complete. That division turns a comfortable balance into a date, which is what a sponsor can act on.

**How should commissioning be benchmarked?**
By systems turned over against plan, and by punch list ageing, not by cost. The construction spend curve is nearly flat through commissioning while the completion date, and therefore every financing and revenue consequence, is still being decided. A metric set that stops at construction stops one step before the expensive part.

**Can a model select comparable plants for you?**
It can shortlist candidates from a database far faster than an analyst, and it will confidently propose plants whose boundaries do not match. Score any such tool on a validation set the cost team has already reviewed, report precision and recall, and keep human review on the survivors. Speed in shortlisting is worth having; unmeasured judgement is not.
