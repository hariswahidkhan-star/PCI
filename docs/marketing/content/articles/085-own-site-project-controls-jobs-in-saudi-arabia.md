# Project controls jobs in Saudi Arabia: the giga-projects

The Saudi giga-programmes hire project controls people because programme-scale delivery fails at the seams between contracts rather than inside them. What they are buying is consolidation: one cost position and one schedule position across dozens of contractors reporting in different formats on different cycles.

That is a different job from project controls on a single contract, and it is the reason experienced planners and cost engineers arrive and still find the first six months hard.

## Where do project controls jobs in Saudi Arabia come from?

They come from the delivery of publicly announced programmes under Vision 2030, the national transformation programme published by the Saudi government. NEOM, Qiddiya, Diriyah, Red Sea Global, Roshn and King Salman International Airport are all publicly announced developments, alongside the transport, utilities and industrial work that has to exist for any of them to open.

One caution before you plan a career around a headline. The capital values quoted for these programmes are developer and government statements of intent, not audited spend, and giga-programmes everywhere are rescoped, resequenced and rephased as they mature.

Treat the announcements as a signal that programme-scale controls capability is being bought, which is verifiable from the roles being advertised, rather than as a promise about any one scheme's final size.

## What changes between project scale and programme scale

| Dimension | Project scale | Programme scale |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | One schedule, one cost breakdown, one owner | Many contractor baselines that must reconcile to one programme baseline |
| Cost breakdown | Aligned to one contract | A programme structure that every contract must map into, usually imperfectly |
| Reporting cycle | One cut-off, one data date | Contractors on different cut-offs, forced into a single reporting calendar |
| Critical path | One network | Interface paths between contracts, where the float belongs to nobody |
| Forecast | One method, one estimator | A method that survives being applied to packages at different maturities |
| What fails | Execution inside the package | Handovers, interfaces, enabling works and the assumptions between contracts |
| What you are asked | Is this contract on track? | Which of forty things do we fix first, and what does that do to the funding profile? |

The float on an interface is the classic programme failure. Neither contractor owns it, both consume it, and it disappears while every individual package still reports green.

## The roll-up arithmetic a programme role is tested on

Earned value at programme scale is the same arithmetic applied to a consolidated position, and it is where most interviews for these roles go.

**Planned value (PV)** is the budgeted cost of the work scheduled to be done by the data date. **Earned value (EV)** is the budgeted cost of the work actually done.

**Actual cost (AC)** is what was spent doing it. Each definition stands alone, which is why the three can be read in any order.

Take a programme of three packages at one data date, in SAR million.

| Package | PV | EV | AC | CPI (EV ÷ AC) | SPI (EV ÷ PV) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A — enabling works | 40 | 36 | 42 | 0.857 | 0.900 |
| B — utilities | 30 | 31 | 29 | 1.069 | 1.033 |
| C — main package | 30 | 24 | 33 | 0.727 | 0.800 |
| **Programme** | **100** | **91** | **104** | **0.875** | **0.910** |

Programme schedule variance is EV − PV = 91 − 100 = **−9**. Cost variance is EV − AC = 91 − 104 = **−13**.

Programme cost performance index is 91 ÷ 104 = **0.875**, and schedule performance index is 91 ÷ 100 = **0.910**.

Now read what the roll-up hides. The programme reports 0.875, while package C is running at 0.727 and package B is subsidising the average at 1.069.

If you report only the consolidated line, the decision gets made about a programme that is 12.5% over on cost, when the actual decision needed is about one package that is 27% over. That is the single most common reporting failure at this scale, and it is why programme roles ask for the package detail behind every consolidated figure.

## The four EAC methods and what each assumes

Estimate at completion is the forecast of total cost. Budget at completion (BAC) for this programme is **SAR 400m**, so the remaining work is BAC − EV = 400 − 91 = **SAR 309m** of budgeted value still to earn.

| Method | Formula | This programme | What it assumes | When it is the right one |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| Remaining work at budget | EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) | 104 + 309 = **413** | The overrun to date was a one-off and will not repeat | An identified, closed cause: a one-time settlement, a corrected error |
| Remaining work at current CPI | EAC = BAC ÷ CPI | 400 ÷ 0.875 = **457.1** | Cost performance to date continues unchanged | The default when the causes are systemic and unresolved |
| Remaining work at CPI × SPI | EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI) | 104 + 309 ÷ 0.79625 = **492.1** | Schedule pressure will add cost, so lateness compounds the overrun | Fixed end date, acceleration likely, time-related cost significant |
| Bottom-up re-estimate | EAC = AC + a re-estimated ETC | Whatever the estimate says | Past performance no longer predicts the remaining scope | After a rebaseline, a major change, or a change of contractor |

The same data produces **413 to 492**, a spread of SAR 79m. The method is not a detail buried in the appendix; it is most of the answer.

Two supporting numbers finish the picture. Variance at completion on the CPI method is BAC − EAC = 400 − 457.1 = **−57.1**.

And the to-complete performance index, the efficiency the remaining work must run at to land on budget, is (BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC) = 309 ÷ 296 = **1.044**.

So the programme must run the rest of the work at 1.044 having demonstrated 0.875. State that as a ratio in a steering meeting and the recovery conversation becomes concrete instead of aspirational.

## Where the money is actually lost

A programme forecast does not stop at the cost report. It lands in a funding drawdown, in revenue recognition for every contractor in the chain, and in the cash position that decides whether the supply chain stays solvent.

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy, and almost never on an interface float. An engineer is examined on progress measurement and float, and almost never on cut-off or a contract asset.

Programme controls sits exactly on that boundary. The person who can say that package C's slip moves a certification milestone past the period end, which moves recognised revenue and delays a payment two quarters, is doing the job these programmes are hiring for.

## What Saudi Arabia adds to the job

Working here brings requirements that have nothing to do with planning technique, and they are the part candidates most often underestimate.

**Professional registration.** Engineers working in Saudi Arabia are generally required to register with the Saudi Council of Engineers, with qualifications verified as part of the process. Requirements and processing times change, so confirm the current position with the employer and the Council before you resign anything.

**Saudization.** The Nitaqat framework sets nationalisation targets by sector and firm size, and it shapes which roles are open to expatriate hire and which are not. It also means genuine demand for developing Saudi controls professionals, which is a growing part of senior job descriptions.

**Attestation and mobilisation.** Degree attestation, medicals, visa processing and client approval of your CV run in series more often than in parallel. Three to four months from offer to start is normal, and it is longer if a client approval sits in the chain.

**Contract forms and language.** FIDIC-based forms are common, with heavy client amendments, and reporting is frequently bilingual. Records discipline matters more here than tool skill, because entitlement is decided by what you recorded at the time.

**Location.** Much of the work is remote or camp-based rather than in Riyadh or Jeddah, on rotation. That is a lifestyle decision as much as a career one, and it should be priced before it is accepted.

## What gets people hired, and what gets them stuck

Hired: evidence of consolidating multiple contractors into one position, a forecast you chose the method for and defended, and the ability to explain an interface delay to somebody who does not read schedules.

Stuck: deep skill in one tool and one package type, reporting that has never been challenged by an external party, and a career spent entirely on one side of the client-contractor line.

The fastest correction is exposure rather than another course. Ask for the consolidation work, the interface register and the forecast review, even where the title does not change.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need Gulf experience to get hired on a Saudi programme?**
It shortens the conversation but it is not a hard requirement at senior level, where programme-scale consolidation experience from anywhere is scarcer than regional familiarity. Below that, employers weight in-region experience heavily because mobilisation cost and time are real. Lead with the specific capability the role names rather than with years.

**Is the work in Riyadh?**
Some of it. A large share of giga-programme delivery is on remote sites, run on rotation from camp accommodation, with the corporate and client functions in Riyadh or Jeddah. Confirm the actual location, the rotation pattern and whether family status is available before you compare two offers, because those terms change the value of a package more than the headline does.

**How stable are these programmes?**
The programmes are real and under construction, and individual schemes are regularly rephased, rescoped or paused as priorities move. That is normal at this scale rather than a warning sign. Protect yourself with a contract you have read, a notice period you can live with, and skills that transfer to the next programme.

**What is different about programme controls compared with project controls?**
Scope of the question. Project controls answers whether one contract is on track; programme controls answers which of many problems to fix first and what that does to the funding profile. The technical arithmetic is the same, and the difficulty moves into reconciliation, interfaces and the assumptions between contracts.

**Which credential helps most for these roles?**
The one that closes your evidenced gap. Planners moving into programme roles usually need the finance and reporting half, because the forecast now lands in a drawdown and in somebody's accounts. Cost professionals usually need network logic and interface analysis. Compare scope and syllabus rather than brand.
