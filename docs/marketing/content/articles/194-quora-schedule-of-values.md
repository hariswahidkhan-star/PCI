# What is a schedule of values in a construction contract?

A schedule of values is an agreed breakdown of the contract sum into priced line items, used each period to value the work done and support the payment application. It is a pricing document, not a cost document: the line values are what the client will pay, not what the work costs the contractor.

The line values must add up to the contract sum exactly. That constraint is what makes it a valuation tool rather than an estimate.

## What goes into a schedule of values?

Each line carries a description tied to a definable piece of work, a value, the percentage complete claimed this period, and the value earned. Most also carry the previous period's figures so the movement is visible.

Lines follow how the work will actually be certified, which is usually by trade or by element: preliminaries, substructure, frame, envelope, services, commissioning. A schedule of values with twelve lines on a two-year job cannot be certified honestly, and one with six hundred cannot be certified at all.

Preliminaries and site set-up sit as their own line and are usually earned on a time basis rather than against physical output. That single line causes more valuation arguments than the rest of the document combined.

## How does a valuation actually work?

Take a £4.00m contract at the end of month five.

| Line | Value | % complete | Value earned |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Preliminaries and site set-up | £320,000 | 45% | £144,000 |
| Piling | £640,000 | 100% | £640,000 |
| Substructure | £780,000 | 70% | £546,000 |
| Frame and envelope | £1,240,000 | 25% | £310,000 |
| MEP first fix | £700,000 | 10% | £70,000 |
| Commissioning and handover | £320,000 | 0% | £0 |
| **Total** | **£4,000,000** | **42.75%** | **£1,710,000** |

The arithmetic: 144 + 640 + 546 + 310 + 70 = £1,710,000, and 1,710,000 ÷ 4,000,000 = 42.75%.

Now build the payment. Add unfixed materials on site of £85,000, giving £1,795,000. Deduct retention at 5% of the measured work value, 5% × 1,710,000 = £85,500, which by common practice is not held against unfixed materials.

Net certified to date is 1,795,000 − 85,500 = £1,709,500. Less £1,290,000 previously certified, the payment due this period is **£419,500**.

## How does it differ from a bill of quantities or a WBS?

| Document | Whose number | Basis | Used for | What it cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schedule of values | Contractor, agreed with client | Price | Valuing work and certifying payment | Whether the work is profitable |
| Bill of quantities | Client's quantity surveyor | Price per measured unit | Tendering and re-measurement | Progress, without a separate valuation |
| WBS / cost breakdown structure | Contractor, internal | Cost | Budgeting, cost capture, forecasting | What the client owes |
| Performance measurement baseline | Contractor, internal | Budgeted cost, time-phased | Earned value and variance analysis | Cash |

The middle two columns explain why a valuation and an earned value report disagree in a normal month. One is measured in price and the other in budgeted cost, so they can only be reconciled through the margin, never compared directly.

A team that reports the schedule of values percentage as "percent complete" to a board has quietly reported margin as progress. On a front-end loaded schedule, that overstates completion every month until the final third.

## What is front-end loading, and how do you spot it?

Front-end loading means shifting value into early lines so that cash arrives sooner than the work is done. It is not fraud, and on a cash-hungry job it is normal commercial behaviour, but it changes what the valuation means.

Suppose preliminaries were priced at £420,000 rather than £320,000, with £100,000 taken out of the frame line. At month five, preliminaries earn 45% × 420,000 = £189,000 instead of £144,000, up £45,000. The frame line earns 25% × 1,140,000 = £285,000 instead of £310,000, down £25,000.

Net effect: £20,000 more certified for identical work, and the contract sum unchanged at £4.00m. Repeated across several lines, that is how a job can look 47% valued and be 41% built.

The test is simple. Compare the shape of the schedule of values against the shape of the cost estimate, line by line. Where the ratio between them varies widely across lines, the pricing has been shaped, and the valuation percentage should not be used as a progress measure.

## Where does the valuation meet the accounts?

A valuation certifies what may be billed. It does not, by itself, determine revenue, and treating the two as the same thing is one of the most common month-end errors on a construction contract.

Where progress towards a performance obligation is measured by a cost-based input method, the measure is costs incurred divided by total expected costs. On this job, take £1.58m of cost incurred against a forecast total cost of £3.62m: 1.58 ÷ 3.62 = 43.6%, so cumulative revenue is 43.6% × £4.00m = **£1.746m**.

Gross value certified to date is £1.795m. The £49,000 excess is billing ahead of revenue, and it sits as a contract liability rather than as profit.

Retention behaves differently again. The £85,500 withheld is a timing difference in cash, not a reduction in revenue, so it sits as an amount receivable until release. Treatment depends on the contract and the reporting framework applied, and nothing PCI publishes is accounting advice.

That gap between what you may bill and what you may recognise is the whole reason PCI examines finance and delivery together. ## Frequently asked questions

**Who prepares the schedule of values?**
The contractor prepares it and the client or the client's quantity surveyor agrees it, usually before the first payment application and often as a condition of it. Agreeing it late is a mistake, because by then the contractor has an interest in a shape that suits their cash position and the client has little room to argue.

**How detailed should the line items be?**
Detailed enough that each line can be assessed on site without argument, and no more. A useful test is whether two competent surveyors walking the job separately would agree the percentage on each line to within a few points. If they would not, the line covers too much scope and needs splitting.

**Can the schedule of values be changed mid-contract?**
Line values change only through agreed variations, because the total must continue to equal the adjusted contract sum. Re-shaping existing lines without a variation is a request to be paid earlier for the same work, and it should be handled as the cash conversation it is rather than as an administrative update.

**Is a schedule of values the same as earned value?**
No. A schedule of values earns price against agreed line values; earned value earns budgeted cost against a performance measurement baseline. They answer different questions — what the client owes, and how the project is performing — and they will differ every month by roughly the margin in the priced lines.

**How is retention handled in the valuation?**
Retention is deducted as a percentage of the certified work value, commonly 5%, with release typically split between practical completion and the end of the defects period. It reduces cash received, not the value earned, so a schedule of values shows gross value and the payment certificate shows the deduction.

**What about materials on site that are not yet installed?**
Where the contract permits it, unfixed materials properly delivered and protected are added to the valuation, often at cost and often excluded from retention. They are not progress: adding them to a percentage complete claim overstates the work done, which is why they belong as a separate line on the certificate rather than inside a percentage.
