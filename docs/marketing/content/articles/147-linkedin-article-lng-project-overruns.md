# LNG project overruns: the causes and the arithmetic

LNG project overruns are usually reported as a rise in a headline dollar total, which is the least informative way to read them. Capacity, train count, scope boundary and price base all move between the first published figure and the outturn. Cost per tonne of capacity is the only comparison that survives.

Written for LinkedIn as an original. It sits under the Institute's project controls fundamentals pillar.

## Why do LNG project overruns look so large in headlines?

Because liquefaction plants are among the largest single capital commitments in industry, and a percentage of a very large number is a very large number. The percentage itself is often measuring two different plants.

PCI does not publish an industry overrun figure for this sector, because the published numbers are built on incompatible boundaries. Before comparing anything, establish what each total contains.

| Boundary question | Why it changes the number |
|---|---|
| How many trains, and at what nameplate capacity? | A total that rose because a third train was added is not an overrun |
| Does it include upstream wells and the feed pipeline? | Integrated projects and tolling plants are not comparable totals |
| Does it include jetty, storage, accommodation and the LNG carriers? | Marine scope is a large share and is often financed separately |
| Does it include owner's costs and interest during construction? | These can be excluded at sanction and included in the outturn |
| What price base and exchange rate? | An estimate in one year's money against an outturn in another's overstates growth |

## What is the cost per tonne calculation, and why does it settle arguments?

Cost per tonne of annual capacity is total capital cost divided by nameplate capacity in tonnes a year. It normalises for size, which is the variable that makes headline totals useless.

Project A: **US$14.0bn** for **8.4 Mtpa** gives 14,000 ÷ 8.4 = **US$1,667 per tonne**.

Project B: **US$21.0bn** for **15.6 Mtpa** gives 21,000 ÷ 15.6 = **US$1,346 per tonne**. The larger total is the cheaper plant, by 19%.

Now take Project A to an outturn of **US$17.5bn**, with capacity debottlenecked to **9.3 Mtpa** along the way. The headline says 17.5 ÷ 14.0 = **25% over budget**.

Per tonne it is 17,500 ÷ 9.3 = **US$1,882**, against 1,667, which is **12.9%**. Roughly half the reported overrun bought capacity the original number did not include.

Those figures are illustrative arithmetic, not project data. Run the same two divisions on any pair of real published totals before repeating either of them.

## Is interest during construction an overrun?

Not in itself. Interest during construction is the financing cost of money drawn while the asset is being built, and it is a planned line in every project-financed structure. It becomes an overrun when the schedule extends and nobody re-runs the calculation.

Take **US$9.0bn** of debt drawn evenly across four years at **7%**. The average drawn balance is roughly half the peak, so about **US$4.5bn**, giving 4.5 × 0.07 = **US$315m a year**, or about **US$1.26bn** over the drawdown period. Real facilities draw on an S-curve rather than a straight line, so [how the drawdown curve is modelled](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/project-cash-flow-forecasting) moves that figure before the programme does.

Now delay completion by twelve months with the facility fully drawn. Holding **US$9.0bn** for an extra year at 7% adds **US$630m** — half the entire planned financing cost of the build, from one year of slip.

This is why lenders' technical advisers care about the programme in a way that owners' teams sometimes find excessive. On a financed asset, schedule is a cost line with a compounding rate attached.

## What does a year of deferred first cargo actually cost?

It costs the margin that year's production would have earned, and that number usually dwarfs the construction variance that caused it.

Take a **4.5 Mtpa** train. Using the common industry approximation of about **52 MMBtu per tonne**, a year of output is 4.5m × 52 = **234 million MMBtu**.

At an illustrative netback of **US$4 per MMBtu**, deferred gross margin is 234m × 4 = **US$936m**. Against a construction variance of, say, US$300m, the delay consequence is three times the cost consequence.

That ratio is the argument for spending money to protect the completion date, and it is the argument the cost report alone will never make. A cost-only dashboard optimises the smaller number.

## Why must trains be measured separately in earned value?

Because a blended performance index hides the train that is in trouble, and a two-train job almost always has one.

At a common data date, in US$bn:

| Scope | PV | EV | AC | CPI | SPI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Train 1 | 3.15 | 3.20 | 3.30 | 0.970 | 1.016 |
| Train 2 | 1.35 | 1.10 | 1.45 | 0.759 | 0.815 |
| **Blended** | **4.50** | **4.30** | **4.75** | **0.905** | **0.956** |

Cost performance index is EV ÷ AC, so Train 2 is at 1.10 ÷ 1.45 = **0.759**: every dollar spent is buying 76 cents of planned work. Schedule performance index is EV ÷ PV, at 1.10 ÷ 1.35 = **0.815**.

Blended, the job reports a CPI of 4.30 ÷ 4.75 = **0.905** and an SPI of 4.30 ÷ 4.50 = **0.956**, which looks recoverable. Train 1 is subsidising the report while Train 2 sets the completion date.

Cost variance is EV − AC, so Train 2 alone is **−US$350m**. Forecasting the job from the blended index would understate the position by a wide margin, and the fix is structural: report by train, by area and by contract, never only in total.

## Where does an overrun hit the accounts first?

On the contractor's side, before it appears anywhere in the owner's cost report.

Where revenue is recognised over time using an input measure — cost incurred divided by total forecast cost — a rise in total forecast cost immediately reduces the percentage complete. Revenue recognised in prior periods is corrected in the period the forecast changes.

Where the expected costs of finishing a contract exceed the economic benefits still to be received, accounting requirements for onerous contracts oblige the contractor to provide for the whole expected loss at once, rather than bleeding it out month by month. A lump-sum turnkey LNG contract is one of the few places this genuinely happens, and it happens in a single reporting period.

Working capital moves at the same time. Unbilled work and retention grow while payables stay on their normal terms, so a project can be forecasting an acceptable margin and still be the reason the group draws on its revolving facility.

## Which causes can still be influenced after FID?

Sanction closes most of the exposure, but not all of it. The table separates what is already decided from what a controls team can still move.

| Cause | When it is effectively decided | Leading indicator | What still works after sanction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design maturity at sanction | Before FID | Engineering deliverables issued for construction against plan | Nothing structural; manage the consequences honestly |
| Module fabrication yard productivity | At contract award | Hours per tonne of steel, against the yard's own history | Alternative yards for later modules, resequencing, early inspection |
| Site labour availability and rate | Rolling | Recruitment against the mobilisation curve, turnover, overtime hours | Camp capacity, rotation design, scope shifted to modules |
| Brownfield or interface tie-ins | Rolling | Permits issued against permits requested | Shutdown planning, isolation strategy, pre-fabrication |
| Commissioning readiness | Late, but decisive | Systems turned over against plan, punch list ageing | Early systems completion discipline, dedicated commissioning team |
| Escalation and currency | At the estimate's price base | Published index against the price base assumption | Hedging, index-linked contract terms, re-forecasting the assumption |

Commissioning is the row most often underestimated. The construction cost curve is nearly flat by then, but the completion date, and therefore the financing and revenue consequences above, is decided in that window.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is cost per tonne comparable between regions?**
Only with care. Labour rates, module content, ambient conditions, marine scope and local content requirements all move the number, and a brownfield expansion on an existing site starts from a different base than a greenfield plant with its own jetty and camp. Cost per tonne is the right normaliser within a comparable class of project, not a global league table.

**How should contingency be reported on a multi-train job?**
Separately for each train, plus a project-level pool held by the owner. A single blended contingency lets an early train consume the cover intended for the later one, and it hides the drawdown rate that is the most useful early indicator. Report contingency drawn against progress achieved, not against time elapsed.

**Why do schedule and cost forecasts disagree so often?**
Usually because they are produced at different data dates from different systems, by people whose incentives differ. Fixing it is procedural rather than technical: one data date, one progress measurement rule, and a single reconciliation showing how the schedule forecast maps to the cost forecast. If the two cannot be reconciled on one page, neither should be issued.

**Does the owner still need earned value if the contractor reports it?**
Yes. The contractor's earned value measures the contractor's scope against the contractor's baseline, which excludes owner's costs, free-issue items, interface scope and financing. Owners who rely solely on contractor reporting discover the gap at handover, when the remaining scope is theirs and nobody has been measuring it.
