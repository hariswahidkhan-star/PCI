# How to build a FIDIC fully detailed claim in 84 days

A FIDIC fully detailed claim is the submission that follows a notice of claim: the event described, the contractual basis relied on, the contemporary records, and the particulars of time and money claimed. Under the 2017 second editions it is due within 84 days of the day the claiming party knew, or should have known, of the event.

*Written first for this newsletter. PCI publishes certification requirements, not law, and nothing here is legal advice. No contract wording is reproduced; the procedure is described in PCI's own words, and Particular Conditions routinely amend it, so read your own contract before relying on any period below.*

## What is a FIDIC fully detailed claim?

It is the substantiated claim, as distinct from the notice that preserved the right to make it. The notice says an event has occurred. The fully detailed claim proves entitlement and quantifies it.

The 2017 forms expect four components: a detailed description of the event or circumstance, a statement of the contractual and any other legal basis, the contemporary records the claim relies on, and detailed supporting particulars of the extension of time or additional payment claimed.

Two of those four are documents that must already exist. Contemporary records cannot be created in week eleven, and a claim built from reconstructed records is the one that gets rejected on the merits.

## When does the clock start, and on what?

On the date of awareness, and this is the single most expensive misunderstanding in the procedure.

| Step | Period | Runs from |
|---|---|---|
| Notice of claim | 28 days | The date the claiming party became aware, or should have become aware, of the event |
| Fully detailed claim | 84 days | The same awareness date, not the date the notice was served |
| Continuing events | Interim claims, typically monthly | The event's effect continuing, with a final claim after it ends |
| Engineer's response | 42 days in the unamended forms | Receipt of the fully detailed claim |

Because both periods run from the same date, serving the notice on day 27 leaves 57 days for the submission, not 84. Teams that diary the 84 days from the notice lose a month before they start.

The deeming provision is the second trap. If the statement of the contractual or legal basis is not delivered inside the 84 days, the notice of claim is treated as having lapsed, subject to the Engineer's response and to anything the parties agree. The basis is the part that must land on time.

## How do you build the submission?

Seven steps, in this order, starting the day the notice goes out.

1. **Fix the awareness date and write it down.** Record who knew what, when, and from which document. Every period in the procedure runs from this date, so it is the first thing an opponent will attack.

2. **Open the record file immediately.** Allocation sheets, daily diaries, dated photographs, progress measurement, plant returns and correspondence, filed against the event reference rather than by date.

3. **State the contractual basis clause by clause.** Name the sub-clause relied on, state what it requires, and show that the facts meet it. A claim that describes a hardship without identifying a contractual route is a complaint.

4. **Prove the delay against the programme.** Use an accepted programme, a defined data date and a named analysis method, and show the critical path before and after the event.

5. **Prove the money from actual cost.** Take prolongation from what the site actually cost in the delay period, and disruption from measured productivity, not from tender allowances.

6. **Link cause to effect, record by record.** Each head of claim should trace to specific records. Global claims, which lump every event against every cost, are the easiest form to defeat.

7. **Submit inside the period, and keep the transmittal proof.** Serve it in the manner and to the address the contract specifies. A submission emailed to the wrong recipient is not a submission.

## How do you prove the time?

By showing that the event consumed the float and then moved the completion date, with the analysis method named and applied consistently.

Take late access to Area 3, given **34 days** after the date the contract required. At the data date before the event, the successor activity had **12 days** of total float.

The first 12 days are absorbed by float, so 34 − 12 = **22 days** of critical delay. Inserting the event into the accepted programme moves completion from 14 November to 6 December, which is the same 22 days arrived at by calculation rather than by argument.

Total float is what makes that arithmetic honest. A 34-day event on an activity with 40 days of float delays nothing, and the same event on a zero-float activity delays everything behind it.

| Analysis method | What it needs | When it is credible |
|---|---|---|
| Impacted as-planned | The baseline programme and the event | Early claims where little has been built; weakest evidentially |
| Time impact analysis | An updated programme at the data date before each event | Prospective assessment of discrete events, the common contractual fit |
| Windows or time slice | Regular accepted updates through the period | Long events, multiple causes, concurrent delay |
| As-planned versus as-built | A reliable as-built record | Retrospective claims where the programme updates are poor |
| Collapsed as-built | A complete, logic-linked as-built programme | Rare, because the as-built logic is usually a reconstruction |

Name the method in the submission and say why it suits the event, which is the harder half of [choosing and defending a delay analysis method](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/delay-analysis-techniques). Switching method between events in one claim invites the argument that the method was chosen for its answer.

## How do you prove the money?

In three separate parts, each with its own evidence, never as a single lump.

**Prolongation.** Time-related site costs across the period of delay, taken from actual cost in that window. If site establishment, supervision and standing plant ran at **£412,000 a month**, the daily rate is 412,000 ÷ 30.4 = **£13,553**, so 22 days of delay is 13,553 × 22 = **£298,166**.

Assess it in the window where the delay occurred rather than at the end of the job, because site costs are not flat and the difference is usually material.

**Disruption.** Use a measured mile: compare productivity in an unaffected area with the same work in the affected one. An unaffected area achieved **1.42 metres of duct per labour hour**; the affected area achieved **0.96** over **18,400 metres**.

Hours actually used: 18,400 ÷ 0.96 = **19,167**. Hours at the unimpacted rate: 18,400 ÷ 1.42 = **12,958**. Lost hours: **6,209**, and at £46 an hour that is **£285,614**.

**Additional direct costs.** Extra plant standing, re-mobilisation of a crew, storage, acceleration where instructed. Here, £64,000 and £41,000 respectively.

| Head of claim | Basis | Amount |
|---|---|---:|
| Prolongation | 22 days at £13,553 a day, from actual site cost | £298,166 |
| Disruption | 6,209 lost hours at £46, measured mile | £285,614 |
| Additional direct costs | Plant standing and crew re-mobilisation | £105,000 |
| **Total** | | **£688,780** |

Head office overhead is the fourth head people reach for, usually through one of the well-known recovery formulas. Treat it carefully, because formula-based recovery is frequently challenged and evidence of a real lost opportunity is worth more than an arithmetic convention.

## What gets a claim rejected?

Rarely the merits. Usually the process, and usually something that was decided months earlier.

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Notice late, or served on the wrong party | The entitlement may be lost before the analysis begins |
| Contractual basis omitted from the 84-day submission | The notice can be treated as lapsed |
| Records reconstructed after the event | The causal link fails, whatever the arithmetic says |
| Delay analysed against a programme nobody accepted | The time case rests on a document the other party can disown |
| Costs taken from the tender rather than from actuals | The quantum is treated as a re-pricing exercise |
| Every event lumped into one global figure | Any single weak event contaminates the whole claim |

The remedy for all six is the same and it is unglamorous: a records system that runs from day one, and a claims register with the awareness date, the notice date and the 84-day date on every line.

That habit is what PCI examines. ## Frequently asked questions

**Does the 84 days run from the notice or from the event?**
From the date the claiming party knew or should have known of the event, which is the same start point as the 28-day notice period. Serving the notice late therefore eats into the 84 days rather than extending them. Diary both dates from awareness on the day the event is identified, and put them in the claims register rather than in an individual's calendar.

**What happens if the notice was served late?**
The 2017 procedure lets the Engineer respond to a late notice within a defined period, and the position can be argued, but the claiming party starts from a much weaker place. Serve the notice even when the entitlement looks uncertain. A notice costs an afternoon; the alternative is arguing about the consequences of not having served one.

**Is an extension of time worth money by itself?**
No. Time and money are separate entitlements with separate tests, and an event can give one without the other. An extension protects against delay damages; recovering prolongation cost requires showing the cost was actually incurred because of that event in that period. Present them as two cases in one submission, never as one case.

**How should concurrent delay be handled?**
Analyse it rather than assert it. Show which delays were genuinely critical in the same window, using consistent updates, and then apply the treatment your contract and its governing law require. Concurrency is a question about what actually drove the completion date, and it is answered with programme updates and records, not with a paragraph of argument.

**Can an AI tool draft the submission?**
It can assemble a chronology, index records against an event and draft descriptive sections, which removes real effort from a fixed 84-day window. It cannot decide the contractual basis or select the delay analysis method, and that boundary is exactly what [a policy naming the decisions a model may not take](https://pciai.org/ai-policy-for-project-controls) is for; its output must also be checked against the records it cites. Any tool used should be named in the claim file, with the human who verified it.
