# IFRS for project controls: the standards that decide profit

IFRS for project controls comes down to a short list of standards. IFRS 15 sets when revenue may be recognised, IAS 37 forces the whole loss on an onerous contract into a single period, IAS 16 and IAS 23 govern what may be capitalised, and IFRS 16 covers leased site plant. A cost forecast feeds four of them.

Nothing below is accounting advice. It describes, in the Institute's own words, what these standards do, so that a project controls professional understands what their numbers are used for after they leave the cost report.

The reason to read it is narrow and practical. The estimate at completion you revise on a Tuesday afternoon is an input to reported profit, and almost nobody tells cost engineers that. [What a forecast movement does to revenue and margin](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/eac-accounting) is worked line by line elsewhere; this piece is about the standards it lands in.

## Which standards does IFRS for project controls actually cover?

| Standard | What it governs | What project controls supplies | Where it bites |
|---|---|---|---|
| IFRS 15 | Revenue from contracts with customers | Cost to date, estimate at completion, progress measurement | Revenue moves whenever the forecast moves |
| IAS 37 | Provisions, onerous contracts, contingent items | Forecast final cost against contract value | An expected loss is booked in full, at once |
| IAS 16 | Property, plant and equipment | The split of what is directly attributable | What sits on the balance sheet rather than the income statement |
| IAS 23 | Borrowing costs | Construction period and spend profile | Interest capitalised only while the asset is being readied |
| IFRS 16 | Leases | Plant hire, accommodation, long-term equipment | Three weeks is a cost; thirty months may be an asset and a liability |
| IAS 21 | Effects of foreign exchange rates | Currency of committed purchase orders | Rate movement between commitment and payment |
| IAS 8 | Accounting policies and changes in estimates | Any revision to the estimate at completion | A forecast change is a change in estimate, not an error |

Two of these mainly govern the contractor's side and two the owner's side. The rest apply to both, and every one of them is fed by numbers a cost engineer produces.

IFRS 18 changes how an income statement is presented for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027. It alters presentation rather than measurement, so none of the arithmetic below moves because of it.

## The IFRS 15 five-step model, in the Institute's own words

IFRS 15 replaced the old construction-contract approach with one model built on the promises inside a contract. The five steps run as follows.

**Step one: identify the contract.** An agreement approved by both parties, with commercial substance, identifiable rights and payment terms, where collection is probable.

**Step two: identify the performance obligations.** The distinct promises inside the contract. On most construction and EPC work the promise is a single integrated outcome, so the whole contract is one obligation.

**Step three: determine the transaction price.** What the entity expects to be entitled to, including variable amounts such as variations, incentives, milestone bonuses and liquidated damages.

**Step four: allocate the price to the obligations.** Where more than one exists, the price is split by relative standalone selling price rather than by whatever the contract's schedule of rates happens to say.

**Step five: recognise revenue as each obligation is satisfied.** Either over time as the work progresses, or at a point in time when control passes.

Step five is where project controls is pulled into financial reporting, and step three is where a good change log is worth more than an opinion.

## When revenue may be recognised over time

Over-time recognition applies when any one of three conditions holds. Described plainly: the customer receives and consumes the benefit as the work is performed; or the customer controls the asset as it is created or enhanced; or the asset has no alternative use to the contractor and there is an enforceable right to payment for work completed to date.

Most on-site construction relies on the second condition, because the works are being built on the customer's land. Bespoke plant and one-off structures usually rely on the third.

The third condition is the one that fails on a technicality. An enforceable right to payment means payment for work performed plus a reasonable margin, not bare recovery of cost, and a termination-for-convenience clause that pays cost only can push an entire scope to point-in-time recognition.

## Measuring progress: input methods and output methods

Once over-time recognition applies, the entity must measure progress, and IFRS 15 permits input methods and output methods. The choice is a policy decision applied consistently to similar contracts.

**Input methods** measure the effort going in. Cost-to-cost dominates: progress equals costs incurred divided by total forecast costs.

**Output methods** measure what has come out. Surveys of work performed, verified quantities installed, physical progress against a rules-of-credit table.

| Measure of progress | What it counts | Strength | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost-to-cost (input) | Cost incurred over total forecast cost | Auditable, and the data already exists | Treats money spent as work done |
| Labour hours (input) | Hours expended over hours forecast | Sound on labour-dominated scope | Ignores materials, plant and subcontract |
| Survey of work performed (output) | Verified physical quantities installed | Closest to what is actually built | Costs time and needs verification discipline |
| Milestones achieved (output) | Discrete completion events | Objective where milestones are real events | Payment milestones are negotiated, not measured |

Cost-to-cost is popular because it is auditable and because the data already exists in the cost ledger. Its weakness is that it treats money spent as work done, which is precisely the assumption earned value management exists to test.

There is a specific correction for that weakness. Where a cost is incurred that is not proportionate to progress, such as materials delivered but not installed, the cost is excluded from the progress measure and the material is recognised at cost with no margin until it is used.

## Worked: a forecast movement that removed £5.6m of profit

Take a fixed-price contract of £100m with an original forecast cost of £80m, so an expected margin of £20m. Costs incurred to date are £40m.

**Position one, forecast £80m.** Progress = 40 ÷ 80 = **50.0%**. Cumulative revenue = 0.50 × 100 = **£50.0m**. Cost recognised £40m. Cumulative margin **£10.0m**.

**Position two, the estimate at completion moves to £90m.** Nothing extra has been spent. A cost engineer has revised a forecast.

Progress = 40 ÷ 90 = **44.4%**. Cumulative revenue = 0.444 × 100 = **£44.4m**. Cost recognised is still £40m, so cumulative margin falls to **£4.4m**.

That is a **£5.6m** reduction in reported profit in one period, produced by a forecast rather than a transaction. The cross-check works: total expected margin is now £10m, and 44.4% of £10m is £4.4m.

This is the single most useful thing a cost engineer can understand about IFRS. The estimate at completion is not an internal management number, and a £10m movement on this contract removed £5.6m of profit before anyone signed a purchase order.

## When the contract turns onerous

**Position three, the estimate at completion moves to £105m.** The contract is now expected to lose £5m across its life, and IAS 37 applies to that expectation.

An onerous contract is one where the unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations exceed the benefits expected from it. Once a contract becomes onerous, the entire expected loss is recognised immediately rather than spread across the remaining work.

Work the arithmetic. Progress = 40 ÷ 105 = **38.1%**, so cumulative revenue is **£38.1m** against costs of £40m, giving a cumulative loss of **£1.9m** through normal measurement.

The full expected loss is £5.0m. The provision required is therefore 5.0 − 1.9 = **£3.1m**, booked in the period the forecast crossed the line.

That is why the month a forecast turns negative is a reporting event, not a project event. A £15m movement in an estimate at completion, in a business running a 10% net margin, removes the profit of a great deal of other work in one journal entry.

## Contract assets, contract liabilities and the thing people call WIP

Cumulative revenue recognised almost never equals cumulative amounts invoiced, and the difference has a name on the balance sheet.

When cumulative revenue exceeds cumulative billings, the entity holds a **contract asset**: work performed for which it does not yet hold an unconditional right to payment. On position two above, revenue of £44.4m against billings of £38m gives a contract asset of **£6.4m**.

When cumulative billings exceed cumulative revenue, the entity holds a **contract liability**: money taken for work not yet delivered. The same revenue against billings of £52m gives a contract liability of **£7.6m**.

The commercial reading is more useful than the accounting one. A large contract liability means front-loaded billing, which helps cash and means the client is funding the work. A large contract asset usually means applications are being disputed, or the schedule of values no longer matches what is being built.

Once the right to payment becomes unconditional it is a receivable instead. That is why a certified application and an uncertified one sit in different places.

## Variations and claims are not revenue until the constraint is passed

Step three includes variable consideration, and step three also constrains it. Variable amounts enter the transaction price only to the extent that it is highly probable a significant reversal of cumulative revenue will not occur later.

The practical effect is blunt. A £4m claim sitting in the cost report as an expected recovery is not £4m of revenue, and booking it early manufactures a reversal for a future period.

What moves a claim across that line is contemporaneous evidence: an instruction, correspondence, a substantiated entitlement, a record of the delay or disruption made while it happened. Project controls produces that evidence, not finance.

Contract modifications have their own treatment. A variation adding distinct scope at its standalone selling price is a separate contract. One that does not, which is most of them, is folded into the existing contract with a cumulative catch-up on revenue already recognised.

## The owner's side: capitalisation, borrowing costs and leases

On the owner's side the question changes from when revenue may be recognised to what may be added to the asset.

IAS 16 allows the purchase price plus costs directly attributable to bringing the asset to the location and condition needed for its intended use. Site preparation, installation, testing and attributable professional fees qualify. General administrative overhead, staff training and the cost of an idle period do not.

IAS 23 requires borrowing costs directly attributable to constructing a qualifying asset to be capitalised while the asset is being readied. Capitalisation is suspended during extended periods of suspended activity and stops when the asset is substantially ready for use.

Depreciation begins when the asset is available for use, which is not the same date as practical completion. A three-month commissioning delay is therefore a schedule event and a depreciation event at once.

IFRS 16 brings most leases onto the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. A tower crane on a thirty-month hire is treated very differently from the same crane taken for three weeks, and getting that right needs the schedule to be right first.

## Why this overlap is where money is lost

A chartered accountant is examined on when revenue may be recognised and what a provision must satisfy. They are almost never examined on a critical path or an earning rule.

An engineer is examined on float and progress measurement. They are almost never examined on cut-off or a contract asset. [How far each controls credential reaches across that boundary](https://credentialfinder.org/best-project-controls-certification) is the question a comparison has to answer.

A project lives in the overlap. The estimate at completion is produced by one profession and consumed, unaltered, by the other, and neither is trained to interrogate what the other did with it.

The failure pattern follows. A forecast stays optimistic for three quarters because nobody wants to be the person who moves it, then it moves £15m in one month and the loss lands in full under IAS 37 in that same month. The engineering event took nine months. The financial event took one.

## Four things a controls professional should be able to produce

An estimate at completion defensible line by line, with the basis of every movement written down.

The percentage complete that estimate implies under cost-to-cost, and the cumulative revenue that follows from it.

The value of any uninstalled materials sitting inside costs incurred, identified separately rather than buried in a control account.

The date on which the forecast final cost first exceeded the contract value, because that date is when IAS 37 started to matter.

Produce those four and the month-end conversation changes from a reconciliation into a decision.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does IFRS 15 still allow percentage of completion?**
Effectively yes, but not by that name and not as a free choice. Where an obligation is satisfied over time, progress must be measured by an input or output method, and cost-to-cost is the input method that most resembles the old approach. The change is that the entity must first prove over-time recognition applies at all.

**Why does revenue fall when I increase the estimate at completion?**
Because cost-to-cost progress is costs incurred divided by total forecast costs, and raising the denominator lowers the fraction. On the example above, moving the forecast from £80m to £90m cut progress from 50% to 44.4% and reversed £5.6m of revenue already reported. No cash moved and no purchase order was signed.

**What makes a contract onerous?**
The unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations exceed the economic benefits expected from it. In project terms, the forecast final cost exceeds the forecast final value, including only variable consideration that has passed the constraint. Once that is true, the whole expected loss is recognised at once rather than as it is incurred.

**Can a pending claim be included in forecast revenue?**
Only to the extent it is highly probable that a significant reversal will not occur, which is a high bar that an optimistic entitlement view does not clear. The claim can and should sit in the commercial forecast used to run the job, while the recognised transaction price stays at the constrained figure until evidence supports more.

**Is this different under US GAAP?**
The revenue model is substantially converged, because IFRS 15 and ASC 606 were developed jointly. Differences remain elsewhere, including aspects of onerous contract treatment and the capitalisation of borrowing costs. Any group reporting under both should confirm its own policy rather than assuming the two answers match.

**Who owns the number in practice?**
Finance owns the entry and project controls owns the estimate behind it. The argument usually starts because nobody wrote that split down. A short, signed forecast basis stating scope, quantities, rates, risk allowance and the date of the underlying data settles most of it before it begins.
