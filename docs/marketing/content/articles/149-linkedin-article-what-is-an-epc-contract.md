# What is an EPC contract? What planners need to know

An EPC contract is one agreement under which a single contractor takes on engineering, procurement and construction, and hands over a facility that works. It is usually lump-sum and turnkey, so the contractor carries most of the cost and schedule risk. That changes what the programme is for.

## What is an EPC contract, exactly?

It is a single-point-responsibility contract. The owner buys a completed, tested facility rather than a set of services, and one party is answerable for the design, the equipment and the build.

Two features define it in practice. The price is normally fixed, so the contractor absorbs quantity growth and productivity loss. Completion is normally defined by tests rather than by an activity finishing, so "done" means demonstrated performance, not a bar reaching 100%.

The turnkey element matters more than most planners expect. A programme that shows mechanical completion as the end date is describing a different obligation from the one the contract created.

## How does EPC differ from EPCM, design-build and construction management?

They differ in who holds the risk, who holds the money, and therefore whose programme governs.

| Model | Who carries construction cost risk | Price basis | Who holds the trade contracts | What it means for the planner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EPC, lump-sum turnkey | Contractor | Fixed sum for a defined performance outcome | Contractor | The contract programme is the contractor's; the owner plans assurance, interfaces and readiness |
| EPCM | Owner | Reimbursable fee for management services | Owner, directly with each trade | The planner integrates many contractor programmes into one owner-held schedule |
| Design-build | Contractor | Fixed or target sum for design and construction | Contractor | Similar to EPC without the performance-test handover; procurement often stays with the owner |
| Construction management at risk | Contractor, above a guaranteed maximum price | Cost plus fee with a GMP | Construction manager | Open-book reporting; the planner supports cost transparency as well as sequence |
| Alliance or target cost | Shared, through pain and gain | Target with an agreed sharing mechanism | Usually the alliance | Progress measurement feeds the sharing calculation, so its rules become contentious |

The frequent error is running an EPCM job with EPC habits. Under EPCM the owner holds the interfaces, and if nobody plans them, nobody does.

## What does lump-sum turnkey actually oblige each side to do?

The contractor owes a completed facility by a date, tested against agreed criteria, and usually pays liquidated damages for lateness up to a cap. The owner owes access, decisions, free-issue items and approvals inside the periods the contract states.

Liquidated damages are the mechanism that makes the date financially real, and their cap is the number that decides behaviour late in the job. Once a contractor is at the cap, further delay costs it nothing under that clause, and the incentive structure quietly inverts.

Extension of time is the counterpart. It protects the contractor against owner-caused delay and keeps a defensible date in place for the owner, which is why notice provisions are strict and late notice is the industry's most common own goal.

## What does the planner owe the contract?

More than a Gantt chart. Most EPC forms make the programme a contractual deliverable with consequences attached, and the obligations are usually procedural.

Submit the baseline within the stated period and in the stated form, because an unsubmitted or rejected programme weakens every later claim. Update at the stated interval with an honest data date and no retrospective logic changes hidden inside the revision.

Give notice inside the stated window when a delay event occurs, and record the facts contemporaneously: instructions, access, weather, resources, approvals. The record beats a retrospective narrative written six months later, every time.

Keep the as-planned, as-built and as-updated versions retrievable. Delay analysis depends on showing what the programme said before anyone knew how the story ended, and [the method chosen to analyse the delay](https://projectcontrolsinstitute.org/delay-analysis-techniques) decides which of those versions has to survive.

## How is progress measured and paid on an EPC job?

Through rules of credit — a published rule that says what proportion of an activity is earned for each verifiable step — rolled up through discipline weightings. It has to be written down before work starts, because arguing about it afterwards is arguing about money.

Take weightings of engineering **12%**, procurement **38%**, construction **42%** and commissioning **8%**, with discipline progress of 78%, 55%, 22% and 0%.

Overall progress is (0.78 × 12) + (0.55 × 38) + (0.22 × 42) + (0 × 8) = 9.36 + 20.90 + 9.24 + 0 = **39.5%**.

Change any weighting and the same physical work reports differently, which is why weightings belong in the contract or an agreed procedure. A contractor weighting engineering at 25% is front-loading its cash, and that should be visible in the arithmetic rather than suspected.

Payment follows either milestones or measured progress. Milestones are cleaner to administer and harsher on cash flow; measured progress is smoother and more disputable.

## How does an EPC contract become revenue? The IFRS 15 five-step model in plain terms

Long-term contract revenue is recognised under the five-step model set out in IFRS 15.

**Step one — identify the contract.** There has to be an enforceable agreement with a customer, with commercial substance and collectability that is probable.

**Step two — identify the performance obligations.** These are the distinct promises inside the contract. A turnkey plant is often a single obligation, because the goods and services are heavily integrated into one combined output.

**Step three — determine the transaction price.** This includes variable amounts such as claims, incentives and liquidated damages. You count a claim or an incentive only as far as you are confident it will not have to be reversed later, which is what keeps an optimistic claim value out of this period's revenue.

**Step four — allocate the price to the obligations.** Where there is only one obligation, the whole price sits with it. Where there are several, allocation follows standalone selling prices.

**Step five — recognise revenue as each obligation is satisfied.** For most EPC work this happens over time, measured by progress, using either an input measure such as cost incurred against total forecast cost, or an output measure such as units or milestones achieved.

Worked through: costs to date **£62m** against a total forecast cost of **£155m** gives 62 ÷ 155 = **40.0%** complete on an input measure. On a transaction price of **£180m**, cumulative revenue is 180 × 0.40 = **£72m**.

If only **£65m** has been certified and billed, the difference of **£7m** is a contract asset — revenue earned and not yet an unconditional receivable. Reverse the position and it is a contract liability.

The line that matters to a planner is total forecast cost. It comes from the project's own forecast, so a change in the delivery forecast changes reported revenue in the same month, and a forecast held back for a fortnight becomes an accounting problem as well as a control one.

## What does an EPC contract do to cash?

It stretches it. Profit and cash diverge on long contracts, and the divergence is measurable with the cash conversion cycle: days of work in progress and unbilled value, plus days of receivables, minus days of payables.

Take unbilled work at **34 days**, receivables at **58 days** and payables at **47 days**. The cycle is 34 + 58 − 47 = **45 days**.

On annual revenue of **£480m**, a day is 480 ÷ 365 = **£1.32m**, so 45 days ties up about **£59m** of working capital before any retention.

Retention makes it worse. At **5%** of a **£180m** contract, **£9m** sits with the customer until completion and often half of it until the defects period ends, which can be a year after the last person leaves site.

Rules of credit and the contract asset are the same event seen from two desks. The planner who agreed the weightings rarely knows they moved reported revenue; the accountant who booked the revenue rarely knows the weightings existed, and neither profession's examinations cover the other's side.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is EPC the same as turnkey?**
Nearly, but not always. EPC describes the scope bundled into one contract. Turnkey describes the handover standard: a facility ready for the owner to operate, usually proven by performance tests. Most EPC contracts are turnkey, and some are not, so read the completion definition rather than the label on the front cover.

**Who owns float in an EPC programme?**
Whatever the contract says, and if it says nothing, expect an argument. Common positions are that float belongs to the project and is available to whoever needs it first, or that terminal float belongs to the contractor. The practical answer is to settle it in writing at baseline acceptance, because float ownership decides who pays for a delay that consumes it.

**Does the owner still need project controls under a lump-sum contract?**
Yes, for three different jobs: verifying reported progress, managing owner-caused delay risk such as approvals and free-issue items, and controlling the owner's own costs, which the contract sum never included. Owners who reduce controls to invoice checking find out at handover that nobody was tracking their half of the programme.

**How do liquidated damages appear in the accounts?**
Usually as a reduction in the transaction price, because they are variable consideration in the customer's favour. The constraint applies, so the contractor includes them at the point a reversal is not expected rather than waiting for a formal deduction. The result is that a schedule slip can hit reported revenue before a single penny is withheld.

**Can an EPC contract be reimbursable?**
It can be, and convertible arrangements are common: reimbursable during early engineering, converting to a lump sum once definition supports a price. The risk is converting too early, on a design not mature enough to price, which produces a fixed sum everyone knows is wrong and a claims campaign from day one.
