# Domain 2 — Financial Reporting & the Standards

## KA 2.3 — Revenue recognition beyond IFRS 15 basics

KA 2.2 built the IFRS 15 five-step model in full — identifying the contract, identifying the performance
obligations, determining the transaction price, allocating it, and recognising revenue as or when control
transfers. This Knowledge Area extends that model into three situations a project controls professional
meets constantly in construction, EPC and engineering contracts, where the basic five steps alone do not
resolve the accounting: contracts where another party actually delivers part of what the customer is
buying, so the entity must decide whether it is the **principal** or merely an **agent** (section 2.3.1); contracts
that bundle several genuinely distinct goods and services at one negotiated price, requiring the transaction
price to be allocated using **standalone selling prices** that are frequently not directly observable
(section 2.3.2); and the practical reality that a contract's **Bill of Quantities (BoQ)**-based interim billing and
its IFRS 15 revenue recognition are measured on different bases and routinely diverge, producing the
contract asset or contract liability the professional must track and explain (section 2.3.3). Each topic below
assumes the five-step model and the contract asset/contract liability concept introduced in KA 2.2 as its
starting point, and is cross-referenced throughout to Domain 7 (Contracts, Commercial Management, BoQ,
Invoicing & Revenue), where the commercial mechanics behind the numbers used here — contract types, BoQ
pricing, interim valuations — are developed in full.

### 2.3.1 Principal versus agent: control-based assessment and gross-versus-net revenue

**Definition and purpose.** Under IFRS 15, when a contract involves a third party providing goods or
services to the end customer, the entity must determine whether it is acting as **principal** — it controls
the specified good or service before it is transferred to the customer, and recognises revenue at the
**gross** amount it is entitled to receive — or as **agent** — it arranges for another party to provide the
good or service, and recognises revenue only at the **net** amount it retains for its own service, a fee or
commission. This assessment is unavoidable in construction, EPC and engineering delivery, where nominated
subcontractors, specialist suppliers, provisional sums and pass-through procurement arrangements are routine
(cross-reference KA 7.1, KA 7.3). Getting the gross-versus-net conclusion wrong materially misstates both
reported revenue and cost of sales, even where it has no effect at all on reported profit.

**The underlying principle — control, not just risk.** IFRS 15 frames the assessment around **control**:
does the entity obtain control of the specified good or service before it is transferred to the customer?
This is a deliberate shift from earlier, superseded guidance that focused primarily on risks and rewards.
Indicators that support a **principal** conclusion, described here in the entity's own words rather than as
a checklist to be applied mechanically, include:

- the entity is primarily responsible for fulfilling the promise to provide the good or service, including
  responsibility for its acceptability to the customer;
- the entity bears inventory risk before the good is transferred to the customer (or after transfer, in the
  case of a right of return);
- the entity has discretion in setting the price the customer pays;
- the entity bears credit risk for the amount receivable from the customer.

No single indicator is determinative on its own; they are weighed together and require professional
judgement, particularly in construction supply chains with nominated and named subcontractors where
contractual responsibility is often deliberately split between the main contractor and the client.

**Formula / presentation.** Principal: `Revenue recognised = gross transaction price billed to the
customer`, with `Cost of sales = amount paid or payable to the third-party supplier` (the margin is the
difference). Agent: `Revenue recognised = fee or commission retained` (net); the gross amount collected on
behalf of the third party is not revenue and does not pass through cost of sales — it is a pass-through or
clearing balance.

**Example 2.3.1 — gross versus net revenue for a nominated-supplier arrangement.**

*Scenario.* Falcon Engineering LLC, an EPC contractor, arranges procurement of a specialist gas turbine for
a client's power project under a framework agreement. The manufacturer's turbine price is $2,000,000. Falcon
invoices the client $2,150,000 (a $150,000, 7.5%-of-cost handling fee). Under the framework agreement: the
manufacturer, not Falcon, warrants the turbine's performance directly to the client; the turbine ships
directly from the manufacturer to site, so Falcon never takes physical or title risk; the client fixes the
specification and negotiates the price cap directly with the manufacturer, so Falcon's fee is contractually
capped at a fixed percentage with no pricing discretion; and payment flows from the client to the
manufacturer through an escrow the client controls, so Falcon bears no credit risk on the $2,000,000.

*Required.* Assess principal versus agent, and state the revenue Falcon should recognise.

*Working.*

| Indicator | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Primarily responsible for fulfilment | No — manufacturer warrants directly to client |
| Inventory risk | No — direct ship, Falcon never takes title |
| Discretion in setting price | No — fee fixed by framework agreement |
| Credit risk | No — client pays manufacturer via escrow |

All four indicators point away from control: Falcon does not control the turbine before it transfers to the
client. Falcon is the **agent**.

*Answer.* **Revenue recognised = $150,000** (SAR 562,500 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1), the
handling fee only. The $2,000,000 collected and passed to the manufacturer is not Falcon's revenue and is
not recorded as cost of sales.

*Interpretation.* Had Falcon instead invoiced the client the full $2,150,000 and recorded $2,000,000 of cost
of sales, revenue would be overstated by $2,000,000 relative to the correct net presentation — a material
misstatement even though profit for the period is unaffected either way.

**Mini-case — same relationship, different facts: when Falcon becomes principal.** Suppose the framework
agreement is instead written so that Falcon itself warrants the turbine's performance to the client (with a
back-to-back indemnity from the manufacturer it must separately pursue), Falcon negotiates its own purchase
price with the manufacturer and independently sets the price it charges the client, and Falcon pays the
manufacturer regardless of whether or when the client pays Falcon. Every indicator now points to control:
Falcon is **principal**.

`Revenue = $2,150,000`; `Cost of sales = $2,000,000`; `Gross margin = $150,000 (7.0% of revenue)`.

The cash margin is identical to the agent scenario — still $150,000 — but revenue is now $2,150,000 instead
of $150,000, and a reported gross margin of 7.0% instead of a fee that is effectively 100% margin before any
of Falcon's own administrative cost. If Falcon also incurs $20,000 of its own administrative cost in
arranging the order, the net cash outcome in both scenarios is identical at $130,000, but it presents as a
6.0% margin on $2,150,000 of gross revenue (principal) or an 86.7% margin on $150,000 of net revenue
(agent) — the same underlying economics, radically different headline numbers for anyone reading revenue or
margin ratios off the financial statements.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Assuming a mark-up or handling fee automatically signals an agent relationship — a
principal can equally charge a straightforward mark-up; the assessment turns on control, not the pricing
mechanism. (2) Treating whoever issues the invoice to the customer as automatically the principal — the
invoicing flow is not, by itself, conclusive. (3) Applying a single principal/agent policy across an entire
contract rather than assessing each specified good or service separately — an entity can be principal for
some components of a bundled contract and agent for others (cross-reference section 2.3.2, section 2.3.3). (4) Failing to
reassess the conclusion when a variation changes who bears risk — for example, a change order that makes the
main contractor directly responsible for a specialist item's defects converts an agent relationship into a
principal one from that point forward.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-analytics and natural-language-processing tools can scan subcontract,
procurement and framework-agreement clauses across a large supply chain and flag indicator-relevant language
— who bears warranty risk, who has price-setting discretion, how title and payment flow — producing a
first-pass indicator matrix far faster than manual clause-by-clause review (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA
13.5). This is a genuine efficiency gain, but the principal-versus-agent conclusion itself is a holistic,
judgement-based determination the standard deliberately leaves to management, and an AI tool has no
independent way to confirm that a contract is administered in practice the way its clauses read on paper.
Indicator extraction is a legitimate AI-assisted first draft; the conclusion and its accounting consequence
require a qualified accountant's sign-off — AI proposes the indicator matrix; the professional decides
the judgement.

**Cross-references.** KA 2.2 (identifying the specified good or service before this assessment is even
possible); KA 7.1 (contract types, including nominated-subcontract structures); KA 7.3 (BoQ provisional
sums, frequently principal/agent-relevant); section 2.3.3 below (linking this assessment to BoQ billing and the
contract asset/liability).

### 2.3.2 Bundled goods and services: identifying distinct performance obligations and estimating standalone selling prices

**Definition and purpose.** Beyond the basic five-step allocation taught in KA 2.2, many project contracts
bundle several genuinely distinct goods and services — design, construction, commissioning, training,
extended maintenance, embedded software licences — into a single, negotiated contract price. Where a good
or service is **distinct** (capable of being distinct, and distinct within the context of the contract — the
two-part test introduced in KA 2.2), IFRS 15 requires the transaction price to be allocated to each
performance obligation on a **relative standalone selling price (SSP)** basis. The complication this topic
addresses is practical: most contract line items are not sold on a standalone basis at a single, observable
price, so the SSP itself frequently has to be **estimated**.

**The underlying principle.** Relative-SSP allocation exists so that revenue reported for each performance
obligation reflects what the entity would have charged for that good or service if sold on its own — not an
arbitrary contractual split, which could otherwise be used to shift revenue between components (for example,
inflating the price attached to whichever obligation is satisfied earliest, to accelerate revenue
recognition). Where SSP is not directly observable, IFRS 15 permits estimation using methods including:

- **Adjusted market assessment approach** — evaluate the market in which the good or service is sold and
  estimate the price customers in that market would be willing to pay, referencing comparable competitor
  pricing adjusted for the entity's own costs and margins.
- **Expected cost plus a margin approach** — forecast the expected cost of satisfying the performance
  obligation and add an appropriate margin for that class of work.
- **Residual approach** — deduct the sum of the observable standalone selling prices of the *other* distinct
  goods or services in the contract from the total transaction price. This method is restricted to
  circumstances where the price for a good or service is **highly variable** (no single, representative price
  emerges from past standalone transactions) or **highly uncertain** (not yet established, never having been
  sold separately). It is not a default and must not be used merely because it is the simplest calculation.

Where a contract's overall price is discounted relative to the sum of the individual SSPs, IFRS 15 requires
that discount to be allocated **proportionally across all performance obligations**, unless observable
evidence shows the discount relates entirely to one or more specific obligations.

**Formula.** For a contract with *n* distinct performance obligations:
`Allocated transaction price (obligation i) = Transaction price × [SSP(i) / Σ SSP(1..n)]`
where SSP(i) is the standalone selling price (observed or estimated) of obligation *i*, and Σ SSP(1..n) is
the sum of the standalone selling prices of all obligations in the contract.

**Example 2.3.2 — allocating a bundled design–construct–maintain contract.**

*Scenario.* A contractor agrees a single $5,130,000 price covering: (a) design services, with an observable
SSP of $600,000 (the entity regularly sells comparable standalone design packages at this price); (b)
construction works, with an observable SSP of $4,500,000 (the market rate for comparable standalone
construction contracts); and (c) a 2-year maintenance service, not sold separately, estimated using the
expected cost plus a margin approach — expected cost of $250,000 plus a 20% margin.

*Required.* Allocate the $5,130,000 transaction price across the three performance obligations.

*Working.*
`Maintenance SSP = 250,000 × (1 + 20%) = 250,000 + 50,000 = 300,000`
`Sum of SSPs = 600,000 + 4,500,000 + 300,000 = 5,400,000`
`Discount = 5,400,000 − 5,130,000 = 270,000` → `Discount % = 270,000 / 5,400,000 = 5.0%`

Allocating the 5.0% discount proportionally (no evidence it relates to a single obligation, so it is spread
across all three):

`Design: 600,000 × 0.95 = 570,000`
`Construction: 4,500,000 × 0.95 = 4,275,000`
`Maintenance: 300,000 × 0.95 = 285,000`

*Answer.* **Design $570,000; Construction $4,275,000; Maintenance $285,000.** Check:
`570,000 + 4,275,000 + 285,000 = 5,130,000` ✓, equal to the contract price.

*Interpretation.* Each obligation's revenue will be recognised according to its own pattern of satisfaction
— design and construction most likely over time via an input method (KA 2.2), maintenance over the two-year
service period on a straight-line or usage basis — but each now carries its fair share of the overall
contract discount rather than an arbitrary allocation that could distort the timing of reported revenue.

**Mini-case — the residual approach for a highly variable-priced licence.** A contract to supply and
commission a proprietary plant control-system (SCADA) is priced at $900,000 in total, covering: a software
licence, installation and commissioning services, and 12 months of software support. Installation and
commissioning has an observable SSP of $150,000 (sold as a standalone service to other clients). Twelve
months of support has an observable SSP of $50,000 (sold separately as annual renewals). The software
licence itself has no discernible single SSP — historically it has been sold on a standalone basis for
between $200,000 and $800,000, depending on negotiated site count and volume, a genuinely highly variable
price with no representative point estimate. The residual approach is therefore appropriate for the licence:

`Residual SSP (licence) = 900,000 − (150,000 + 50,000) = 700,000`

$700,000 falls within the historically observed $200,000–$800,000 range, so the residual result is accepted
as reasonable. Had the residual computation produced a figure outside that range, or zero, or negative, that
would signal the residual approach is not producing a faithful estimate in this instance, and the estimation
approach would need to be reconsidered rather than mechanically applied.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Allocating a bundle discount entirely to the obligation delivered last (or first),
to defer (or accelerate) revenue, without objective evidence the discount relates specifically to that item
— this is not permitted under the general allocation rule. (2) Reaching for the residual approach as a
convenient default when observable or reasonably estimable SSP evidence in fact exists for that item. (3)
Allocating between components that are not, on closer analysis, actually distinct — where design is so
integrated with, and significantly customises, the construction scope that the two form a single combined
output, no separate SSP allocation between them is required; only the overall revenue-recognition method for
the single obligation (KA 2.2) applies. (4) Using a stale or non-comparable market reference for the adjusted
market assessment approach.

**AI in this topic.** Data-analytics tools can mine historical standalone sales data across a large contract
portfolio to propose an adjusted-market-assessment or expected-cost-plus-margin SSP estimate far faster than
a manual pricing survey, and can flag, quantitatively, when a component's historical pricing is genuinely
"highly variable" — a relevant signal for whether the residual approach is appropriate (cross-reference KA
13.4, KA 13.5). Such tools cannot determine whether goods or services are genuinely distinct in the context
of a specific contract, which is a contract-specific integration judgement, nor can they validate that a
residual result is reasonable in substance rather than merely mathematically convenient. SSP estimation
methodology and the distinct-obligation conclusion remain matters for accountant review and sign-off.

### 2.3.3 Linking recognised revenue to billing and the Bill of Quantities

**Definition and purpose.** Construction, EPC and infrastructure contracts are typically billed through
periodic **interim valuations** measured against a priced **Bill of Quantities (BoQ)** — an output-based,
quantity-surveyor-certified measure of physical progress (full mechanics in KA 7.3–KA 7.4). IFRS 15 revenue
for the same contract, however, is measured by whichever method — input or output — faithfully depicts the
transfer of control (KA 2.2), commonly an input (cost-to-cost) method for complex, engineered scope. Because
BoQ billing progress and IFRS 15 revenue recognition can be assessed on different bases, the two figures
routinely diverge, producing the **contract asset** or **contract liability** the professional must track.

**The underlying principle.** The BoQ is a commercial billing mechanism, not a revenue-recognition
mechanism — it exists to give the client a measurable, auditable basis for interim payment (KA 7.3), and its
rates may deliberately front-load items such as mobilisation and preliminaries, or back-load items subject
to final measurement and retention, for cash-flow and risk-allocation reasons unrelated to when control
transfers under IFRS 15. Using the canonical formula:

`Contract asset/(liability) = cumulative revenue recognised − cumulative amount billed`

a positive result is a **contract asset** (revenue earned ahead of billing — an unbilled, receivable-type
asset); a negative result is a **contract liability** (billed ahead of revenue earned — a deferred-revenue-
type liability). This sign convention is the one to be locked and reused identically in KA 7.5. Where a
contract also contains an agent-type pass-through element (section 2.3.1) — for example, a BoQ provisional sum for
specialist works the contractor merely arranges — only the entity's own revenue (its principal-scope billing
plus any arranging fee) enters this comparison; the gross pass-through amount billed and collected on the
client's behalf is excluded from both sides.

**Formula.** As above, together with `% complete (input method) = costs incurred to date / total estimated
costs` (KA 2.2, KA 3.2), applied to the contractor's own (principal) scope only.

**Example 2.3.3 — Silver Bridge Construction JV: combining principal/agent and BoQ billing in one
contract-asset calculation.**

*Scenario.* Silver Bridge Construction JV holds an $8,000,000 BoQ contract. Of this, $7,500,000 is the
contractor's own scope (design, structure, finishes); the remaining $500,000 is a provisional sum for a
specialist elevator installation, which Silver Bridge merely arranges on the client's behalf for an 8%
arranging fee ($40,000) — assessed as **agent** under section 2.3.1's indicators (the specialist elevator
manufacturer warrants and is directly responsible for the installation). At the first data date: total
estimated cost of the own-scope work is $6,000,000; costs incurred to date are $3,600,000; the elevator
installation is separately measured at 80% physically complete; the BoQ interim valuation certified to date
is $4,800,000 for own-scope items and $400,000 for the elevator provisional sum (billed in line with its 80%
progress).

*Required.* Revenue recognised to date, the amount billed relevant to that revenue, and the resulting
contract asset or liability.

*Working.*
`% complete (own scope) = 3,600,000 / 6,000,000 = 60%`
`Own-scope revenue = 60% × 7,500,000 = 4,500,000`
`Agent fee revenue = 80% × 40,000 = 32,000` (recognised in line with the specialist's own progress, since
that is what the arranging service is satisfied against)
`Total revenue recognised = 4,500,000 + 32,000 = 4,532,000`

`Fee billed to date = 8% × elevator amount billed (400,000) = 32,000`
`Amount billed relevant to revenue = own-scope billed (4,800,000) + fee billed (32,000) = 4,832,000`
(the $400,000 gross elevator billing itself is excluded — it is a pass-through, not Silver Bridge's revenue)

`Contract asset/(liability) = 4,532,000 − 4,832,000 = (300,000)`

*Answer.* **Revenue recognised to date $4,532,000; amount billed (revenue-relevant) $4,832,000; contract
liability of $300,000** (SAR 1,125,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1).

*Interpretation.* Despite collecting cash ahead of the manufacturer's warranty position, Silver Bridge has
recognised **less** revenue than it has billed, because the BoQ's output-based (measured-quantity) own-scope
billing of $4,800,000 has run ahead of the cost-based, input-method own-scope revenue of $4,500,000. A
$300,000 contract liability must be carried on the statement of financial position — and note that the
$400,000 gross elevator billing never entered the calculation at all, because Silver Bridge is agent, not
principal, for that item.

**Mini-case — the same contract, a later data date: the liability flips into an asset.** Six months later,
own-scope costs incurred rise to $5,400,000 (90% complete: `5,400,000 / 6,000,000 = 90%`), so own-scope
revenue is `90% × 7,500,000 = 6,750,000`. The elevator is now 100% complete, so the full $40,000 fee is
recognised. Total revenue recognised is `6,750,000 + 40,000 = 6,790,000`. Cumulative BoQ billed for own
scope, however, is only $6,500,000 — the final tranche of BoQ items is subject to retention-linked final
measurement not yet certified, so billing has lagged behind cost-based progress this time. The elevator
provisional sum is now billed in full ($500,000), with the fee billed in full at `8% × 500,000 = 40,000`.
Cumulative amount billed relevant to revenue is `6,500,000 + 40,000 = 6,540,000`.

`Contract asset/(liability) = 6,790,000 − 6,540,000 = +250,000` → a **contract asset of $250,000**.

*Interpretation.* The same contract has moved from a $300,000 contract liability to a $250,000 contract
asset purely because the relationship between output-based BoQ billing progress and input-method revenue
recognition reversed as the final, back-loaded BoQ items awaited certification — a common and entirely
expected pattern as a construction contract approaches completion, and a routine reconciliation item for the
professional to explain to management, not a sign that anything has gone wrong.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Comparing revenue recognised against total BoQ billed *including* agent pass-through
gross amounts, instead of excluding pass-through billing that is never the entity's revenue — this produces
a materially wrong contract asset/liability figure, as the Silver Bridge example shows. (2) Treating a BoQ
measured-quantity progress percentage as automatically equal to the IFRS 15 percentage complete — the two
need not match, because they can be based on different methods (output versus input), different data dates,
or commercially front- or back-loaded BoQ rates. (3) Forgetting to reassess the underlying principal/agent
conclusion when a provisional sum is later converted into the contractor's own scope by a variation order (a
common real-world occurrence) — fee-only recognition must stop, and full principal (gross) recognition must
begin, from the point control actually transfers. (4) Presenting a contract liability as though it were an
unrelated cash advance, without recognising it specifically as the IFRS 15 contract-liability construct tied
to a specific, identifiable performance obligation.

**AI in this topic.** Robotic process automation and data-extraction tools can pull certified BoQ interim-
valuation amounts from a billing/ERP system and cost-to-cost percentages from the cost ledger, and compute
the contract asset/liability roll-forward across hundreds of contracts far faster than a manual
reconciliation, flagging contracts where the gap between revenue and billing widens abnormally period on
period (cross-reference KA 4.4, KA 13.5). Such tools cannot determine, on their own, whether a specific
provisional-sum item should still be treated as agent (fee-only) or has effectively become principal scope
through a variation, nor can they judge whether a BoQ rate is commercially front-loaded rather than
reflective of genuine output-method progress — both require the professional's contract and commercial
knowledge. AI proposes the reconciliation; the professional decides the principal/agent and measurement-
method judgements behind it.

### Key terms

- **Principal** — an entity that controls a specified good or service before it is transferred to the
  customer; recognises revenue gross.
- **Agent** — an entity that arranges for another party to provide a good or service to the customer;
  recognises revenue net, as a fee or commission.
- **Control (in the principal/agent context)** — the ability to direct the use of, and obtain substantially
  all the remaining benefit from, a specified good or service before it transfers to the customer.
- **Gross revenue presentation** — reporting the full transaction price as revenue, with the third-party
  cost separately reported as cost of sales (principal).
- **Net revenue presentation** — reporting only the retained fee or commission as revenue (agent).
- **Distinct performance obligation** — a good or service that is capable of being distinct and is distinct
  within the context of the contract (KA 2.2).
- **Standalone selling price (SSP)** — the price at which an entity would sell a promised good or service
  separately to a customer.
- **Adjusted market assessment approach** — estimating SSP from comparable market prices, adjusted for the
  entity's own costs and margins.
- **Expected cost plus a margin approach** — estimating SSP from the expected cost of satisfying the
  obligation plus an appropriate margin.
- **Residual approach** — estimating SSP by deducting the sum of other obligations' observable SSPs from the
  total transaction price; permitted only where the price is highly variable or highly uncertain.
- **Bill of Quantities (BoQ)** — a priced, measured schedule of the quantities of work in a contract, used as
  the basis for interim billing (KA 7.3).
- **Provisional sum** — an estimated allowance in a BoQ for work not yet fully defined or priced, commonly
  used for specialist or nominated-subcontractor items.
- **Interim valuation / application for payment** — a periodic assessment of work done, certified against
  the BoQ, forming the basis of progress billing (KA 7.4).
- **Contract asset** — revenue recognised in excess of amounts billed to date.
- **Contract liability** — amounts billed in excess of revenue recognised to date.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 2.3.1 [topic 2.3.1, level: recall]**
Which of the following is an indicator that an entity is acting as **principal** rather than agent under
IFRS 15?
A. The entity's invoice to the customer is itemised by supplier
B. The entity bears inventory risk for the good before it transfers to the customer ✔
C. The entity's fee is calculated as a fixed percentage of the supplier's price
D. The entity is not mentioned by name in the underlying supply contract

*Rationale:* Bearing inventory risk before transfer is a recognised control indicator supporting a
principal conclusion (section 2.3.1). A and D are not, by themselves, indicative of control; C describes a pricing
mechanism that is equally consistent with either role.

**MCQ 2.3.2 [topic 2.3.1, level: application — numerical]**
A contractor arranges a specialist crane rental costing $400,000 on behalf of the client and invoices the
client $428,000 (a $28,000 handling fee). The contractor is assessed, on the facts, as agent. What amount
of revenue should the contractor recognise?
A. $428,000
B. $400,000
C. $28,000 ✔
D. $0

*Rationale:* As agent, only the net fee retained is revenue: `428,000 − 400,000 = 28,000` (section 2.3.1). A
wrongly recognises the gross amount, as if principal; B recognises the pass-through cost as revenue, which
is incorrect under either role; D ignores that a genuine service (arranging) has still been performed and
earned.

**MCQ 2.3.3 [topic 2.3.2, level: application — numerical]**
A contract bundles two distinct performance obligations with standalone selling prices of $300,000 and
$900,000, sold together for a combined contract price of $1,080,000. What amount is allocated to the
$300,000 obligation?
A. $300,000
B. $270,000 ✔
C. $250,000
D. $1,080,000

*Rationale:* Sum of SSPs = $1,200,000; discount = $120,000 (10.0%); allocated amount =
`300,000 × 0.90 = 270,000` (section 2.3.2). A ignores the proportional discount; C and D do not follow from the
relative-SSP method.

**MCQ 2.3.4 [topic 2.3.2, level: analysis]**
Under IFRS 15's allocation guidance, the residual approach to estimating a standalone selling price is
appropriate when:
A. It is the simplest method available for the entity to apply
B. The good or service has a highly variable or highly uncertain price with no representative standalone
   figure ✔
C. The customer has requested a specific allocation split
D. All other obligations in the contract also use the residual approach

*Rationale:* The residual approach is restricted to highly variable or highly uncertain pricing situations,
not chosen for convenience (section 2.3.2). A, C and D describe reasons that do not, on their own, justify its use.

**MCQ 2.3.5 [topic 2.3.3, level: application — numerical]**
At a contract's data date, cumulative revenue recognised is $2,300,000 and cumulative amount billed is
$2,100,000. What does this represent?
A. A contract liability of $200,000
B. A contract asset of $200,000 ✔
C. A contract asset of $2,300,000
D. No contract asset or liability arises

*Rationale:* `Contract asset/(liability) = revenue recognised − amount billed = 2,300,000 − 2,100,000 =
+200,000`, a positive result, which is a contract asset (section 2.3.3). A reverses the sign; C confuses the total
with the difference; D ignores that the two figures differ.

**MCQ 2.3.6 [topic 2.3.3, level: analysis]**
Why can a contract's BoQ-based interim billing progress differ from its IFRS 15 percentage of completion for
the same reporting period?
A. They cannot differ; both are always based on the same measurement method
B. The BoQ uses an output-based, quantity-surveyor-certified measure, while revenue recognition may use a
   cost-based input method, and BoQ rates can be commercially front- or back-loaded ✔
C. IFRS 15 prohibits the use of a Bill of Quantities in construction contracts
D. Billing is always required to equal revenue recognised under IFRS 15

*Rationale:* The BoQ is a commercial billing mechanism (an output measure, often with front- or back-loaded
rates), while IFRS 15 revenue follows whichever method faithfully depicts the transfer of control, commonly
an input method for complex scope (section 2.3.3) — the two are not the same measurement and routinely diverge. A,
C and D are all incorrect statements of the standard or of practice.

### Self-check questions

1. Explain, in your own words, why a principal's and an agent's cash margin can be identical even though
   reported revenue differs enormously. Use the Falcon Engineering figures in your answer.
2. A contract bundles two distinct performance obligations with observable standalone selling prices of
   $250,000 and $750,000, sold together for a combined contract price of $900,000. Allocate the transaction
   price to each obligation.
3. Under what two circumstances is the residual approach to estimating a standalone selling price permitted,
   and why does a "highly variable" historical price specifically undermine the adjusted market assessment
   approach as an alternative?
4. Using the Silver Bridge Construction JV data at the first data date (section 2.3.3), explain in one sentence why
   the elevator's $400,000 gross billing does not appear anywhere in the contract asset/liability
   calculation.

**Answers**

1. Falcon's cash margin is $150,000 either way — as principal, $2,150,000 revenue less $2,000,000 cost of
   sales equals $150,000; as agent, the $150,000 fee is recognised directly as revenue with no offsetting
   cost of sales for the pass-through amount. The underlying economics and cash flow are identical; only the
   principal-versus-agent control assessment determines whether that $150,000 is presented as a 7.0% margin
   on $2,150,000 of gross revenue or as the full $150,000 of net revenue.
2. Sum of SSPs = $1,000,000; discount = $100,000 (10.0%); allocated amounts =
   `250,000 × 0.90 = 225,000` and `750,000 × 0.90 = 675,000`. Check: `225,000 + 675,000 = 900,000` ✓.
3. The residual approach is permitted when the price for a good or service is highly variable (no single
   representative figure emerges from past standalone sales) or highly uncertain (not yet established,
   never sold separately). A highly variable historical price specifically undermines the adjusted market
   assessment approach because that approach depends on being able to point to a representative market
   price for the item, which by definition does not exist when observed prices vary too widely to be
   representative of any single figure.
4. Silver Bridge is acting as agent for the elevator installation — it does not control that good or service
   before it transfers to the client — so only the $32,000 arranging fee, not the $400,000 gross
   pass-through billing, is the entity's own revenue-relevant transaction, and the gross amount is excluded
   from both the revenue-recognised and amount-billed sides of the comparison.
