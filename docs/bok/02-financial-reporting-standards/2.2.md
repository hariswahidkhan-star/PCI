# Domain 2 — Financial Reporting & the Standards

## KA 2.2 — IFRS 15 Revenue from Contracts with Customers — in depth

Revenue is the single most scrutinised number in any set of project accounts: it drives the top of the
Statement of Profit or Loss, it is the number analysts, lenders and boards anchor on, and — for a project
controls professional specifically — it is the number that should, if measured correctly, move in step with
physical progress on the ground. IFRS 15 *Revenue from Contracts with Customers* is the single standard that
governs when and how much revenue an entity recognises across virtually every contract a project-based
business signs, from a one-off equipment sale to a multi-year, multi-billion-dollar construction contract.
Before IFRS 15 took effect (for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2018), revenue was governed by
several separate standards — most relevantly for this audience, IAS 11 for construction contracts and IAS 18
for the sale of goods and services — which IFRS 15 replaced with a single, unified **five-step model**
applicable to *all* contracts with customers. For a project controls professional, IFRS 15 is where the
percentage-of-completion method the profession has long used operationally is grounded in formal accounting
principle, where the everyday practice of progress billing meets the concept of a contract asset or contract
liability, and where a change order on site becomes a "contract modification" with specific accounting
consequences. This Knowledge Area is flagged **flagship** and is treated in full depth for exactly that
reason: get IFRS 15 wrong, and every EVM curve, every invoice application and every management report built
on top of it inherits the error. The chapter works through the five steps in sequence (sections 2.2.2–2.2.6), then
the two topics that make the model operable for long-duration contracts — measuring progress (section 2.2.7) and
constraining variable consideration (section 2.2.8) — before covering financing components, modifications, contract
assets/liabilities, the full construction-contract application, and disclosures (sections 2.2.9–2.2.13). Three running
examples carry the arithmetic through the chapter: **TechBuild Automation LLC** (a multi-performance-
obligation equipment, maintenance and training contract, used for Steps 1–5), **Silverline Infrastructure
LLC**'s **Route 9 Flyover Bridge** contract (a single, over-time construction contract, used for progress
measurement, contract assets/liabilities and the full multi-year application), and **Coastal Energy
Constructors LLC**'s **Coastal Pipeline Extension** (used for variable consideration). Throughout, IFRS 15's
principles are described in this chapter's own words — never reproduced from, or cited to, a specific clause
or paragraph number of the standard — and every worked figure has been independently re-checked to reconcile
to its contract total.

### 2.2.1 The core principle and the five-step model — overview

**Definition and purpose.** IFRS 15's central idea can be stated simply: an entity should recognise revenue
only when — and only to the extent that — it has actually transferred the goods or services it promised to a
customer, and only for the amount it genuinely expects to be entitled to receive in exchange for that
transfer. Revenue is not recognised merely because cash has changed hands, an invoice has been issued, or a
contract has been signed; it is recognised because a real, measurable transfer of value to the customer has
occurred. IFRS 15 operationalises this idea through a **five-step model** applied to every contract with a
customer:

1. **Identify the contract(s) with a customer** (section 2.2.2).
2. **Identify the performance obligations in the contract** — the distinct promises to transfer goods or
   services (section 2.2.3).
3. **Determine the transaction price** — the amount of consideration the entity expects to be entitled to
   (section 2.2.4).
4. **Allocate the transaction price** to each performance obligation, generally in proportion to standalone
   selling prices (section 2.2.5).
5. **Recognise revenue** as (over time) or when (at a point in time) each performance obligation is satisfied
   (section 2.2.6).

**The underlying principle.** The five steps are sequential and cumulative — each depends on the one before
it. A contract that does not qualify under Step 1 generates no revenue under this model at all; a performance
obligation not correctly separated out in Step 2 will have the wrong amount allocated to it in Step 4 even if
Steps 3 and 5 are performed perfectly. The model's discipline is precisely what makes revenue recognition
auditable and comparable across entities and industries — a software licence, a consulting engagement and a
three-year highway contract are all analysed through the same five questions, even though the answers differ
enormously.

**Formula.** No single formula applies at this overview level; each step has its own formula, developed in
sections 2.2.2–2.2.6. The identity that threads the whole model together is:

`Revenue recognised (cumulative, per performance obligation) = Transaction price allocated to that obligation
× Proportion of that obligation satisfied to date`

**Example 2.2.1 — the five steps applied at a glance.**

*Scenario.* TechBuild Automation LLC contracts with Northbridge Water Utility to supply and install a SCADA
(supervisory control and data acquisition) system for a treatment plant, provide two years of maintenance,
and deliver operator training, for a total fixed price of $540,000 (SAR 2,025,000 at an illustrative
SAR 3.75 = USD 1). This running example is developed in full in sections 2.2.2–2.2.6; the table below previews the
answer at each step.

*Required.* State, at a headline level, the outcome of each of the five steps.

*Working / Answer.*

| Step | Question | Headline answer for TechBuild/Northbridge |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify the contract | Does a contract exist under the model's criteria? | Yes — signed, approved, priced, commercially substantive, collection probable (section 2.2.2) |
| 2. Identify performance obligations | What are the distinct promises? | Three: equipment & installation; 2-year maintenance; training (section 2.2.3) |
| 3. Determine the transaction price | What is the total consideration expected? | $540,000 fixed; no variable consideration, financing or non-cash elements in this contract (section 2.2.4) |
| 4. Allocate the transaction price | How much of the $540,000 belongs to each obligation? | Equipment $360,000; Maintenance $135,000; Training $45,000 (section 2.2.5) |
| 5. Recognise revenue | When/how is each obligation's allocated price recognised? | Equipment: point in time, on commissioning. Maintenance: over time, straight-line over 24 months. Training: point in time, on delivery (section 2.2.6) |

*Interpretation.* One contract, one customer, one signature — but three separate revenue-recognition
patterns within it. This is the normal, not the exceptional, outcome of applying the model to a bundled
commercial contract, and it is precisely why the five steps must be worked through explicitly rather than
assumed from the contract's single headline price.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Starting the analysis from the invoice or billing schedule rather than the contract

principle, to a single-item sale and a multi-year infrastructure contract; only the *complexity* of each step
differs.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted contract-review tools can scan an incoming contract and produce a draft

KA 13.4, KA 13.5). The governance limit is that every one of the five steps involves at least one genuine
accounting judgement (is the contract commercially substantive? are these promises distinct? what is the
standalone selling price?), and an AI tool's draft classification is a starting point for the qualified
accountant's review, not a substitute for it. AI proposes the map; the professional decides the
judgements behind each step.

### 2.2.2 Step 1 — Identifying the contract

**Definition and purpose.** A "contract" for IFRS 15 purposes is not simply a signed document — it is an
arrangement that meets five specific criteria, all of which must be met before any revenue can be recognised
under the model. In the entity's own words, those criteria are: (a) the parties have approved the contract
and are committed to performing their respective obligations; (b) each party's rights to the goods or
services to be transferred can be identified; (c) the payment terms can be identified; (d) the contract has
commercial substance (the entity's future cash flows are expected to change as a result of the contract); and
(e) it is probable that the entity will collect the consideration to which it will be entitled — which
requires an assessment of the customer's ability and intention to pay, not merely a wish that it will.

**The underlying principle.** The criteria exist to stop revenue being recognised on arrangements that are
not yet genuine, enforceable commercial commitments, or where there is real doubt the entity will ever be
paid. A verbal understanding not yet approved, a scope with no defined rights, or a contract with a customer
in financial distress and no credit assessment performed, all fail the test — and if the contract fails, no
revenue is recognised under the model even if work has started, until the criteria come to be met (or, in
limited circumstances, until cash received becomes non-refundable with no remaining obligation, at which
point it may be recognised on that narrower basis).

**Formula.** No numerical formula; Step 1 is a **pass/fail gate**: all five criteria must be met (**Approval
& commitment ∧ Identifiable rights ∧ Identifiable payment terms ∧ Commercial substance ∧ Collectability
probable**) before Steps 2–5 proceed.

**Example 2.2.2 — assessing whether a contract exists.**

*Scenario.* TechBuild Automation LLC and Northbridge Water Utility sign a $540,000 SCADA supply, maintenance
and training agreement on 2 January 2026. Northbridge is a long-established, investment-grade-rated public
utility with an eight-year payment history with TechBuild showing no late payments.

*Required.* Assess each of the five criteria.

*Working.*

| Criterion | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Approval & commitment | Both parties signed the contract; TechBuild has begun procurement and Northbridge has issued a purchase order — met |
| Identifiable rights | Scope schedule defines the SCADA system, maintenance service levels and training days precisely — met |
| Identifiable payment terms | $200,000 on signing, $200,000 on commissioning, $140,000 over the maintenance term — met |
| Commercial substance | The contract materially changes both parties' expected future cash flows — met |
| Collectability probable | Investment-grade counterparty, eight-year clean payment history, credit assessment performed and documented — met |

*Answer.* **All five criteria are met — a contract exists under the model with effect from 2 January 2026.**

*Interpretation.* Because collectability is assessed for the consideration the entity expects to be
*entitled to* (not necessarily every dollar of the stated price if, for example, a portion is genuinely
contingent), the assessment should be revisited if a customer's financial position deteriorates materially
during a long contract — a single point-in-time assessment at signing is not, by itself, sufficient for a
multi-year arrangement.

**Example 2.2.2b — contract combination.** On the same day, TechBuild also signs a second purchase order with
Northbridge for a related control panel upgrade, priced separately at $60,000. The two purchase orders were
negotiated together as a single commercial package (the control-panel price was set with reference to the
SCADA deal, and Northbridge would not have proceeded with either alone). Because the two contracts were
entered into at the same time with the same customer and were negotiated as a single package with an
interdependent price, they are **combined and accounted for as one contract** of $600,000 for the purposes of
Steps 2–5, rather than as two separate $540,000 and $60,000 contracts. Had the two purchase orders instead
been negotiated independently, on different dates, with no pricing interdependence, they would remain
separate contracts even though both are with the same customer.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating a purchase order or a letter of intent as automatically satisfying "approval
and commitment" — an LOI that is explicitly non-binding, or that is subject to further approvals not yet
obtained, may not meet the criterion. (2) Assessing collectability against the full stated contract price
when a portion is genuinely at risk (for example, a milestone payment contingent on a third-party approval
that may not be obtained) rather than against the amount the entity is genuinely entitled to. (3) Failing to
revisit contract combination when related purchase orders or amendments are issued close together with the
same customer — combination is a substance-over-form test, not a form-over-substance one.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted contract-intake and document-extraction tools can flag the presence (or
absence) of signatures, defined payment terms and scope schedules across a large contract population, and
can surface a customer's payment-history and credit-data trends to support the collectability assessment
(cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). These tools materially speed up the *evidence-gathering* stage. They do
not make the collectability judgement itself, and they cannot reliably detect whether two contracts were
"negotiated as a package" — that requires understanding the commercial negotiation, which sits with the deal
team and the accountant, not in the contract text alone. AI proposes the evidence; the professional decides
of the judgement.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.1 (the model overview); section 2.2.10 (contract modifications, which assume a contract
already exists under this step); KA 7.2 (contract management — approvals, milestones); KA 1.3 (accrual
accounting, relevant once revenue recognition begins).

### 2.2.3 Step 2 — Identifying performance obligations

**Definition and purpose.** A **performance obligation** is a promise in a contract to transfer a distinct
good or service (or a distinct bundle of goods or services) to the customer. A single contract price can hide
several separate performance obligations, each of which must be identified individually because each may be
recognised on a different pattern and timing (Step 5). A promised good or service is **distinct** if it meets
two conditions, both of which must hold: (a) the customer can benefit from the good or service either on its
own or together with other resources readily available to it (**capable of being distinct**); and (b) the
entity's promise to transfer the good or service is separately identifiable from other promises in the
contract — it is not so integrated with, or so significantly customising or dependent on, other promised
items that the two are, in substance, one combined output (**distinct within the context of the contract**).

**The underlying principle.** The test exists because commercial contracts routinely bundle genuinely
separate deliverables (equipment plus an unrelated maintenance service) alongside genuinely integrated
deliverables (a bespoke component that is meaningless without a complex, customised installation process
built specifically around it). Only the former should be split into separate performance obligations; the
latter should be treated as a single, combined obligation because that is what the entity is really promising
to deliver — a finished, integrated outcome, not a collection of parts.

**Formula.** No numerical formula; a two-part logical test: **Distinct = Capable of being distinct AND
Distinct within the context of the contract.**

**Example 2.2.3 — separating TechBuild's contract into performance obligations.**

*Scenario/Required.* Using the $540,000 (combined $600,000 including the control panel, per Example 2.2.2b —
simplified back to the core $540,000 SCADA agreement for this illustration) TechBuild/Northbridge contract,
identify the distinct performance obligations.

*Working.*

| Candidate item | Capable of being distinct? | Distinct in context of contract? | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| SCADA equipment supply & standard installation | Yes — a functioning SCADA system with standard commissioning | Yes — standard installation is routine commissioning, not significant customisation of the equipment itself; not highly interdependent with the other two items | Distinct performance obligation |
| 2-year maintenance service | Yes — Northbridge could, in principle, source maintenance from a third party | Yes — separately priced, separately timed, not integrated into equipment delivery | Distinct performance obligation |
| Operator training | Yes — a stand-alone training service, deliverable independently | Yes — training does not modify or depend on the equipment or maintenance | Distinct performance obligation |

*Answer.* **Three performance obligations: equipment & installation; maintenance; training.**

*Interpretation.* This is the same conclusion previewed in Example 2.2.1; the value of working the test
explicitly is that it is repeatable and defensible — a reviewer or auditor can trace the "yes/yes" answer for
each item rather than relying on an assumption that "three deliverables means three obligations," which is
not always true (Example 2.2.3b).

**Example 2.2.3b — a contrast where installation is not distinct.** Suppose instead that TechBuild's
installation scope required extensive, bespoke re-engineering of Northbridge's legacy control architecture —
custom firmware, non-standard interfacing, and integration testing specific to that one site, such that the
equipment cannot function as promised without this significant customisation work. Here, the installation
significantly modifies the equipment and the two are highly interdependent: the customer is not really buying
"equipment" and "installation" separately — it is buying one integrated, functioning system. The two items
fail the "distinct within the context of the contract" test and must be **combined into a single performance
obligation** (equipment-plus-integration), even though maintenance and training remain separately distinct.
This is a common exam-style nuance: the same two labels ("equipment," "installation") can be either two
performance obligations or one, depending entirely on the facts of how integrated the work genuinely is.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Assuming the number of line items on an invoice or quotation equals the number of
performance obligations — pricing structure is not the test; distinctness is. (2) Missing a "series" of
substantially the same distinct goods or services delivered over time (for example, monthly maintenance
visits) which, if each period is distinct and the pattern of transfer is the same, is accounted for as a
single performance obligation satisfied over time (relevant to sections 2.2.6–2.2.7) rather than many tiny separate
obligations. (3) Over-splitting genuinely integrated work into artificial "components" to manipulate the
timing of revenue recognition — the distinctness test is a matter of substance, not a drafting choice.

**AI in this topic.** Natural-language-processing tools can scan a contract's scope-of-work and technical
schedules and propose a candidate list of performance obligations by pattern-matching against clause
structure and defined terms (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.4). They are considerably weaker at the
"distinct within the context of the contract" half of the test, which requires understanding the *engineering
and commercial substance* of how integrated the work really is (Example 2.2.3b) — a judgement that depends on
technical facts a text-extraction tool cannot independently verify. A proposed AI-generated list of
obligations should always be reviewed against the underlying scope and, where relevant, technical
specifications, by someone who understands both the accounting test and the engineering reality.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.1 (model overview); section 2.2.5 (allocating the transaction price once obligations are
identified); section 2.2.6 (recognition pattern, which can differ by obligation); KA 7.1 (contract types, which
shape how bundled deliverables are typically structured).

### 2.2.4 Step 3 — Determining the transaction price

**Definition and purpose.** The **transaction price** is the amount of consideration an entity expects to be
entitled to in exchange for transferring the promised goods or services, excluding amounts collected on
behalf of third parties (such as sales taxes collected as agent for a tax authority). Determining it requires
considering five factors: (i) **fixed consideration** stated in the contract; (ii) **variable consideration**
— amounts that depend on future events such as bonuses, penalties, discounts, rebates or refunds (developed
fully in section 2.2.8, including the constraint on how much variable consideration may be included); (iii) a
**significant financing component** — where the timing of payments provides either party with a meaningful
benefit of financing (developed fully in section 2.2.9); (iv) **non-cash consideration**, measured at fair value; and
(v) **consideration payable to the customer** (rebates, credits or similar amounts the entity pays or credits
to the customer), which reduces the transaction price unless it is paid in exchange for a distinct good or
service the customer transfers to the entity.

**The underlying principle.** The transaction price is not simply "the contract price" read off the cover
page — it is the entity's best current estimate of what it will genuinely be entitled to receive, adjusted for
all five factors above, reassessed as circumstances change over the life of the contract. Two of the five
factors (variable consideration and significant financing components) are substantial enough to be given
their own dedicated topics later in this chapter; this topic establishes the full picture and works through
the remaining two (non-cash consideration and consideration payable to the customer) in a clean, self-
contained example.

**Formula.**

`Transaction price = Fixed consideration + Variable consideration (constrained, section 2.2.8) ± Significant
financing adjustment (section 2.2.9) + Non-cash consideration (at fair value) − Consideration payable to the
customer (not for a distinct good/service)`

**Example 2.2.4 — non-cash consideration and consideration payable to the customer.**

*Scenario.* Apex Site Services performs demolition and site-clearance work for a property developer. In
exchange, the developer pays $180,000 in cash and transfers to Apex a small parcel of surplus land, with a
reliably measurable fair value of $75,000. Apex separately agrees to pay the developer a $5,000 site-
tidiness rebate that is not conditional on, or in exchange for, any distinct good or service from the
developer.

*Required.* Determine the transaction price.

*Working.*
`Cash consideration = 180,000`
`Non-cash consideration (land, at fair value) = 75,000`
`Subtotal = 180,000 + 75,000 = 255,000`
`Less: consideration payable to customer (rebate, not for a distinct good/service) = (5,000)`
`Transaction price = 255,000 − 5,000 = 250,000`

*Answer.* **Transaction price = $250,000.**

*Interpretation.* Two adjustments moved the transaction price away from the $180,000 headline cash figure: an
upward adjustment for genuine non-cash value received, and a downward adjustment for a rebate that gives
nothing distinct back to Apex. If the fair value of the land could not be reliably measured directly, the
fallback is to estimate the non-cash consideration indirectly by reference to the standalone selling price of
the service Apex is providing — a reminder that Steps 3 and 4 (section 2.2.5) are closely linked wherever direct fair
values are unavailable.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Recognising non-cash consideration at the *customer's* cost of the asset transferred
rather than its fair value to the entity receiving it. (2) Failing to reduce the transaction price for
consideration payable to the customer that is not for a distinct good or service — a common miss where a
"marketing contribution" or volume rebate is, in substance, just a discount. (3) Treating consideration
payable to a customer's own customer (rather than the direct contracting party) as outside the scope of this
adjustment — the same principle generally extends down the distribution chain where relevant.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted contract- and clause-extraction tools can flag non-cash consideration
clauses (asset transfers, barter arrangements) and consideration-payable-to-customer clauses (rebates,
credits, marketing funds) across a contract population far faster than manual review, which is valuable for
identifying which contracts need this adjustment at all (cross-reference KA 13.4). Measuring the *fair value*
of a non-cash asset, and judging whether a rebate is genuinely "for a distinct good or service" or is, in
substance, a price reduction, both remain accounting judgements that the professional must make and document
— an AI-flagged clause is a prompt for review, not a valuation.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.5 (allocating the resulting transaction price across performance obligations);
section 2.2.8 (variable consideration and the constraint, in full); section 2.2.9 (significant financing components, in
full); KA 2.4 (fair value concepts as they touch other standards, e.g. IAS 16).

### 2.2.5 Step 4 — Allocating the transaction price

**Definition and purpose.** Once the transaction price is determined (section 2.2.4) and the contract's distinct
performance obligations are identified (section 2.2.3), the price must be **allocated** across those obligations —
because each will typically be recognised on a different pattern (section 2.2.6). The default allocation basis is
each obligation's relative **standalone selling price (SSP)**: the price at which the entity would sell that
good or service separately to a similar customer in similar circumstances. Where a discount is embedded in
the overall contract price relative to the sum of the individual SSPs, that discount is allocated
proportionally across all the obligations unless there is observable evidence the discount relates entirely
to one or some of them.

**The underlying principle.** Allocating by relative SSP — rather than by whatever line-item prices happen to
appear on the contract's payment schedule — ensures that revenue recognised for each obligation reflects the
economic value genuinely being transferred, not an arbitrary or negotiated pricing structure that might, for
example, front-load price onto the item delivered earliest to accelerate reported revenue. Where an SSP is
not directly observable (the good or service is not sold separately, or its pricing is highly variable), the
entity estimates it using one of three approaches, described here in this chapter's own words: the
**adjusted market assessment approach** (what would the market pay for this good or service, adjusted for the
entity's specific circumstances?); the **expected cost-plus-a-margin approach** (the entity's expected cost to
fulfil the obligation, plus an appropriate margin); and, in limited circumstances where the SSP is highly
uncertain or variable and not reliably estimable by the other two methods, the **residual approach** (total
transaction price less the sum of the observable SSPs of the other obligations).

**Formula.**

`Allocated transaction price (obligation i) = Transaction price × (SSP of obligation i / Σ SSPs of all
obligations)`

**Example 2.2.5 — allocating TechBuild's $540,000 transaction price.**

*Scenario.* TechBuild's three performance obligations (section 2.2.3) have the following standalone selling prices:
equipment & installation, estimated via the adjusted market assessment approach using observable prices for
comparable SCADA packages, **$400,000**; 2-year maintenance, estimated via the expected cost-plus-a-margin
approach, **$150,000**; training, estimated via the adjusted market assessment approach using observable
day-rates for comparable training, **$50,000**.

*Required.* Allocate the $540,000 transaction price.

*Working.*
`Σ SSPs = 400,000 + 150,000 + 50,000 = 600,000`
`Allocation ratio = 540,000 / 600,000 = 0.90 (a 10.0% discount spread proportionally across all three)`
`Equipment: 400,000 × 0.90 = 360,000`
`Maintenance: 150,000 × 0.90 = 135,000`
`Training: 50,000 × 0.90 = 45,000`
`Check: 360,000 + 135,000 + 45,000 = 540,000`

*Answer.* **Equipment $360,000; Maintenance $135,000; Training $45,000 — reconciling exactly to the $540,000
transaction price.**

*Interpretation.* The contract's overall 10.0% discount (relative to the sum of standalone prices) is spread
proportionally across all three obligations because there is no observable evidence that the discount relates
specifically to any one of them (for example, no separate discount clause tied only to training). If
Northbridge had instead negotiated the training specifically at a steep, documented discount because it was
purchasing training separately from another supplier for comparison, that evidence could support allocating
more of the discount to training alone.

**Example 2.2.5b — the residual approach.** A separate contract bundles observable, market-priced hardware
(SSP $220,000, reliably estimable via the adjusted market assessment approach) with a bespoke software
customisation whose pricing is highly variable across past deals and not reliably estimable by either the
market-assessment or cost-plus approach. Total transaction price: $300,000.

`Software SSP (residual) = Total transaction price − SSP of obligations estimable by other methods`
`= 300,000 − 220,000 = 80,000`

The residual approach is used here **only** because the software's SSP is genuinely too uncertain to estimate
directly — it is not a general-purpose shortcut, and using it where a reasonable direct estimate is available
for every obligation would understate the rigour the standard expects.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Using contractually stated line-item prices as if they were standalone selling
prices — they frequently are not, especially where one item is priced as a loss-leader to win the deal. (2)
Applying the residual approach as a default rather than a last resort for a genuinely unreliable SSP. (3)
Allocating a contract-level discount entirely to the most conveniently timed obligation (for example, pushing
all of it onto a later-delivered obligation to accelerate near-term revenue) without observable evidence that
the discount actually relates to that obligation.

**AI in this topic.** Pricing-analytics tools can estimate SSPs from historical transaction data using the
adjusted market assessment or expected cost-plus approaches at scale — genuinely useful where an entity has a
large volume of similar standalone sales to draw on (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). Judging whether an
SSP is reliably estimable at all, selecting the residual approach only where genuinely warranted, and
assessing whether a discount relates to specific obligations rather than the whole bundle, remain professional
judgements an AI-generated pricing estimate should be checked against, not a replacement for them.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.3 (identifying the obligations being allocated to); section 2.2.4 (the transaction price
being allocated); section 2.2.6 (how each allocated amount is then recognised); KA 7.3 (Bills of Quantities pricing,

### 2.2.6 Step 5 — Recognising revenue: over-time versus point-in-time

**Definition and purpose.** The final step recognises each performance obligation's allocated transaction
price as the obligation is satisfied — that is, as **control** of the promised good or service transfers to
the customer. Control can transfer **over time** or **at a point in time**, and the distinction matters
enormously: over-time recognition spreads revenue across the performance period as it occurs; point-in-time
recognition recognises the full allocated amount in a single moment. A performance obligation is satisfied
**over time** if it meets at least one of three criteria (stated here in this chapter's own words): (a) the
customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefits of the entity's performance as the entity performs
(typical of routine, recurring services); (b) the entity's performance creates or enhances an asset that the
customer controls as the asset is created or enhanced (typical of work performed directly on a customer-owned
site or asset); or (c) the entity's performance does not create an asset with an alternative use to the
entity, **and** the entity has an enforceable right to payment for performance completed to date (typical of
a bespoke, made-to-order asset). If **none** of the three criteria is met, the obligation is satisfied — and
revenue recognised — **at a point in time**, based on indicators of control transfer: the entity has a present
right to payment, the customer holds legal title, the entity has transferred physical possession, the
customer has the significant risks and rewards of ownership, and the customer has accepted the asset.

**The underlying principle.** The test asks, in substance: as work proceeds, is the *customer* progressively
gaining control of something of value (over time), or is the entity building or holding something that only
becomes the customer's at a single, identifiable handover moment (point in time)? A bespoke bridge built on
the customer's own right-of-way, which the entity could not realistically redirect to another buyer, and for
which the entity is contractually entitled to be paid for work done even if the customer cancels for
convenience, is the clearest case of criterion (c) and is recognised over time. Standard, off-the-shelf
equipment manufactured in a factory, which could in principle be redirected to a different customer before
installation, does not meet any of the three criteria and is recognised at the point of commissioning and
acceptance.

**Formula.** No numerical formula at this step; a **decision test**: `Over time if (a) OR (b) OR (c);
otherwise, point in time based on control-transfer indicators.` The *measurement* of over-time progress (how
much revenue to recognise as time passes) is developed in section 2.2.7.

**Example 2.2.6 — classifying TechBuild's and Silverline's performance obligations.**

*Scenario/Required.* Assess each of TechBuild's three performance obligations (section 2.2.3) and Silverline
Infrastructure LLC's Route 9 Flyover Bridge contract (introduced in full in section 2.2.7 and developed as the
chapter's flagship construction case in section 2.2.12) against the three over-time criteria.

*Working.*

| Performance obligation | Criterion (a) met? | Criterion (b) met? | Criterion (c) met? | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TechBuild — equipment & installation | No — customer does not consume benefit as work proceeds | No — asset is not built directly on/into a customer-controlled asset until commissioning | No — the SCADA unit could, before installation, realistically be redirected to another customer at modest cost, and no enforceable right to progress payment for work-in-process exists | **None met — point in time** (on commissioning/acceptance) |
| TechBuild — 2-year maintenance | **Yes** — the customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefit of stand-ready maintenance cover as it is provided | — | — | **Over time** (straight-line, section 2.2.7) |
| TechBuild — training | No | No | No — a discrete, deliverable training event with acceptance at completion | **Point in time** (on delivery) |
| Silverline — Route 9 Flyover Bridge (single PO) | No | Arguably yes, but criterion (c) is the clearer, decisive test here | **Yes** — a bespoke bridge design has no alternative use to Silverline, and the contract entitles Silverline to payment for work completed to date even on termination for convenience | **Over time** (input method, section 2.2.7) |

*Answer.* Four performance obligations assessed; three distinct outcomes reached (two point-in-time, two
over-time) from four different underlying fact patterns.

*Interpretation.* Note that TechBuild's equipment and Silverline's bridge look superficially similar (both
are "we build/supply something for a customer") but reach opposite conclusions, because the *facts* differ:
alternative use and enforceable payment-for-work-to-date are what separate a standard product sale from a
bespoke, over-time construction contract — not the industry or the contract's size.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Assuming all construction-type contracts are automatically over-time — a contract
for a standardised, resaleable modular unit, with no enforceable right to progress payment and no
customer-specific customisation, could fail all three criteria and be point-in-time. (2) Assuming "the
customer owns the land, therefore criterion (b) is automatically met" without checking whether the entity's
performance is genuinely creating or enhancing an asset the customer controls *as it is created*, rather than
only once complete. (3) Overlooking that criterion (c) requires **both** no alternative use **and** an
enforceable right to payment for performance to date — a bespoke asset with no alternative use but *no*
enforceable payment right for interim work does not meet criterion (c).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted clause classifiers can scan termination and payment clauses across a
contract population and flag likely candidates for "enforceable right to payment for performance to date" —
a genuinely useful triage step before manual legal and accounting review (cross-reference KA 13.4). Whether an
asset truly has "no alternative use," which requires understanding both the contractual and the practical/
technical redirection constraints, is a judgement an AI tool cannot make reliably from contract text alone; it
must be assessed by someone who understands the asset and the market it could (or could not) be redirected
into.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.5 (the allocated amounts being recognised here); section 2.2.7 (measuring over-time
progress); section 2.2.12 (the full construction application for Route 9); KA 7.1 (contract types and how they shape
enforceable payment rights).

### 2.2.7 Measuring progress over time — input and output methods

**Definition and purpose.** For a performance obligation satisfied over time (section 2.2.6), revenue must be
recognised in a pattern that faithfully depicts the entity's progress toward completion. IFRS 15 recognises
two families of measurement method: **output methods**, which measure progress directly by reference to the
value transferred to the customer (units produced or delivered, milestones achieved, an independent survey of
work performed, or — for a service delivered evenly over time, such as TechBuild's maintenance — straight-line
recognition), and **input methods**, which measure progress indirectly by reference to the entity's efforts or
inputs consumed relative to the total expected inputs (most commonly, costs incurred as a proportion of total
estimated costs — the classic **cost-to-cost** approach that is the direct descendant of the traditional
percentage-of-completion method long used in construction accounting).

**The underlying principle.** An input method is a *proxy*: it assumes that resources are consumed in
reasonably direct proportion to value delivered to the customer. That assumption breaks down wherever costs
are incurred that do not, by themselves, represent progress toward satisfying the obligation — most notably,
materials purchased and delivered to site but not yet installed or used. A single, consistent method must be
selected for each performance obligation, based on the nature of the good or service, and applied consistently
throughout the obligation's life — it is not switched between periods to produce a more favourable result.

**Formula.**

`Input method (cost-to-cost) % complete = Costs incurred to date / Total estimated costs`
`Cumulative revenue recognised = % complete × Transaction price allocated to the performance obligation`
`Output method % complete = Units delivered (or milestones achieved, or surveyed physical progress) / Total
units (or total milestones, or 100% of surveyed scope)`

**Example 2.2.7 — comparing input and output methods: Route 9 Flyover Bridge, Year 1.**

*Scenario.* Silverline Infrastructure LLC contracts with the Metro Transit Authority to design and build the
Route 9 Flyover Bridge for a fixed price of **$20,000,000** (SAR 75,000,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 =
USD 1). The contract is a single performance obligation satisfied over time (Example 2.2.6, criterion (c)).
At inception, total estimated costs to complete are **$15,000,000** (implying an expected margin of
$5,000,000, or 25.0% of contract price). By the end of Year 1, costs incurred are **$4,500,000**, and an
independent engineer's survey separately certifies physical completion at **32.0%**.

*Required.* Compute revenue recognised for Year 1 under (a) the input (cost-to-cost) method and (b) the
output (survey) method, and compare.

*Working.*

(a) Input method:
`% complete = 4,500,000 / 15,000,000 = 0.300 = 30.0%`
`Revenue = 30.0% × 20,000,000 = 6,000,000`

(b) Output method (engineer's certified survey):
`% complete = 32.0% (directly observed)`
`Revenue = 32.0% × 20,000,000 = 6,400,000`

*Answer.* **Input method: $6,000,000. Output method: $6,400,000 — a $400,000 difference for the same
underlying project in the same year.**

*Interpretation.* The two methods are not interchangeable estimates of the same fact; they measure progress
differently, and Silverline must select one method for this performance obligation — appropriate to the
nature of the work — and apply it consistently for the life of the contract, not alternate between whichever
gives the higher figure in a given period. For a bespoke, cost-driven construction contract like this one,
the input (cost-to-cost) method is the conventional and most commonly supportable choice, precisely because it
is anchored to auditable cost-ledger data rather than to a survey estimate that is itself subject to
professional judgement. This chapter's flagship multi-year schedule (section 2.2.12) therefore proceeds on the input
method, consistent with this selection.

**Example 2.2.7b — adjusting for uninstalled materials (illustrative mini-case; not part of the Route 9
running schedule used elsewhere in this chapter).** Suppose that of the $4,500,000 of Year 1 costs incurred
above, $1,000,000 relates to structural steel reinforcement purchased and delivered to site but not yet
installed — a cost incurred, but not yet representing progress toward the customer actually receiving the
finished structure. Including it in the cost-to-cost ratio at full margin overstates progress. The adjusted
treatment excludes the uninstalled cost from the progress ratio and recognises it separately, at cost only
(no margin), until it is installed:

`Adjusted progress costs = 4,500,000 − 1,000,000 = 3,500,000`
`Adjusted % complete = 3,500,000 / 15,000,000 = 23.3%`
`Progress-based revenue = 23.3% × 20,000,000 = 4,666,667`
`Plus: uninstalled materials, recognised at cost (no margin) = 1,000,000`
`Adjusted total revenue = 4,666,667 + 1,000,000 = 5,666,667`

Compared with the unadjusted (naive) figure of $6,000,000, the adjusted treatment recognises **$333,333
less** revenue — and correspondingly less margin — in Year 1, deferring it until the steel is actually
installed and genuinely represents progress. This is flagged as illustrative only: the main Route 9 schedule
carried through section 2.2.11–section 2.2.12 does not assume any uninstalled-materials issue, to keep that flagship
reconciliation clean; the adjustment mechanism is shown here purely to illustrate the principle.

to the automated data, not replaced by it.

### 2.2.8 Variable consideration and the constraint

**Definition and purpose.** Many contracts include consideration that varies with future events — performance
bonuses, liquidated-damages penalties, discounts, rebates, refunds, or price concessions. IFRS 15 requires an
entity to **estimate** variable consideration using one of two methods — the **expected value** (the
probability-weighted sum of a range of possible consideration amounts, best suited where there are many
possible outcomes or a large number of similar contracts) or the **most likely amount** (the single most
probable outcome, best suited where there are essentially two possible outcomes, such as a binary bonus/no-
bonus clause) — and then apply **the constraint**: variable consideration is included in the transaction
price only to the extent it is highly probable that a significant reversal of cumulative revenue will not
occur when the uncertainty is later resolved.

**The underlying principle.** Without the constraint, an entity could recognise revenue today for an
optimistic bonus or favourable outcome that later fails to materialise, forcing a large revenue *reversal* in
a future period — precisely the kind of front-loaded, unreliable revenue recognition the model is designed to
prevent. The constraint asks a second, deliberately more conservative question after the estimate is made:
even if this is genuinely our best estimate, how confident are we that we will not have to significantly claw
it back later? Only the portion that passes this second test enters the transaction price.

**Formula.**

`Expected value = Σ (possible outcome amount × probability of that outcome)`
`Most likely amount = the single outcome amount with the highest probability`
`Variable consideration included in transaction price = MIN(estimate under chosen method, the amount that is
highly probable not to result in a significant future revenue reversal)`

**Example 2.2.8 — expected value with the constraint: Coastal Pipeline Extension.**

*Scenario.* Coastal Energy Constructors LLC contracts with Delta Midstream Co. to extend a pipeline for a
fixed price of **$8,000,000** (SAR 30,000,000 at the illustrative rate). The contract includes an early-
completion bonus of $500,000 and liquidated damages for late completion. Management assesses three possible
outcomes based on the project schedule and historical experience on comparable marine-adjacent pipeline
works:

| Outcome | Probability | Consideration effect |
|---|---|---|
| On time or early | 55% | +$500,000 (bonus earned) |
| 1–4 weeks late (within grace period) | 30% | $0 |
| More than 4 weeks late | 15% | −$120,000 (liquidated damages) |

*Required.* Compute the expected value of variable consideration, then apply the constraint.

*Working.*
`Expected value = (0.55 × 500,000) + (0.30 × 0) + (0.15 × (−120,000))`
`= 275,000 + 0 − 18,000 = 257,000`

*Constraint.* Management's historical experience on similar marine-adjacent works shows the completion-date
outcome is highly sensitive to weather and tidal-window access, factors substantially outside Coastal's
control. Based on that experience, management concludes it is highly probable, without significant reversal
risk, that only **$150,000** of the $257,000 expected value will not need to be clawed back once the
completion date is known — the remainder is judged too uncertain to include.

`Constrained variable consideration = 150,000`
`Transaction price = Fixed consideration + Constrained variable consideration = 8,000,000 + 150,000 =
8,150,000`

*Answer.* **Transaction price = $8,150,000** (the full $257,000 expected value is *not* used — the
constrained estimate of $150,000 is).

*Interpretation.* The constraint is deliberately the more binding of the two tests here: the raw expected-
value calculation is mechanical, but the constraint requires a genuine, evidence-based judgement about
outcome uncertainty specific to this contract's risk profile — flagged explicitly in this chapter's "For SME
verification" section as a judgement a finance SME must review, since the $150,000 figure is illustrative and
would, in practice, need to be supported by documented historical outcome data.

**Example 2.2.8b — most likely amount, and a case where the constraint does not bind.** The same Coastal
Pipeline Extension contract separately includes a potential $200,000 retention deduction relating to a single
non-conformance report (NCR), with only two possible outcomes: the NCR is closed with no deduction (80%
probability) or the NCR results in the full $200,000 deduction (20% probability). Because there are only two
realistic outcomes, the **most likely amount** method is the more predictive estimate:

`Most likely amount = $0 (the 80%-probability outcome)`

*Constraint check.* The potential downside exposure if the less-likely outcome occurs is `0.20 × 200,000 =
40,000`, which is 0.5% of the $8,150,000 transaction price already determined above (`40,000 / 8,150,000 =
0.5%`) — management concludes this is not significant, so **no constraint-driven reduction is required**; the
$0 most-likely-amount estimate stands and the transaction price is **unaffected**, remaining $8,150,000.

*Interpretation.* Contrasting Examples 2.2.8 and 2.2.8b on the same contract shows both possible outcomes of
applying the constraint: in 2.2.8 it materially reduced the estimate that could be included; in 2.2.8b it did
not bind at all, because the potential reversal, while real, was judged immaterial relative to the total
transaction price. Both are correct applications of the same test — the constraint does not always reduce the
answer to zero, nor does it always leave it unchanged.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Choosing the expected value method for a genuinely binary (two-outcome) scenario,
which tends to produce an amount that is not actually any of the possible real-world outcomes (in Example
2.2.8b, an expected-value calculation would give `0.8×0 + 0.2×(−200,000) = −40,000`, a figure that will never
actually occur — the contract will end with either $0 or a $200,000 deduction, never $40,000). (2) Applying
the constraint only qualitatively ("we're fairly confident") without a documented, evidence-based basis, such
as historical outcome data on comparable contracts. (3) Forgetting to reassess variable consideration
estimates and the constraint at each reporting date as new information (weather delays actually experienced,
NCRs actually closed) becomes available — this is not a one-time estimate made at contract inception.

**AI in this topic.** Probability-weighting and Monte Carlo-style simulation tools can support the expected-
value calculation across a large number of contract outcomes or scenarios, and can help quantify historical
outcome-variability data to support the constraint assessment (cross-reference KA 12.2's quantitative risk
treatment, and KA 13.5). These tools materially strengthen the *evidence base* behind the estimate. The
judgement of how much weight to place on that evidence, and where exactly to draw the constrained-estimate
line, remains a professional accounting judgement — an area explicitly flagged for SME sign-off in this
chapter, since it is one of the most subjective figures in the entire revenue-recognition model.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.4 (variable consideration as one of the five transaction-price factors); section 2.2.12
(variable consideration in a construction contract context); KA 12.2 (quantitative risk analysis — expected
value and probability-weighted techniques used identically there); KA 7.2 (liquidated damages and bonus
clauses within contract management).

### 2.2.9 Significant financing components

**Definition and purpose.** Where the timing of payments agreed in a contract provides the customer or the
entity with a significant benefit of financing — because performance and payment are separated by a
meaningful period — the transaction price must be adjusted for the time value of money, and the difference
recognised as interest income or interest expense over the financing period, separately from revenue itself.
This applies whichever direction the financing runs: a customer paying substantially in **advance** of
performance effectively lends money to the entity (entity recognises the extra amount as **interest
expense**, accreting the liability up to the amount ultimately recognised as revenue); a customer paying
substantially **after** performance effectively borrows from the entity (entity recognises **interest
income**, accreting a receivable). As a practical matter, no adjustment is required where the timing gap
between payment and performance is expected to be one year or less.

**The underlying principle.** Revenue should reflect the price the customer would have paid for the good or
service **at the time of transfer**, not distorted upward or downward simply because payment happened to be
timed early or late. Separating out the financing element keeps revenue measuring what was actually
delivered, while the interest income/expense line correctly reflects the separate economic reality that cash
changed hands at a different time than performance.

**Formula.**

`Revenue recognised at transfer = Cash amount × (1 + discount rate)^n` (advance-payment case; n = periods
between payment and transfer)
`Interest expense/income (period t) = Opening liability/receivable balance × discount rate`
`No adjustment required if the expected timing gap between payment and performance is ≤ 12 months (practical
expedient)`

**Example 2.2.9 — advance payment: Delta Fabrication Inc.**

*Scenario.* Delta Fabrication Inc. agrees to design and fabricate a custom piece of equipment for Ashford
Municipal Authority. Ashford pays the full $1,000,000 price upfront on 1 January 2026; Delta will deliver the
completed equipment on 1 January 2028 — two years later. A rate of 6.0% per annum is judged to reflect the
financing characteristics of this arrangement.

*Required.* Determine the revenue to recognise on delivery and the interest expense over the two years.

*Working.*
`Year 1 accretion: 1,000,000 × 6.0% = 60,000 → liability grows from 1,000,000 to 1,060,000`
`Year 2 accretion: 1,060,000 × 6.0% = 63,600 → liability grows from 1,060,000 to 1,123,600`
`Total interest expense = 60,000 + 63,600 = 123,600`
`Revenue recognised on delivery (1 January 2028) = 1,123,600`

*Answer.* **Revenue recognised at delivery = $1,123,600; total interest expense over the two years =
$123,600.**

*Interpretation.* Delta received $1,000,000 in cash two years before performing, but recognises $1,123,600 of
revenue when it eventually delivers — the customer effectively paid a price reflecting what a delivery-date
cash price would have been, and the $123,600 gap is recognised as financing expense across the intervening
two years, not as revenue at any point. Had the payment instead fallen due within 12 months of delivery, the
practical expedient would apply and no such adjustment would be needed.

**Example 2.2.9b — the mirror case: deferred payment.** Suppose instead Delta delivers the same equipment
immediately (cash selling price $1,000,000) but Ashford does not pay until two years later, again at 6.0% per
annum.

`Revenue recognised at delivery (point in time) = 1,000,000 (the cash selling price)`
`Year 1 interest income: 1,000,000 × 6.0% = 60,000 → receivable grows from 1,000,000 to 1,060,000`
`Year 2 interest income: 1,060,000 × 6.0% = 63,600 → receivable grows from 1,060,000 to 1,123,600`
`Total interest income = 123,600, matching the $1,123,600 eventually collected`

*Interpretation.* The two mini-cases are mirror images: in the advance-payment case the entity is the
borrower (interest expense, revenue grossed up at delivery); in the deferred-payment case the entity is the
lender (interest income, revenue recognised immediately at the cash price). In both, the $123,600 financing
element is kept separate from revenue.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Confusing this adjustment with IAS 23's requirement to capitalise borrowing costs
directly attributable to constructing a qualifying asset (cross-reference KA 2.4) — the two operate on
different sides of the transaction (financing provided *by the customer* to the entity or vice versa, versus
the entity's *own* external borrowing cost) and can both apply to the same construction contract without
conflict. (2) Applying a financing adjustment to milestone or retention payment schedules that reflect
performance-related protection (ensuring the entity performs before being paid in full) rather than a genuine
financing arrangement — the standard's intent is not to treat every progress-billing schedule as embedding a
financing component. (3) Using an arbitrary discount rate rather than one that reflects the credit
characteristics of the party actually receiving the financing benefit in the specific arrangement.

**AI in this topic.** Time-value-of-money calculators and AI-assisted contract-clause extraction can identify
payment-timing gaps across a contract population and compute the mechanical present-value/accretion schedule
once a discount rate is confirmed (cross-reference KA 13.4). Selecting the appropriate discount rate, and
judging whether a payment-timing gap reflects genuine financing versus ordinary performance-related payment
terms (a construction retention, for example), remain professional judgements that determine whether this
topic even applies to a given contract.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.4 (financing as one of the five transaction-price factors); KA 2.4 (IAS 23
borrowing costs — a related but distinct concept); KA 7.4 (retention and progress payment structures, most of
which are performance-related rather than financing-related).

### 2.2.10 Contract modifications

**Definition and purpose.** A **contract modification** is a change in the scope or price (or both) of a
contract approved by both parties — a variation order, change order, or amendment. IFRS 15 requires each
modification to be classified into one of three treatments: (a) a **separate contract**, where the added
goods or services are distinct and priced at their standalone selling price; (b) **prospective treatment**
(in substance, termination of the existing contract and creation of a new one), where the remaining goods or
services to be delivered are distinct from those already transferred, but the additional consideration does
**not** reflect standalone selling price — the remaining, not-yet-recognised consideration from the original
contract is combined with the new consideration and reallocated across what remains to be delivered, on a
prospective basis, from the date of the modification; or (c) a **cumulative catch-up adjustment**, where the
remaining goods or services are **not** distinct from those already transferred (they are part of a single
performance obligation partially satisfied, as in over-time construction) — the modification is treated as if
it had always been part of the original contract, and revenue recognised to date is immediately adjusted (up
or down) to reflect the revised transaction price and scope.

**The underlying principle.** The three treatments exist because a modification can genuinely change three
different things: it can add a wholly separate deal (treatment a), it can change what remains to be delivered
under an existing relationship without touching what has already been delivered (treatment b), or it can
change the very obligation that is already partway through being delivered (treatment c) — and each requires
a different accounting response to keep revenue recognition faithful to what has actually happened.

**Formula.**

`(a) Separate contract: no adjustment to the original contract; new contract accounted for independently`
`(b) Prospective: New remaining price = Unrecognised consideration from original contract + New consideration;
reallocated over remaining distinct performance from the modification date`
`(c) Cumulative catch-up: Adjustment = (Revised % complete × Revised transaction price) − Revenue already
recognised to date`

**Example 2.2.10a — separate contract.** Metro Transit Authority separately requests additional signage for
the Route 9 Flyover Bridge, priced at $150,000 — a figure that equals the standalone selling price for
comparable signage work. Because the added scope is distinct and priced at its standalone selling price, this
is accounted for as a **wholly separate contract**, with no effect whatsoever on the accounting for the
original $20,000,000 flyover contract.

**Example 2.2.10b — prospective treatment (termination of the old, creation of a new).** Returning to
TechBuild's 24-month maintenance performance obligation (allocated price $135,000, recognised straight-line
at `135,000 / 24 = 5,625.00` per month, sections 2.2.6–2.2.7): at the end of month 12, Northbridge extends the
maintenance term by a further 12 months for additional consideration of $60,000 — a price that does **not**
reflect the standalone selling price of a further 12 months of maintenance, which is estimated at $75,000.
Because the extended maintenance is distinct (a further period of the same stand-ready service) but not
priced at its standalone selling price, this is treated prospectively:

`Revenue recognised, months 1–12 = 12 × 5,625 = 67,500 (unchanged, not restated)`
`Remaining unrecognised consideration from the original contract = 135,000 − 67,500 = 67,500`
`Combined remaining consideration = 67,500 (original) + 60,000 (new) = 127,500`
`New monthly recognition, months 13–36 (24 remaining months: 12 original + 12 new) = 127,500 / 24 = 5,312.50`

*Interpretation.* Revenue already recognised in months 1–12 is untouched; only the rate applied prospectively
from month 13 onward changes, from $5,625.00 to $5,312.50 per month — a lower rate, reflecting that the
extension was priced at less than its standalone value and that discount is spread over the whole remaining
period rather than loaded entirely onto the extension.

**Example 2.2.10c — cumulative catch-up adjustment (illustrative variant based on Route 9 Flyover Bridge; not
part of the 3-year schedule used in section 2.2.12).** At the start of Year 2, after Year 1's costs incurred of
$4,500,000 and revenue recognised of $6,000,000 (section 2.2.7), Metro Transit Authority approves a variation adding
an extra lane to the flyover: **+$3,000,000** to the contract price and **+$2,000,000** to estimated costs.
Because the additional lane is integrated into, and not distinct from, the single over-time performance
obligation already partway complete, this is a **cumulative catch-up** adjustment.

`New contract price = 20,000,000 + 3,000,000 = 23,000,000`
`New total estimated cost = 15,000,000 + 2,000,000 = 17,000,000`
`Revised % complete (cumulative) = 4,500,000 / 17,000,000 = 26.5% (26.47058...%, unrounded)`
`Revised cumulative revenue = 26.47058...% × 23,000,000 = 6,088,235 (rounded to the nearest dollar)`
`Cumulative catch-up adjustment = 6,088,235 − 6,000,000 already recognised = 88,235`

*Interpretation.* Silverline recognises an **additional $88,235 of revenue immediately** in the period the
modification is approved — on top of Year 2's normal progress-based revenue, which from this point is
measured against the new $23,000,000 price and $17,000,000 cost base. This illustrative variant is kept
separate from the clean, un-modified 3-year Route 9 schedule developed in section 2.2.11–section 2.2.12 so that the
flagship reconciliation there is not disturbed by this optional variation-order scenario.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Applying prospective treatment to a modification of a single, continuous over-time
performance obligation (like a construction contract's core scope) — that combination is unusual and, in most
cases, a genuine scope change to an already-partly-satisfied single obligation calls for cumulative catch-up,
not prospective reallocation; this is a genuinely complex judgement area, flagged for SME review. (2)
Forgetting to reassess whether the *modified* contract still meets the Step 1 contract-existence criteria and
whether the modified scope changes the Step 2 performance-obligation analysis. (3) Netting a cumulative
catch-up adjustment against ordinary period revenue in reporting without disclosing it separately where
material, obscuring a one-off adjustment within routine progress revenue.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted contract-comparison tools can detect that a variation order or amendment has
been executed (by comparing successive contract versions or by reading a change-order log) and can propose a
preliminary classification among the three treatments based on typical fact patterns (cross-reference
KA 13.4, KA 5.4 on change control). Classifying a modification correctly is one of the more judgement-heavy
areas of the standard — particularly distinguishing prospective from cumulative-catch-up treatment on a
single continuous performance obligation — and an AI-proposed classification must be reviewed and the
rationale documented by the professional before it is applied.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.2 (the contract-existence criteria a modified contract must still meet); section 2.2.7
(the % complete calculation cumulative catch-up feeds into); section 2.2.12 (the full construction application);
KA 5.4 (change control and cost impact — the operational trigger for a modification); KA 7.2 (variations and
claims within contract management).

### 2.2.11 Contract assets and contract liabilities

**Definition and purpose.** Because revenue recognition (Steps 2–5) and billing (a commercial, contractual
process) are not the same thing, a **contract asset** or **contract liability** arises whenever cumulative
revenue recognised and cumulative amounts billed diverge. A **contract asset** exists where revenue recognised
exceeds amounts billed — the entity has performed more than it has billed for, and its right to that excess
consideration is **conditional** on something other than the mere passage of time (typically, further
performance or a future certification event). A **contract liability** exists where amounts billed exceed
revenue recognised — the entity has billed (and often collected) more than it has earned, and owes further
performance. Neither should be confused with a **receivable**: a receivable is an **unconditional** right to
consideration — once an amount has been billed (or certified), the only thing standing between the entity and
payment is the passage of time, regardless of how much of the underlying performance obligation remains.

**The underlying principle.** Billing schedules in commercial contracts are negotiated for cash-flow and risk-
management reasons (mobilisation advances, retention, milestone payments) and routinely diverge from the
pattern of revenue recognition, which instead follows the performance measured in sections 2.2.6–2.2.7. Tracking the
contract asset/liability position at each reporting date is therefore essential both for correct balance-sheet
presentation and — from a project controls perspective — as a direct, quantified signal of whether a contract
is running ahead of or behind its billing plan.

**Formula.** As stated in this Body of Knowledge's canonical formula table:

`Contract asset/(liability), cumulative = Cumulative revenue recognised − Cumulative amount billed`

A positive result is a contract **asset**; a negative result is a contract **liability** of that magnitude.
This is the identical convention used for over-/under-billing in KA 7.5's treatment of the commercial cycle —
do not duplicate that treatment here; this topic establishes the accounting mechanics, KA 7.5 develops the
operational billing-cycle view built on it.

**Example 2.2.11 — the Route 9 Flyover Bridge contract asset/liability waterfall.**

*Scenario.* Using Silverline's Route 9 Flyover Bridge contract (contract price $20,000,000; Year 1 revenue
$6,000,000 per section 2.2.7), cumulative amounts billed to Metro Transit Authority (per the agreed progress-billing
schedule) are:

| Year | Revenue recognised, cumulative ($) | Amount billed, cumulative ($) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6,000,000 | 6,800,000 |
| 2 | 14,500,000 | 14,300,000 |
| 3 | 20,000,000 | 20,000,000 |

*Required.* Compute the contract asset/(liability) position at the end of each year.

*Working.*
`Year 1: 6,000,000 − 6,800,000 = (800,000) → Contract liability of $800,000`
`Year 2: 14,500,000 − 14,300,000 = 200,000 → Contract asset of $200,000`
`Year 3: 20,000,000 − 20,000,000 = 0 → Fully settled at completion`

*Answer.* **Year 1: contract liability $800,000. Year 2: contract asset $200,000. Year 3: $0.**

*Interpretation.* The position **flips** from a liability to an asset between Year 1 and Year 2: Silverline
was billing ahead of performance in Year 1 (a mobilisation-heavy billing schedule, common on infrastructure
contracts), then billing fell behind the pace of revenue recognition in Year 2, before both converge to zero
exactly at contract completion — as they always must, since cumulative billing must eventually equal
cumulative revenue on a contract billed in full for its total price. Billing by year reconciles independently:
`6,800,000 (Y1) + 7,500,000 (Y2, = 14,300,000 − 6,800,000) + 5,700,000 (Y3, = 20,000,000 − 14,300,000) =
20,000,000`, matching the total contract price exactly.

`#1D4ED8`) or below it (contract liability, neutral grey), one bar per year.
*Underlying data:*

|---|---|---|---|

**Example 2.2.11b — contract liability and accounts receivable coexisting.** At the end of Year 1, of the
$6,800,000 cumulative amount billed, Metro Transit Authority has paid $6,300,000 in cash.

`Accounts receivable = Amount billed − Amount collected = 6,800,000 − 6,300,000 = 500,000`
`Contract liability (from Example 2.2.11) = 800,000`

Both balances appear on Silverline's Statement of Financial Position **simultaneously**: an accounts
receivable **asset** of $500,000 (an unconditional right to be paid for what has already been billed) and a
contract **liability** of $800,000 (Silverline owes further performance against amounts already billed and
partly collected). These are not netted against one another and are not the same figure measuring the same
thing twice — one tracks collection risk on amounts already billed; the other tracks remaining unsatisfied
performance against amounts already billed. This coexistence is a common point of confusion and is worth
re-deriving by hand whenever both balances appear together on a contract.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating "amount billed but not yet paid" (a receivable) and "revenue not yet
earned relative to billing" (a contract liability) as the same balance — they are computed from different
comparisons and can both exist at once, as Example 2.2.11b shows. (2) Assuming a contract asset is
automatically a receivable once it appears on the balance sheet — it remains conditional (typically on
further performance) until billed, at which point the billed portion becomes an unconditional receivable and
only the excess, if any, remains a contract asset. (3) Failing to reconcile the contract asset/liability
roll-forward to the billing schedule and the revenue schedule independently each period — an unreconciled
break usually signals a coding, billing-cutoff, or revenue-recognition error.

**AI in this topic.** Automated reconciliation tools can compute the contract asset/liability roll-forward
directly from the billing system and the revenue-recognition sub-ledger, flag any period where the two do not
tie out to the reported balance, and produce the waterfall view shown in Fig 2.2.11 without manual
spreadsheet work (cross-reference KA 13.5). This is a strong automation candidate given how mechanical the
calculation is once revenue and billing data are both reliable — but it depends entirely on the underlying
revenue-recognition judgement (section 2.2.2–section 2.2.10) already having been correctly applied; an automated waterfall
built on an incorrect % complete is simply a fast, wrong waterfall, and the professional remains accountable
for the inputs before the roll-forward is relied upon.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.7 (the revenue figures feeding this waterfall); section 2.2.12 (the waterfall integrated
into the full multi-year schedule); KA 7.4 (invoicing and applications for payment — how "amount billed" is
generated); KA 7.5 (over-/under-billing in the commercial cycle — the operational view of this same identity;
do not duplicate, cross-refer).

### 2.2.12 Construction and long-term contract application — the full multi-year schedule

**Definition and purpose.** This topic brings together every element of the model — the single over-time
performance obligation (section 2.2.6), the input (cost-to-cost) measurement of progress (section 2.2.7), contract
modifications where relevant (section 2.2.10), and the contract asset/liability waterfall (section 2.2.11) — into the
complete, multi-year worked application that a project controls professional will recognise as the direct
formal counterpart of the percentage-of-completion method long used operationally in construction. This is
the chapter's centrepiece worked example.

**The underlying principle.** On a single-performance-obligation, cost-driven construction contract, three
things must be tracked every reporting period, consistently: **costs incurred** (from the accrual-adjusted
cost ledger — the same discipline as KA 6.1's treatment of Actual Cost), the **total estimated cost to
complete** (re-forecast each period as new information emerges — the same discipline as KA 6.3's EAC
treatment), and the **cumulative revenue** those two together imply via the input-method formula (section 2.2.7).
Because the input method defines cumulative cost of revenue to equal costs incurred to date by construction,
cumulative gross margin recognised is always exactly `% complete × total estimated margin` — a useful
internal check performed automatically each period. At completion, the final year always "true-ups" any
estimation error accumulated in earlier periods, because at 100% complete, revenue recognised must equal the
full contract price and cost of revenue must equal actual total cost, regardless of what earlier estimates
assumed.

**Formula.** As established in section 2.2.7, applied cumulatively across periods:

`% complete (Year t, cumulative) = Costs incurred to date / Total estimated costs (as revised at Year t)`
`Revenue recognised (Year t, cumulative) = % complete (Year t) × Contract price`
`Revenue recognised (Year t, period) = Revenue (Year t, cumulative) − Revenue (Year t−1, cumulative)`
`Cost of revenue (Year t, period) = Costs incurred (Year t, period)`
`Gross margin (Year t, period) = Revenue (Year t, period) − Cost of revenue (Year t, period)`

**Example 2.2.12 — Route 9 Flyover Bridge: the complete three-year schedule.**

*Scenario.* Silverline Infrastructure LLC's Route 9 Flyover Bridge contract: fixed price **$20,000,000**
(SAR 75,000,000 at the illustrative rate); original total estimated cost at inception **$15,000,000**
(original expected margin $5,000,000, 25.0%). Progress and cost re-forecasts across the contract's three
years are as follows (no modification is assumed in this core schedule — see Example 2.2.10c for the
modification variant kept separate from this reconciliation):

| Year | Costs incurred, period ($) | Total estimated cost, cumulative, as revised ($) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4,500,000 | 15,000,000 (original estimate) | No revision yet |
| 2 | 7,100,000 | 16,000,000 (revised up) | Ground conditions and schedule pressure increase the cost-to-complete estimate |
| 3 | 4,600,000 | 16,200,000 (final, actual) | Contract completes; final actual cost known |

Cumulative amounts billed each year are as previously given in Example 2.2.11: $6,800,000 (Year 1),
$14,300,000 (Year 2, cumulative), $20,000,000 (Year 3, cumulative, final).

*Required.* Prepare the full three-year revenue, cost, margin and contract asset/liability schedule, and
reconcile to the contract total.

*Working — Year 1.*
`Costs incurred, cumulative = 4,500,000`
`% complete = 4,500,000 / 15,000,000 = 30.0%`
`Revenue, cumulative = 30.0% × 20,000,000 = 6,000,000`
`Revenue, period = 6,000,000 (first year, no prior balance)`
`Cost of revenue, period = 4,500,000`
`Gross margin, period = 6,000,000 − 4,500,000 = 1,500,000 (25.0% of period revenue)`
`Contract asset/(liability) = 6,000,000 − 6,800,000 = (800,000)`

*Working — Year 2.*
`Costs incurred, cumulative = 4,500,000 + 7,100,000 = 11,600,000`
`% complete = 11,600,000 / 16,000,000 = 72.5%`
`Revenue, cumulative = 72.5% × 20,000,000 = 14,500,000`
`Revenue, period = 14,500,000 − 6,000,000 = 8,500,000`
`Cost of revenue, period = 7,100,000`
`Gross margin, period = 8,500,000 − 7,100,000 = 1,400,000 (16.5% of period revenue)`
`Contract asset/(liability) = 14,500,000 − 14,300,000 = 200,000`
`Check: cumulative margin to date = 14,500,000 − 11,600,000 = 2,900,000 = 72.5% × (20,000,000 − 16,000,000) =
72.5% × 4,000,000 = 2,900,000 ✓`

*Working — Year 3 (completion).*
`Costs incurred, cumulative = 11,600,000 + 4,600,000 = 16,200,000 (final, actual)`
`% complete = 100.0% (contract complete)`
`Revenue, cumulative = 100.0% × 20,000,000 = 20,000,000`
`Revenue, period = 20,000,000 − 14,500,000 = 5,500,000`
`Cost of revenue, period = 4,600,000`
`Gross margin, period = 5,500,000 − 4,600,000 = 900,000 (16.4% of period revenue)`
`Contract asset/(liability) = 20,000,000 − 20,000,000 = 0`

*Answer — the full schedule.*

| Year | Revenue, period ($) | Cost of revenue, period ($) | Gross margin, period ($) | Margin % of period revenue | Revenue, cumulative ($) | Cost, cumulative ($) | % complete, cumulative | Contract asset/(liability), cumulative ($) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6,000,000 | 4,500,000 | 1,500,000 | 25.0% | 6,000,000 | 4,500,000 | 30.0% | (800,000) |
| 2 | 8,500,000 | 7,100,000 | 1,400,000 | 16.5% | 14,500,000 | 11,600,000 | 72.5% | 200,000 |
| 3 | 5,500,000 | 4,600,000 | 900,000 | 16.4% | 20,000,000 | 16,200,000 | 100.0% | 0 |
| **Total** | **20,000,000** | **16,200,000** | **3,800,000** | **19.0%** | | | | |

*Reconciliation check.* `Total period revenue = 6,000,000 + 8,500,000 + 5,500,000 = 20,000,000 = contract
price ✓`. `Total period cost = 4,500,000 + 7,100,000 + 4,600,000 = 16,200,000 = final cumulative cost ✓`.
`Total period margin = 1,500,000 + 1,400,000 + 900,000 = 3,800,000 = contract price − final cost = 20,000,000
− 16,200,000 = 3,800,000 ✓`.

*Interpretation.* The contract's margin story is one of gradual erosion, not sudden failure: originally
expected at $5,000,000 (25.0%) at inception, re-estimated down to $4,000,000 (20.0%) once the Year 2 cost
pressure was known, and finally settling at an actual $3,800,000 (19.0%) once the contract completed — a
further, smaller overrun in Year 3 beyond even the revised Year 2 estimate. This progressive narrowing is
exactly what a project controls professional should expect to see reflected in the revenue schedule as cost
estimates mature, and it is exactly why the total-estimated-cost denominator must be re-forecast rigorously
each period (cross-reference KA 6.3's EAC discipline) rather than held static. Margin never turned negative in
this case, so no onerous-contract loss provision was triggered; had total estimated cost at any point exceeded
the contract price, the resulting expected loss would need to be recognised in full immediately under IAS 37's
principles for onerous contracts (cross-reference KA 2.4) — a distinct, separate assessment from the revenue
schedule itself.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Re-forecasting total estimated cost only annually rather than as new information

both inefficient and a control weakness.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted cost-to-complete forecasting tools — the same category discussed in KA 6.3
and KA 13.5 — can flag trend-based early warning of a total-estimated-cost overrun before it is formally
re-forecast, directly relevant to keeping this schedule's denominator current. Given that a revised cost
estimate flows straight through to recognised revenue and margin — a direct profit-and-loss impact — any
AI-generated or AI-assisted revision to total estimated cost must be reviewed and formally approved by the
professional (commercial/project controls lead and finance) before it is used to restate the % complete
calculation. This is one of the clearest illustrations in the whole Body of Knowledge of "AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable": an unreviewed automated cost re-forecast changing recognised revenue without sign-off
would be a significant control failure.

### 2.2.13 Disclosures under IFRS 15

**Definition and purpose.** IFRS 15 requires disclosures sufficient to enable users of financial statements to
understand the nature, amount, timing and uncertainty of revenue and cash flows from contracts with
customers. For a project controls professional, the most operationally relevant disclosure areas are: the
**disaggregation of revenue** (typically by geography, contract type, or timing of transfer — over time versus
point in time); a **reconciliation of contract balances** (opening and closing contract assets, contract
liabilities and receivables, and what caused the movement); the **transaction price allocated to remaining
performance obligations** (in effect, a revenue "backlog" figure, often of direct interest to project
controls and commercial teams); and a description of the **significant judgements** made — particularly the
methods used to determine the transaction price, measure progress, and constrain variable consideration
(section 2.2.7, section 2.2.8).

**The underlying principle.** Disclosures exist because the five-step model, however rigorously applied,
still embeds real judgement (SSP estimation, the constraint, cost-to-complete forecasts); disclosure is the
mechanism by which those judgements — and their potential effect on reported figures — are made transparent to
users rather than buried inside a single revenue number.

**Example 2.2.13 — a remaining-performance-obligation disclosure extract.**

*Scenario/Required.* State the transaction price allocated to Silverline's unsatisfied performance obligation
on the Route 9 Flyover Bridge contract at the end of Year 1, and when it is expected to be recognised.

*Working.*
`Remaining transaction price = Contract price − Cumulative revenue recognised to date = 20,000,000 − 6,000,000
= 14,000,000`

*Answer.* **$14,000,000** remains to be recognised as revenue, expected over the remaining construction
period (Years 2–3), consistent with the schedule in section 2.2.12.

*Interpretation.* This single figure is exactly the kind of number a project controls or commercial team
tracks operationally as "revenue backlog" or "unrecognised contract value" — the disclosure requirement and
the internal project-controls metric are, in substance, the same underlying number viewed from two audiences.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating disclosure as a purely financial-reporting exercise disconnected from the
operational data (cost-to-complete, billing schedule) that project controls already maintains — in a well-
run function, the disclosure numbers should tie directly back to the same schedules developed in section 2.2.11–
section 2.2.12. (2) Under-disclosing the judgements behind SSP estimates, the constraint, and the input-method
cost-to-complete estimate, leaving users unable to assess estimation uncertainty. (3) Omitting a contract-
balance reconciliation that explains *why* the contract asset/liability position moved, not merely what it
moved to.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI drafting assistants can produce a first-draft disclosure note — disaggregation
tables, contract-balance reconciliations, remaining-performance-obligation figures — directly from the
underlying revenue, cost and billing data, saving significant drafting time in a routine reporting cycle
(cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.5, KA 2.5). Given that disclosures carry direct regulatory and audit
significance, every AI-drafted disclosure must be checked line-by-line against the underlying schedules by a
qualified accountant, and formally signed off, before issuance — AI drafts the note; the professional and, ultimately,
the auditor, are accountable for what is published.

**Cross-references.** section 2.2.11–section 2.2.12 (the schedules disclosures are drawn from); KA 2.5 (management versus
statutory reporting, and AI-assisted disclosure drafting more broadly); KA 4.3 (management reporting cadence).

### Key terms

- **Five-step model** — identify the contract; identify performance obligations; determine the transaction
  price; allocate the transaction price; recognise revenue as/when obligations are satisfied.
- **Performance obligation** — a promise to transfer a distinct good or service (or bundle) to a customer.
- **Distinct** — capable of being distinct AND distinct within the context of the contract.
- **Transaction price** — the consideration an entity expects to be entitled to, adjusted for variable
  consideration, financing, non-cash consideration and consideration payable to the customer.
- **Standalone selling price (SSP)** — the price at which a good or service would be sold separately;
  estimated via the adjusted market assessment, expected cost-plus-margin, or (as a last resort) residual
  approach.
- **Over time vs point in time** — the two patterns of revenue recognition, determined by the three over-time
  criteria (simultaneous receipt/consumption; creating/enhancing a customer-controlled asset; no alternative
  use plus enforceable right to payment) or, failing all three, point-in-time control-transfer indicators.
- **Input method / cost-to-cost** — `% complete = costs incurred to date / total estimated costs`.
- **Output method** — progress measured directly (units, milestones, survey, or straight-line for an evenly
  delivered service).
- **Variable consideration** — consideration that depends on future events (bonuses, penalties, rebates,
  refunds); estimated by expected value or most likely amount.
- **The constraint** — variable consideration is included only to the extent highly probable it will not
  result in a significant future revenue reversal.
- **Significant financing component** — an adjustment for a meaningful gap between payment and performance
  timing, recognised as interest income/expense, separate from revenue; not required where the gap is ≤ 12
  months.
- **Contract modification** — a change in scope/price, classified as a separate contract, prospective
  treatment, or a cumulative catch-up adjustment.
- **Contract asset** — cumulative revenue recognised exceeds cumulative amounts billed; conditional on
  further performance.
- **Contract liability** — cumulative amounts billed exceed cumulative revenue recognised; deferred revenue,
  in effect.
- **Receivable** — an unconditional right to consideration, once billed, regardless of remaining performance.
- **Cumulative catch-up adjustment** — an immediate revenue adjustment recognising a modification as if it
  had always been part of the original, not-yet-fully-satisfied performance obligation.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 2.2.1 [topic 2.2.1, level: recall]**
Which of the following correctly states the order of IFRS 15's five-step model?
A. Determine the transaction price; identify the contract; identify performance obligations; allocate;
   recognise revenue
B. **Identify the contract; identify performance obligations; determine the transaction price; allocate the
   transaction price; recognise revenue** ✔
C. Identify performance obligations; identify the contract; allocate the transaction price; determine the
   transaction price; recognise revenue
D. Identify the contract; allocate the transaction price; identify performance obligations; determine the
   transaction price; recognise revenue

*Rationale:* B is the model's defined sequence (section 2.2.1); the transaction price cannot be allocated (Step 4)
before it is determined (Step 3), and obligations must be identified (Step 2) before either. A, C and D each
reorder steps in a way that would require re-work if performed out of sequence.

**MCQ 2.2.2 [topic 2.2.3, level: application]**
A contractor supplies specialised equipment that requires extensive, site-specific custom re-engineering
before it can function, such that the equipment and the re-engineering work are highly interdependent. How
should this be treated under Step 2?
A. Always as two separate performance obligations, because "equipment" and "installation" are always distinct
B. **As a single, combined performance obligation**, because the two items are not distinct within the
   context of the contract ✔
C. As a single performance obligation only if the customer requests it
D. As three performance obligations, to match the three-part price breakdown on the invoice

*Rationale:* Where installation significantly modifies or is highly interdependent with the equipment, the
"distinct within the context of the contract" test fails and the two must be combined (Example 2.2.3b). A
wrongly assumes distinctness is automatic; C misapplies the test as a customer choice; D confuses invoice
structure with the distinctness test.

**MCQ 2.2.3 [topic 2.2.5, level: application — numerical]**
A contract has a transaction price of $540,000. The standalone selling prices of its three performance
obligations are $400,000, $150,000 and $50,000. What amount is allocated to the $150,000 (maintenance)
obligation?
A. $150,000
B. **$135,000** ✔
C. $120,000
D. $108,000

*Rationale:* `Allocation ratio = 540,000 / (400,000+150,000+50,000) = 0.90`; `150,000 × 0.90 = 135,000`
(Example 2.2.5). A wrongly uses the unadjusted SSP; C and D apply incorrect ratios.

**MCQ 2.2.4 [topic 2.2.6, level: application]**
A contractor builds a bespoke asset on the customer's own site under a contract that entitles the contractor
to payment for work completed to date even if the customer terminates for convenience, and the asset has no
realistic alternative use to the contractor. How should revenue be recognised?
A. At a point in time, on physical handover
B. At a point in time, when legal title transfers
C. **Over time**, because the "no alternative use plus enforceable right to payment for performance to date"
   criterion is met ✔
D. Over time, but only once the customer has accepted the completed asset

*Rationale:* This is precisely criterion (c) for over-time recognition (section 2.2.6) — no alternative use combined
with an enforceable right to payment for performance to date. A and B describe point-in-time indicators that
do not apply once an over-time criterion is met; D incorrectly ties over-time recognition to final acceptance,
which is a point-in-time concept.

**MCQ 2.2.5 [topic 2.2.7, level: application — numerical]**
A performance obligation satisfied over time has a $10,000,000 transaction price. Total estimated costs are
$8,000,000, and costs incurred to date are $2,400,000. Using the input (cost-to-cost) method, what revenue
should be recognised to date?
A. $2,400,000
B. **$3,000,000** ✔
C. $2,666,667
D. $8,000,000

*Rationale:* `% complete = 2,400,000 / 8,000,000 = 30.0%`; `Revenue = 30.0% × 10,000,000 = 3,000,000`. A
mistakes cost incurred for revenue; C and D misapply the ratio.

**MCQ 2.2.6 [topic 2.2.8, level: analysis — numerical]**
A contract includes a $200,000 performance bonus. Management assesses a 70% probability the bonus will be
earned in full and a 30% probability it will not be earned at all. Using the expected value method, what
amount of variable consideration (before applying the constraint) should be estimated?
A. $200,000
B. **$140,000** ✔
C. $60,000
D. $0

*Rationale:* `Expected value = (0.70 × 200,000) + (0.30 × 0) = 140,000`. A ignores the probability weighting;
C incorrectly uses the 30% (non-occurring) probability against the bonus amount; D ignores the bonus
altogether. Note the constraint (section 2.2.8) is then applied separately and could further reduce this estimate.

**MCQ 2.2.7 [topic 2.2.11, level: application]**
At a reporting date, cumulative revenue recognised on a contract is $14,500,000 and cumulative amounts billed
are $14,300,000. What does this represent?
A. A contract liability of $200,000
B. **A contract asset of $200,000** ✔
C. A receivable of $200,000
D. An onerous contract provision of $200,000

*Rationale:* `Contract asset/(liability) = Revenue recognised − Billed = 14,500,000 − 14,300,000 = +200,000`,
a positive result, which is a contract asset (section 2.2.11) — revenue has been earned ahead of billing. A reverses
the sign; C confuses this conditional balance with an unconditional receivable; D is an unrelated concept
(IAS 37, cross-reference KA 2.4).

**MCQ 2.2.8 [topic 2.2.10, level: analysis]**
A customer approves additional, integrated scope on a single, partially-satisfied over-time construction
performance obligation, at a price that does not separately identify a standalone selling price for the
addition because the added work is not distinct from the work already underway. Which modification treatment
applies?
A. Separate contract
B. Prospective treatment (termination of the old contract, creation of a new one)
C. **Cumulative catch-up adjustment** ✔
D. No adjustment is required until the contract completes

*Rationale:* Because the additional scope is not distinct from the performance obligation already partly
satisfied, the modification is treated as if it had always been part of the original contract, with an
immediate cumulative catch-up adjustment to revenue recognised to date (section 2.2.10, Example 2.2.10c). A and B
both require the added scope to be distinct, which it is not here; D would misstate revenue throughout the
remaining contract period.

### Self-check questions

1. Explain why TechBuild's SCADA equipment and Silverline's Route 9 Flyover Bridge — both, in a loose sense,
   "something built and delivered to a customer" — are recognised on opposite patterns (point in time versus
   over time). Which specific criterion drives the difference?
2. Using the Route 9 Flyover Bridge Year 2 data (costs incurred in Year 2 of $7,100,000; total estimated cost
   revised to $16,000,000; contract price $20,000,000; Year 1 cumulative revenue already recognised of
   $6,000,000), recompute cumulative % complete, cumulative revenue, and Year 2 revenue and gross margin.
3. A contract asset of $200,000 and an accounts receivable of $500,000 appear on the same balance sheet for
   the same contract at the same date. Explain, in your own words, how both can exist simultaneously without
   double-counting.
4. Explain why the expected value method would have been the wrong choice for the NCR scenario in Example
   2.2.8b, and what the most likely amount method gave instead.

**Answers**

1. Silverline's bridge meets over-time criterion (c): it is a bespoke asset with no alternative use to
   Silverline, and Silverline has an enforceable right to payment for work completed to date even if the
   customer terminates for convenience. TechBuild's equipment meets none of the three over-time criteria —
   before installation, it could realistically be redirected to another customer at modest cost, and no
   enforceable right to progress payment exists — so it is recognised at a point in time, on commissioning
   (section 2.2.6, Example 2.2.6).
2. `Cumulative costs = 4,500,000 + 7,100,000 = 11,600,000`. `% complete = 11,600,000 / 16,000,000 = 72.5%`.
   `Cumulative revenue = 72.5% × 20,000,000 = 14,500,000`. `Year 2 revenue = 14,500,000 − 6,000,000 =
   8,500,000`. `Year 2 gross margin = 8,500,000 − 7,100,000 = 1,400,000` (section 2.2.12, Example 2.2.12).
3. The contract asset ($200,000) measures the excess of cumulative revenue recognised over cumulative amounts
   billed — a conditional right, dependent on further performance. The accounts receivable ($500,000)
   measures amounts already billed but not yet collected — an unconditional right, dependent only on the
   passage of time. They answer two different questions (how much has been earned but not billed, versus how
   much has been billed but not collected) and can both be non-zero for the same contract at the same time
   (Example 2.2.11b).
4. The NCR scenario has only two realistic outcomes ($0 deduction, 80% probability, or a $200,000 deduction,
   20% probability) rather than a range of many possible outcomes; an expected-value calculation
   (`0.8×0 + 0.2×(−200,000) = −40,000`) would produce a figure that can never actually occur, since the
   contract will resolve to exactly one of the two outcomes. The most likely amount method instead selects
   the single most probable outcome — $0 — which is then checked against the constraint (the potential
   $200,000 reversal, weighted at $40,000, is 0.5% of the transaction price and judged not significant), so
   no adjustment is needed (Example 2.2.8b).
