# Domain 2 — Financial Reporting & the Standards

## KA 2.5 — Management reporting vs statutory reporting

Every project generates one underlying stream of transactions — costs incurred, revenue earned, claims
raised, commitments placed — but that stream is reported twice, to two different audiences, under two
different sets of rules. **Statutory reporting** produces the general-purpose financial statements filed
with regulators and given to shareholders, lenders and tax authorities: audited, standardised under IFRS
Accounting Standards (or local GAAP), and bound by recognition thresholds — such as the variable-
consideration constraint in IFRS 15 (KA 2.2) or the onerous-contract test in IAS 37 (KA 1.4, KA 2.4) —
that deliberately exclude figures that are not yet sufficiently certain. **Management reporting** produces
the internal project cost reports, forecasts and dashboards that the project team and the board actually
use to run the business day to day: unaudited, unstandardised in format, and free to include forward-
looking, risk-adjusted judgement that statutory rules would not yet permit. Both views are legitimate and
both are necessary — the professional's job is to know which is which, and to be able to reconcile one to
the other on demand. This KA builds that reconciliation skill directly; it does not repeat the report-
design, dashboard and cadence material that belongs to KA 4.3, nor the deep IFRS 15/IAS 37 mechanics that
belong to KA 2.2/2.4 — it cross-references both. AI is now a real part of this bridge: genAI tools draft
statutory disclosure notes from underlying ledger and management data, run automated consistency checks
between the internal and external figures, and map an entity's reporting lines against a standard's
disclosure checklist — always as a first draft for a qualified preparer or reviewer to check and sign off,
never as the filed answer.

### 2.5.1 Purpose and audiences: why reporting splits into statutory and management views

**Definition and purpose.** **Statutory (external) financial reporting** is *general-purpose* reporting: a
single set of financial statements — Statement of Financial Position, Statement of Profit or Loss and OCI,
Statement of Cash Flows, Statement of Changes in Equity and notes (KA 1.2) — prepared to meet the common
information needs of users who cannot demand information tailored to themselves (shareholders, lenders,
regulators, tax authorities, credit-rating agencies). It is governed by IFRS Accounting Standards (or the
applicable local GAAP), is normally subject to independent audit, and is issued at fixed, comparatively
infrequent intervals (typically annually, with a condensed interim statement where required — §2.5.2).
**Management reporting** (management accounts) is *internal* reporting: produced for the people who run
the project or the entity — the project manager, the commercial and finance teams, the board — to support
day-to-day decisions. It is not governed by IFRS, is not normally audited, and its format, frequency and
content are chosen entirely by the entity to fit the decision being made.

**The underlying principle.** Both views are built from the same ledger (KA 1.1, KA 5.2), but they trade
off the Conceptual Framework's qualitative characteristics differently. Statutory reporting weights
**faithful representation and verifiability** heavily — a figure is not recognised until the recognition
criteria of the relevant standard are met, even if management is privately confident it will materialise.
Management reporting weights **relevance and timeliness** more heavily — a risk-adjusted forecast that is
"probably right and available now" is more useful for steering a project than a verified figure that will
only be confirmed in three months. Neither trade-off is wrong; each serves its audience's actual decision.

**Example 2.5.1 — the same commercial fact, reported two ways.**

*Scenario.* Solstice Infrastructure Group plc is delivering the **Coastal Interchange** contract (fixed
price, $2,500,000 / SAR 9,375,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1). A four-week client-caused delay has
occurred; the commercial team has raised a $600,000 delay/disruption claim against the client. Based on the
strength of the supporting records and past settlement experience on similar claims, the commercial team
assesses the claim as **55% likely** to be settled at or near the full amount.

*Required.* State how the claim is treated in (a) the internal management report and (b) the statutory
financial statements.

*Working.*
- Management report (internal, risk-adjusted): `Included value = 55% × $600,000 = $330,000`, shown as an
  anticipated recovery within the project's forecast outturn, clearly labelled as unrecognised and
  unresolved.
- Statutory financial statements: the claim is not yet agreed with the client and is far short of the
  "highly probable" threshold that IFRS 15's variable-consideration constraint applies before a contested
  amount can be included in the transaction price (KA 2.2). `Included value = $0`.

*Answer.* **Management report: $330,000 (memo/forecast item). Statutory accounts: $0 (not recognised).**

*Interpretation.* Neither figure is "wrong." $330,000 is a legitimate, disclosed, risk-adjusted planning
number for the board's cash and margin forecast; $0 is the legitimate, faithfully-represented statutory
position until the claim is resolved or becomes highly probable. A professional who reports $330,000 as
statutory revenue, or who tells the board the claim is "worth nothing" because it isn't yet on the balance
sheet, has made two different but equally serious errors.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating the management figure as "the real number" and the statutory figure as
merely conservative — both are correct answers to different questions, not two attempts at the same
answer. (2) Presenting an unrecognised, risk-adjusted management figure to an external party (a lender, for
example) without clearly labelling it as internal and unaudited — this can mislead and, in some
jurisdictions, carries regulatory or legal consequences. (3) Assuming statutory figures are always more
"accurate" — they are more *verifiable*, not necessarily more predictive of the eventual outcome.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI drafting tools can summarise the same underlying project data into either a
management-style narrative or a statutory-style note, which is useful for preparers moving between the two
audiences quickly. The tool has no inherent understanding of *which* recognition threshold applies to which
output — it will happily draft a confident-sounding statutory note including an unrecognised claim if
prompted carelessly. The professional must specify which reporting basis is required and verify the output
against that basis before use; a mislabelled AI draft is a governance failure, not a technology one.

### 2.5.2 The statutory reporting framework: general-purpose statements, materiality and assurance

**Definition and purpose.** Statutory reporting is built on **IAS 1 Presentation of Financial Statements**,
which sets the minimum structure and content of a complete set of general-purpose financial statements
(KA 1.2), and — for entities required to report more frequently than annually, typically listed entities —
**IAS 34 Interim Financial Reporting**, which governs a condensed interim statement (commonly half-yearly
or quarterly). External assurance is provided by an independent audit conducted under International
Standards on Auditing (ISA), resulting in an audit opinion on whether the statements give a true and fair
view (or are fairly presented) in accordance with the applicable framework. Many jurisdictions also expect
a narrative accompaniment — a strategic report or management commentary — for which the IASB's own
**IFRS Practice Statement 1 Management Commentary** provides non-mandatory guidance on linking the numbers
to the narrative story of the business.

**The underlying principle.** Because a general-purpose statement cannot report every fact with equal
prominence, auditors and preparers apply **materiality**: information is material if omitting, misstating
or obscuring it could reasonably be expected to influence the decisions of the primary users. ISA 320
*Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit* requires materiality to be determined by professional
judgement, commonly anchored to a benchmark such as profit before tax, revenue or total assets — the
specific percentage is a matter of practice and judgement, not a fixed rule in any standard, and varies with
the entity's circumstances.

**Formula.** A commonly used, illustrative benchmarking approach (practice convention, not a standard-
mandated percentage):

`Overall materiality ≈ 5% of profit before tax (PBT)` (or an alternative benchmark where PBT is volatile or
close to break-even)
`Performance materiality ≈ 75% of overall materiality` (a buffer set below overall materiality to reduce the
risk that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements exceeds it)
`Clearly trivial threshold ≈ 5% of overall materiality` (individual items below this are not separately
accumulated)

**Example 2.5.2 — setting materiality for Solstice Infrastructure Group plc.**

*Scenario.* Solstice Infrastructure Group plc reports group profit before tax of **$2,400,000** for the
year. The group's auditor applies the illustrative benchmarks above.

*Required.* Overall materiality, performance materiality and the clearly trivial threshold.

*Working.*
`Overall materiality = 5% × 2,400,000 = 120,000`
`Performance materiality = 75% × 120,000 = 90,000`
`Clearly trivial threshold = 5% × 120,000 = 6,000`

*Answer.* **Overall materiality $120,000; performance materiality $90,000; clearly trivial threshold
$6,000.**

*Interpretation.* Any single misstatement approaching $120,000 (for example, an incorrectly recognised
element of the Coastal Interchange claim in §2.5.1) is a candidate for audit adjustment or qualification;
the auditor tests and accumulates potential misstatements down to $90,000 individually to guard against
several smaller errors adding up to something material in aggregate; items below $6,000 are not separately
tracked. Management accounts have no equivalent formal threshold — the project team may reasonably want to
know about a $10,000 cost overrun that would never reach statutory materiality.

**Mini-case — materiality is not the same at every level.** A $95,000 costing error on the Coastal
Interchange project is *material to that project's own management report* (it is roughly 4.75% of the
project's $2,000,000 budget at completion, §2.5.3) but *immaterial to Solstice's group statutory accounts*
(it is below the group's $120,000 overall materiality). A project controls professional escalating an issue
must be explicit about which level — project, segment or group — the materiality assessment relates to;
"immaterial" at group level does not mean the project team should ignore it.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Applying group statutory materiality to decide whether a project-level issue is
worth investigating — the two levels serve different purposes and different thresholds are appropriate.
(2) Treating the 5%/75%/5% figures used above as fixed rules from a standard — they are illustrative,
commonly used practice conventions; the actual percentages are a matter of auditor judgement under ISA 320
and vary by engagement. (3) Ignoring qualitative materiality — a small misstatement that masks a covenant
breach, related-party transaction, or fraud indicator can be material regardless of its size.

**AI in this topic.** Analytics tools can scan a full population of postings and flag every item near or
above a materiality or performance-materiality threshold far faster than manual sampling, supporting audit
and management review alike. Setting the materiality benchmark itself, and judging qualitative materiality
(the covenant-breach or related-party scenario above), remain professional judgements an AI tool can
inform with data but not make — the auditor or preparer signs off the threshold and its application.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.2 (the statements materiality is applied to); KA 2.1 (the reporting framework and
qualitative characteristics generally); §2.5.3 (project-level thresholds in the management cost report);
KA 11.3 (internal control and the audit trail).

### 2.5.3 Management accounts and the project cost report

**Definition and purpose.** **Management accounts** are the internal financial reports — most often, for a
project controls professional, the **project cost report** — that track budget, actual cost, forecast and
margin at whatever frequency and granularity decisions require. Unlike statutory accounts, there is no
mandated line-item structure: the report is built around the project's own cost breakdown structure and
control accounts (KA 1.5, KA 5.3) and typically brings together the budget baseline (KA 3.1, KA 3.3), the
cost-to-date from the ledger (KA 5.2) and a forward-looking forecast of cost at completion, whether derived
formally (the EAC family, KA 6.3) or, as here, from bottom-up engineering/commercial judgement.

**The underlying principle.** A forecast final cost is an **accounting estimate** in the sense used by
IAS 8 *Accounting Policies, Changes in Accounting Estimates and Errors*: a best-judgement figure that will
be revised as new information arrives, recognised prospectively rather than treated as a correction of a
prior error. Management reporting exists precisely to surface these revised estimates to decision-makers
promptly — well before they would be reflected, filtered through materiality and recognition rules, in the
next statutory statement.

**Example 2.5.3 — the Coastal Interchange monthly management cost report.**

*Scenario.* Six months into the Coastal Interchange contract (fixed price $2,500,000; original budget at
completion, BAC, $2,000,000 — an originally budgeted margin of $500,000). A known productivity issue in the
earthworks package leads the project team to revise its forecast final cost upward.

| Item | Amount ($) |
|---|---|
| Contract price (revenue) | 2,500,000 |
| Original budget at completion (BAC) | 2,000,000 |
| Actual cost to date (AC) | 1,100,000 |
| Revised forecast final cost | 2,200,000 |

*Required.* (a) The cost variance at completion; (b) actual cost to date as a % of BAC; (c) forecast margin
at completion and the margin erosion versus the original budget.

*Working.*
(a) `Cost variance at completion = BAC − Forecast final cost = 2,000,000 − 2,200,000 = (200,000)`, i.e. a
$200,000 forecast overrun.
(b) `AC / BAC = 1,100,000 / 2,000,000 = 55.0%` of budget consumed.
(c) `Forecast margin = Revenue − Forecast final cost = 2,500,000 − 2,200,000 = 300,000`
`Forecast margin % = 300,000 / 2,500,000 = 12.0%`
`Margin erosion = Original margin − Forecast margin = 500,000 − 300,000 = 200,000` (equivalently,
`20.0% − 12.0% = 8.0` percentage points)

*Answer.* **Forecast overrun $200,000; 55.0% of budget spent; forecast margin $300,000 (12.0%), an erosion
of $200,000 (8.0 percentage points) from the original 20.0% budgeted margin.**

*Interpretation.* None of these figures appears anywhere in Solstice's statutory accounts in this form —
they are exactly what the project manager and commercial director need to see monthly, and exactly what
would trigger a management action (a recovery plan, a claim against the delay-causing party, or an early
warning to the board) long before the year-end statutory close.

**Mini-case — reconciling the cost report to the ledger.** At the same data date, the finance function
confirms the $1,100,000 actual cost to date reconciles to the general ledger control account for Coastal
Interchange (cross-reference KA 1.5, KA 5.2): labour $480,000 + materials $390,000 + equipment $140,000 +
subcontract $90,000 = $1,100,000. This reconciliation step — the management report's cost-to-date figure
must always tie to the ledger, even though the *forecast* figure is a judgement the ledger cannot itself
produce — is a routine but essential control; an unreconciled cost report is not a reliable basis for
either the board or, later, the statutory revenue calculation in §2.5.4.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Confusing the *budget* (BAC, fixed once baselined) with the *forecast* (revised
continually) — a management report that quietly relabels a revised forecast as the new "budget" loses the
ability to measure variance against the original plan. (2) Reporting a forecast final cost that has not
been reconciled to the ledger's actual-cost-to-date component, so the report's own arithmetic cannot be
checked. (3) Omitting the *reason* for a forecast change (here, a named productivity issue) — a number
without a driver gives management nothing to act on.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted cost-extraction and reconciliation tools can automatically tie a project's
cost-to-date to the ledger and flag postings that do not reconcile (cross-reference KA 5.2, KA 13.5),
materially reducing the manual effort in producing a monthly cost report. Forecasting the *final* cost from
a productivity issue, weather delay or design change remains a professional engineering/commercial
judgement; an AI trend-line extrapolation from past cost run-rate can be offered as one input but should
never silently replace the team's bottom-up, driver-based forecast without review.

### 2.5.4 Reconciling management and statutory reporting: building the bridge

**Definition and purpose.** The **management-to-statutory bridge** is the explicit reconciliation between
what the internal report shows and what the statutory accounts recognise for the same project, at the same
date. Building it is a project-controls skill in its own right: it requires knowing which management
figures already comply with a statutory recognition basis (and so need no adjustment) and which do not (and
so must be stripped out, or separately disclosed as unrecognised) before the statutory numbers can be
produced or explained to the board.

**The underlying principle.** Under IFRS 15's input method (percentage-of-completion, KA 2.2), statutory
revenue recognised to date is driven by the **transaction price** — which itself excludes any variable or
contingent consideration that does not meet the "highly probable no significant reversal" recognition
threshold — multiplied by the percentage complete. Management's own "expected total outturn revenue" figure
is not so constrained: it may legitimately include risk-adjusted amounts (like the delay claim in §2.5.1,
or a variation order not yet agreed) that the statutory transaction price must exclude. The bridge is simply
the arithmetic difference, made explicit and disclosed rather than left implicit.

**Formula.**
`Statutory revenue recognised to date = % complete (input method) × Transaction price (IFRS 15-compliant)`
`Management expected total outturn revenue = Base contract price + Σ (risk-adjusted anticipated variations/claims)`
`Bridge (reconciling item) = Management expected total outturn revenue − Statutory transaction price`

**Example 2.5.4 — the Coastal Interchange revenue bridge.**

*Scenario.* Continuing §2.5.3 (contract price $2,500,000; % complete = AC / forecast final cost =
1,100,000 / 2,200,000 = 50.0%). Separately, the client has verbally indicated openness to a **$400,000**
variation order for additional retaining-wall works; the commercial team assesses this as **65% likely** to
be formally agreed, but no written instruction or price agreement exists yet.

*Required.* (a) Statutory revenue recognised to date; (b) management's expected total outturn revenue;
(c) the reconciling bridge amount.

*Working.*
(a) Statutory transaction price excludes the variation (not agreed, well short of "highly probable"):
`Statutory revenue to date = 50.0% × 2,500,000 = 1,250,000`
Statutory cost of sales to date (input method: cost incurred) = `1,100,000`
Statutory gross profit to date = `1,250,000 − 1,100,000 = 150,000` (`150,000 / 1,250,000 = 12.0%` margin —
consistent with the 12.0% forecast margin at completion computed in §2.5.3, as expected under a consistent
cost-to-cost method).

(b) Risk-adjusted variation value = `65% × 400,000 = 260,000`
Management expected total outturn revenue = `2,500,000 + 260,000 = 2,760,000`

(c) Bridge (reconciling item) = `2,760,000 − 2,500,000 = 260,000`

*Answer.* **Statutory revenue to date $1,250,000 (gross profit $150,000, 12.0% margin). Management expected
total outturn revenue $2,760,000. Reconciling bridge: $260,000 of anticipated, risk-adjusted revenue not
yet recognised statutorily.**

*Interpretation.* Because the variation's own scope has not started, none of the $260,000 flows into
revenue recognised *to date* under either view — the bridge is entirely a forward-looking, "total contract
value" reconciling item. This is exactly the kind of figure a board needs explained *before* year-end,
so that a forecast conversation about a $2,760,000 outturn is never mistaken for, or quoted alongside, the
$1,250,000 that will actually appear as statutory revenue-to-date.

**Mini-case — when the bridge crosses into an onerous contract.** Suppose instead the earthworks
productivity issue had worsened further, taking the forecast final cost (base scope only) to $2,600,000.
`Forecast result = 2,500,000 − 2,600,000 = (100,000)`, a loss-making contract. Under IAS 37 (KA 1.4, KA 2.4)
this triggers an **onerous contract provision** for the *entire* expected loss, $100,000, recognised
immediately in full at the reporting date — not spread across the remaining percentage of completion the
way the management report would typically phase a "trending overrun." This is one of the sharpest
differences between the two views: management reporting tends to show a deteriorating trend line; statutory
reporting, once the threshold is crossed, must recognise the whole forecast loss at once.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Presenting the management "expected total outturn" figure to a lender or investor
without the bridge, implying it is the statutory position. (2) Failing to re-test the onerous-contract
threshold each period once a forecast is trending toward loss — a bridge that only ever tracks revenue, and
never checks whether the forecast result has turned negative, will miss a required IAS 37 provision. (3)
Double-counting: applying the percentage complete to the *inflated* management revenue figure rather than
to the constrained statutory transaction price, which would overstate statutory revenue.

**AI in this topic.** A standard-mapping or consistency-check tool can automatically compare a project's
management-reported "expected outturn" figure against the transaction price used in the statutory revenue
calculation and flag any gap for review — precisely the bridge computed above — turning a manual, easily
skipped reconciliation into a routine, systematic one (cross-reference KA 13.5). It cannot itself judge
whether a specific claim or variation has crossed the "highly probable" recognition threshold; that remains
a commercial and accounting judgement for the professional, informed by the contract, correspondence and
past settlement history, not inferred by a model from the size of the number alone.

### 2.5.5 AI in management and statutory reporting: drafting, consistency checks and standard-mapping (with human sign-off)

**Definition and purpose.** Three AI-assisted tool categories are now genuinely in use across the
management/statutory boundary, and this is the KA where the domain's dedicated AI focus sits (cross-
reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5 for the full tool-category and lifecycle treatment). **Disclosure-drafting
assistants** use generative AI to produce a first draft of a statutory note (e.g. the IFRS 15 disaggregation
of revenue note, or the IFRS 8 segment note below) from underlying ledger and management data. **Consistency-
check tools** automatically compare figures that are *supposed* to agree across the internal and external
views — a segment revenue figure, a cost-to-date figure, a contract-asset balance — and flag any gap.
**Standard-mapping assistants** compare an entity's chart of accounts or management report structure against
a disclosure checklist derived from the applicable standards and flag missing or incomplete required
disclosures.

**The underlying principle.** **IFRS 8 Operating Segments** requires an entity to report segment
information on the same basis as the information regularly reviewed by its **chief operating decision
maker (CODM)** — the "management approach." This is the one place in the whole statutory framework where
the *internal* management reporting basis is not just a reference point but the *mandated* basis for an
*external* disclosure, which is exactly why a consistency check between the two figures is so directly
useful here: any drafted segment note that does not match the internally reported figure is, by definition
under IFRS 8, drafted on the wrong basis.

**Example 2.5.5 — an AI consistency check catches a segment-note error.**

*Scenario.* Solstice Infrastructure Group plc's Southern Region segment (which includes Coastal
Interchange, among other projects) is internally reported to the CODM at **$18,400,000** of revenue for the
period. A GenAI drafting tool is asked to prepare the statutory IFRS 8 segment note from a general-ledger
extract and produces a figure of **$17,950,000** — it applied the *consolidated, intersegment-eliminated*
GL view rather than the CODM's management-approach view, silently dropping a $450,000 intercompany
work-order revenue line that the CODM does, in fact, review as part of the segment's internally reported
result.

*Required.* (a) The gap the consistency check should flag; (b) the correct resolution.

*Working.*
(a) `Gap = 18,400,000 − 17,950,000 = 450,000`
(b) Under IFRS 8's management approach, the segment note must use the $18,400,000 figure actually reported
to the CODM (including the intercompany work order), with the $450,000 intersegment amount then separately
identified and eliminated in the note's required reconciliation of total segment revenue to the entity's
consolidated statutory revenue — not omitted from the segment figure itself.

*Answer.* **$450,000 gap flagged; the AI's draft is corrected to $18,400,000 at the segment level, with the
elimination shown in the reconciliation note, not embedded silently in the segment figure.**

*Interpretation.* The tool did two things at once: it usefully drafted most of the note in seconds, and it
made a basis error a reviewer unfamiliar with IFRS 8's management-approach requirement might not have
caught either. The consistency check — comparing the draft to the number the CODM actually saw — is what
surfaced the error; a human reviewer with segment-reporting knowledge is what resolved it correctly. Neither
step is optional.

**Mini-case — standard-mapping for a first-time disclosure.** Solstice adds a new performance obligation
type (a five-year maintenance service bundled with a design-and-build contract) for the first time this
year. A standard-mapping assistant, run against an IFRS 15 disclosure checklist, flags that the entity's
draft notes are missing the required disaggregation of revenue by timing of transfer (over-time vs
point-in-time, KA 2.2) for the new obligation type — a gap a preparer working from last year's note as a
template could easily miss, since last year's contract population did not include this obligation type. The
tool's contribution is coverage — checking against the full checklist mechanically, every year, regardless
of what changed — not judgement about *how* to word the new disclosure, which remains the preparer's task,
reviewed by the auditor.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating a consistency check that finds *no* discrepancy as proof the note is
correct — the tool only checks that two figures match, not that either figure is itself right (an error
present in both the management figure and the AI draft would pass silently). (2) Feeding confidential,
pre-release financial data into an ungoverned public AI tool to draft a note ahead of the market
announcement — a data-governance and market-disclosure risk in its own right (cross-reference KA 13.2,
KA 13.6). (3) Allowing an AI-drafted note to be filed without a named, qualified sign-off — the audit trail
of who approved what, and when, is itself part of the control environment (cross-reference KA 11.3).

**AI in this topic.** This entire topic is the practical, domain-specific expression of "AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable" for Domain 2: drafting assistants save real preparation time on notes that are
substantially mechanical (built from data already reconciled elsewhere), consistency checks close a gap
that manual review often misses precisely because it requires comparing two documents that are rarely
opened side by side, and standard-mapping tools give mechanical, exhaustive checklist coverage that a
time-pressed preparer cannot always give by memory. None of the three removes the need for a qualified
preparer or reviewer who understands *why* a rule like IFRS 8's management approach exists, can judge
whether an item newly flagged is really missing or is a false positive, and takes accountable sign-off
before anything is filed. Tool capability in this space is evolving quickly; specific product claims should
be verified against current vendor documentation rather than assumed from this text.

### Key terms

- **Statutory (external) reporting** — general-purpose financial statements prepared under IFRS/local GAAP
  for shareholders, lenders, regulators and tax authorities; normally audited.
- **Management reporting / management accounts** — internal reports (e.g. the project cost report) prepared
  for decision-makers, with no mandated format, frequency or recognition basis.
- **General-purpose financial statements** — statements meeting the common needs of users who cannot demand
  tailored information (IAS 1).
- **IAS 1 / IAS 34** — presentation of annual financial statements / condensed interim financial reporting.
- **Materiality / performance materiality / clearly trivial threshold** — the auditor's judgement-based
  thresholds (ISA 320) for what could reasonably influence a user's decisions.
- **Variable-consideration constraint** — the IFRS 15 requirement that contingent/variable amounts are
  included in the transaction price only to the extent it is highly probable no significant reversal will
  occur (KA 2.2).
- **Onerous contract provision** — the IAS 37 requirement to recognise the entire expected loss on a
  loss-making contract immediately (KA 1.4, KA 2.4).
- **Management-to-statutory bridge** — the explicit reconciliation between an internally reported figure and
  its statutory-compliant equivalent.
- **IFRS 8 management approach** — the requirement that segment disclosures be reported on the same basis as
  information reviewed by the chief operating decision maker (CODM).
- **Chief operating decision maker (CODM)** — the person/group that allocates resources and assesses
  performance for operating-segment purposes under IFRS 8.
- **Standard-mapping assistant** — an AI tool comparing an entity's reporting structure against a disclosure
  checklist derived from the applicable standard(s).

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 2.5.1 [topic 2.5.1, level: recall]**
Which of the following best distinguishes statutory financial reporting from management reporting?
A. Statutory reporting is always more accurate than management reporting
B. Statutory reporting follows a mandated external framework and recognition criteria; management reporting does not ✔
C. Management reporting is only produced by listed companies
D. Statutory reporting is produced more frequently than management reporting

*Rationale:* B captures the defining distinction (§2.5.1). A confuses verifiability with predictive
accuracy; C is false — management reporting is universal to any entity that manages projects; D reverses the
usual frequency relationship (management reports are typically more frequent).

**MCQ 2.5.2 [topic 2.5.2, level: application — numerical]**
An entity reports profit before tax of $3,000,000. Using the illustrative benchmark of overall materiality
= 5% of PBT and performance materiality = 75% of overall materiality, what is performance materiality?
A. $150,000
B. $112,500 ✔
C. $225,000
D. $3,000,000

*Rationale:* Overall materiality = 5% × 3,000,000 = 150,000; performance materiality = 75% × 150,000 =
112,500 (§2.5.2). A stops at overall materiality; C incorrectly grosses up rather than applying 75%; D uses
the unadjusted PBT figure.

**MCQ 2.5.3 [topic 2.5.4, level: application — numerical]**
A project has a $4,000,000 base contract price and a further $500,000 variation the commercial team assesses
as 70% likely to be agreed, but not yet formally agreed. What is the statutory transaction price for revenue
recognition purposes?
A. $4,350,000
B. $4,500,000
C. $4,000,000 ✔
D. $0

*Rationale:* The unagreed variation does not meet the "highly probable no significant reversal" threshold,
so it is excluded entirely from the statutory transaction price, leaving the base contract price of
$4,000,000 (§2.5.4). A applies the 70% risk-adjustment (a management-report treatment, not a statutory one);
B includes the full variation; D wrongly excludes the base contract too.

**MCQ 2.5.4 [topic 2.5.5, level: analysis]**
Under IFRS 8's management approach, which of the following is the correct basis for a segment revenue
disclosure?
A. The consolidated, fully intersegment-eliminated general ledger balance
B. Whatever figure produces the smallest reconciling difference to total statutory revenue
C. The same basis as the information regularly reported to the chief operating decision maker ✔
D. An average of the internal and external reporting figures

*Rationale:* IFRS 8 mandates the "management approach" — segment information matching what the CODM
actually reviews (§2.5.5), with eliminations shown separately in the required reconciliation, not embedded
in the segment figure. A describes the error the AI draft made in Example 2.5.5; B and D are not IFRS 8
bases at all.

**MCQ 2.5.5 [topic 2.5.5, level: application]**
An AI-assisted consistency check confirms that a draft statutory disclosure exactly matches the internally
reported management figure. What can be correctly concluded?
A. The disclosure is definitely correct and ready to file without further review
B. The two figures are consistent with each other, but neither figure's own correctness has been verified ✔
C. No human sign-off is required because the AI has already validated the figure
D. The management figure must now be restated to match the statutory figure

*Rationale:* A consistency check only confirms agreement between two figures, not that either is correct in
its own right (§2.5.5, Common pitfalls) — an error present in both would pass undetected. A and C overstate
what the check proves; D reverses the correct direction of any needed correction.

### Self-check questions

1. Using the Coastal Interchange delay claim (§2.5.1), explain why the management report and the statutory
   accounts can legitimately show different figures for the same claim on the same date, without either
   being wrong.
2. A group reports profit before tax of $1,800,000. Compute overall materiality (5% of PBT), performance
   materiality (75% of overall materiality) and the clearly trivial threshold (5% of overall materiality).
3. Using §2.5.4's bridge logic, explain what would change in the Coastal Interchange revenue bridge if the
   $400,000 variation order became formally agreed and enforceable during the period, rather than remaining
   at 65% likely.
4. Why does IFRS 8's "management approach" make the segment note a special case among statutory disclosures,
   and why does that make an AI consistency check particularly useful there?

**Answers**

1. The management report includes a risk-adjusted estimate (55% × $600,000 = $330,000) because its purpose
   is to give decision-makers the most useful, timely view of the likely outcome. The statutory accounts
   recognise $0 because the claim has not met IFRS 15's "highly probable no significant reversal" threshold
   for variable consideration — a deliberately higher bar designed for verifiability, not predictive best
   estimate. Both figures faithfully serve their own purpose (§2.5.1).
2. Overall materiality = 5% × 1,800,000 = $90,000. Performance materiality = 75% × 90,000 = $67,500. Clearly
   trivial threshold = 5% × 90,000 = $4,500.
3. Once formally agreed and enforceable, the $400,000 would become (or move toward) a contract modification
   with an agreed price, and the "highly probable" constraint would be reassessed — likely allowing some or
   all of it into the statutory transaction price. The management report's risk-adjusted $260,000 estimate
   would be replaced by the actual agreed value, and the bridge's reconciling item would shrink or disappear
   as the two views converge (§2.5.4).
4. IFRS 8 is unusual because it mandates that an external, statutory disclosure be prepared on the *internal*
   management reporting basis (what the CODM actually reviews), rather than requiring the segment note to be
   independently derived from the eliminated consolidated ledger. This makes a direct, automatable
   comparison between the internal figure and the draft statutory note especially meaningful — any gap is,
   by definition, a basis error, which is exactly what an AI consistency check is well suited to catch
   (§2.5.5).
