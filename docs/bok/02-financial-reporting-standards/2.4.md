# Domain 2 — Financial Reporting & the Standards

## KA 2.4 — Other relevant standards for project controls

IFRS 15 (KA 2.2, KA 2.3) governs *when and how much* revenue a project earns, but revenue is only one line
of the financial statements a project controls professional must be able to read and populate. Five further
IFRS Accounting Standards recur constantly in project cost reports, asset registers and contract files:
**IAS 37** governs the provisions and contingent liabilities that sit behind every warranty reserve,
onerous-contract charge and dispute disclosure; **IAS 2** governs the materials and work-in-progress a
project holds in store before it is consumed; **IAS 16** and **IFRS 16** govern, respectively, the plant a
project owns and the plant and premises it leases; and **IAS 23** governs the financing cost of building a
major asset. A sixth topic is historical rather than a live standard: **IAS 11**, the construction-contracts
standard IFRS 15 replaced, is covered briefly so that a professional who meets legacy terminology — "the
percentage-of-completion method," "contract costs," "recoverable costs" — in an older file or a non-IFRS
jurisdiction can translate it correctly. None of these six topics is treated with the exhaustive depth of
the IFRS 15 flagship chapter; each is developed to the same depth standard as every other Knowledge Area
(definition, principle, formula, a fully worked example, pitfalls, AI use) but kept tightly to what a
project controls professional actually needs: the principle, its project-cost relevance, and one clean
worked example. A single illustrative EPC contractor, **Falcon Marine Engineering LLC** (an offshore
platform fabricator, USD functional currency, SAR shown at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1 where useful),
recurs through the chapter so the six standards can be seen operating on the same underlying project rather
than as six disconnected topics.

### 2.4.1 IAS 37 — Provisions, contingent liabilities and contingent assets

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 37 *Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets* sets out when
a **provision** — a liability of uncertain timing or amount — must be recognised in the financial
statements, and when an uncertain obligation or a possible asset must instead be merely disclosed in the
notes (or not mentioned at all). It exists to stop entities either recognising liabilities too early (before
a genuine obligation exists) or hiding real obligations by calling them merely "possible."

**The underlying principle.** A provision is recognised only when **all three** of the following hold at the
reporting date:

1. The entity has a **present obligation** (legal or **constructive** — arising from an established pattern
   of past practice, published policy, or specific statement that creates a valid expectation in others that
   the entity will discharge it) as a result of a **past event**.
2. It is **probable** that an outflow of economic resources will be required to settle the obligation.
3. A **reliable estimate** can be made of the amount of the obligation.

If the obligation is merely *possible* (its existence will only be confirmed by an uncertain future event
not wholly within the entity's control), or the outflow is not probable, or no reliable estimate can be
made, the item is a **contingent liability**: disclosed in the notes, not recognised on the statement of
financial position — unless the probability of any outflow is remote, in which case even disclosure is not
required. A **contingent asset** (a possible asset whose existence will be confirmed by an uncertain future
event) is disclosed only where an inflow is probable, and is recognised only when realisation becomes
**virtually certain** — at which point it is, by definition, no longer contingent.

**Formula.** Where a range of outcomes exists, IAS 37 requires the provision to be measured at the **best
estimate** of the expenditure required to settle the obligation. For a large population of similar
obligations (e.g. a warranty across many contracts), the best estimate is the **expected value**:

`Provision = Σ (probability_i × outcome_i)`, for all possible outcomes *i*, in the reporting currency.

For a single obligation with a range of possible outcomes clustered around one most likely figure, the best
estimate is usually that most likely outcome, adjusted for other possible outcomes. Where the effect of the
time value of money is material — typically for long-dated obligations such as site restoration — the
provision is **discounted** to present value using a pre-tax rate that reflects current market assessments
of the time value of money and risks specific to the liability:

`PV = FV / (1 + r)^n` — where FV = the estimated future settlement amount, r = the discount rate per period,
n = the number of periods to settlement.

**Example 2.4.1 — a warranty provision by expected value.**

*Scenario.* Falcon Marine has just completed a $10,000,000 offshore platform fabrication contract carrying
a two-year defects-liability warranty. Based on its claims history on comparable contracts, management
assesses three outcomes for the warranty period:

| Outcome | Probability | Cost if it occurs ($) |
|---|---|---|
| No defects | 80% | 0 |
| Minor defects (touch-up/rework) | 15% | 150,000 |
| Major defects (structural rework) | 5% | 600,000 |

*Required.* The warranty provision to recognise at the reporting date.

*Working.*
`Provision = (0.80 × 0) + (0.15 × 150,000) + (0.05 × 600,000)`
`Provision = 0 + 22,500 + 30,000 = 52,500`

*Answer.* **Provision = $52,500** (SAR 196,875 at the illustrative rate).

*Interpretation.* All three IAS 37 recognition tests are met: a present (constructive) obligation exists
from completing the work under warranty terms, an outflow is probable across the population of contracts
even though any single contract may have zero claims, and the expected-value method gives a reliable
estimate. The $52,500 is not "the cost of the worst case" or "the cost of the most likely single outcome" —
it is the probability-weighted average across all outcomes, which is the correct measurement basis for a
population of similar risks.

**Example 2.4.1b — a discounted, long-dated provision.** Falcon Marine also has a legal obligation, imposed
by its offshore licence, to remove a temporary construction platform in 10 years' time. The estimated future
cost of removal is $1,000,000; management uses a pre-tax discount rate of 6% p.a., reflecting the time value
of money for a liability of this duration.

`PV = 1,000,000 / (1.06)^10 = 1,000,000 / 1.790848 = 558,395` (rounded)

**Provision = $558,395** (SAR 2,093,981 at the illustrative rate). Because the obligation itself arises now
(licence condition, past event = building the platform) even though settlement is a decade away, the
provision is recognised now, at its present value — not deferred until year 10. Each year, the discount
unwinds through profit or loss as a finance cost as the provision accretes toward the $1,000,000 settlement
figure; this unwinding is *not* part of the initial cost of the related asset (KA 2.4.3 below shows how the
*initial* $558,395 estimate, not the unwinding, is capitalised).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Recognising a provision for a **future operating loss** or for costs the entity can
still avoid through its own future actions (e.g. a decision not yet made to restructure) — IAS 37 requires a
present obligation from a *past* event, not merely an intention. (2) Confusing "probable" with "certain" —
outflows that are merely *possible* stay as disclosed contingent liabilities, not recognised provisions.
(3) Measuring a warranty provision at the *worst-case* cost rather than the probability-weighted expected
value across the population of similar risks.

**AI in this topic.** Machine-learning models trained on historical warranty-claim data can generate a more
data-driven probability distribution than a purely judgemental estimate — genuinely useful for the expected-
value calculation in Example 2.4.1. The governance limit is real: a model trained on past claims assumes the
future resembles the past (a new design, a new site condition, or a new supplier changes the risk profile in
ways historical data cannot capture), and the model has no way to judge whether the *legal* recognition
tests (present obligation, constructive obligation) are met at all — that remains a judgement for the
accountant, informed but not replaced by the AI-generated probability estimate. AI proposes a probability
distribution; the professional disposes of both the recognition judgement and the final estimate.

asset under IAS 16); KA 7.2 (contract claims and disputes, a common source of contingent liabilities).

### 2.4.2 IAS 2 — Inventories, materials and work-in-progress

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 2 *Inventories* prescribes the accounting for inventories: assets held for
sale in the ordinary course of business, in the process of production for such sale, or in the form of
materials or supplies to be consumed in production or in rendering services. It matters to project controls
because materials stockpiled at site or in a laydown yard, spare parts, and off-site prefabricated modules
awaiting allocation to a contract are all inventory *until* they are consumed into a specific customer
contract — at which point their cost transfers out of inventory and into contract fulfilment cost under
IFRS 15 (KA 2.2). IAS 2 explicitly does **not** cover costs already recognised as fulfilling a contract with
a customer under IFRS 15 — historically this boundary sat with the now-withdrawn IAS 11 (KA 2.4.5); today it
sits with IFRS 15.

**The underlying principle.** Inventory is measured at the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)**.
Carrying inventory above what it can actually realise would overstate assets and understate the loss the
entity has effectively already suffered; carrying it below cost when it can still be sold or used at full
value would understate assets for no good reason. The rule is deliberately conservative and asymmetric: an
increase above original cost is never recognised, but a fall below cost is recognised as soon as evidence of
it exists, and can be reversed (up to the original cost) only if the circumstances that caused the write-down
no longer apply.

**Formula.**

`Cost of inventory = costs of purchase (purchase price + import duties + directly attributable transport/`
`handling − trade discounts and rebates) + costs of conversion (direct labour + a systematic allocation of`
`production overheads) + other costs to bring the item to its present location and condition`

`NRV = estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business − estimated costs of completion −`
`estimated costs necessary to make the sale`

`Carrying value = MIN(Cost, NRV)`; where multiple identical units are held, cost is assigned using **FIFO**
or **weighted average cost** (IFRS does not permit LIFO).

**Example 2.4.2 — steel pipe inventory: cost build-up, consumption and a year-end NRV test.**

*Scenario.* Falcon Marine purchases 500 tonnes of steel pipe for a pipeline tie-in package:

| Cost element | Amount ($) |
|---|---|
| Purchase price (500 t × $1,200/t) | 600,000 |
| Import duty | 30,000 |
| Freight to the laydown yard | 45,000 |
| Less: volume trade discount | (15,000) |
| **Total cost of purchase** | **660,000** |

By the year-end, 400 tonnes have been installed into the contract works (transferred out of inventory into
contract fulfilment cost, KA 2.2), leaving 100 tonnes in stock. A specification change on a separate,
unrelated project means Falcon Marine now expects to resell the remaining 100 tonnes rather than use them;
estimated resale price is $900/tonne and estimated costs to sell (handling, transport) are $40/tonne.

*Required.* (a) The cost per tonne; (b) the cost transferred to contract fulfilment cost; (c) the year-end
inventory write-down, if any.

*Working.*
(a) `Cost per tonne = 660,000 / 500 = $1,320/t`
(b) `Cost transferred (400 t installed) = 400 × 1,320 = $528,000`
(c) Remaining 100 t: `Cost = 100 × 1,320 = $132,000`. `NRV = (900 − 40) × 100 = 860 × 100 = $86,000`.
Since NRV ($86,000) < cost ($132,000): `Write-down = 132,000 − 86,000 = $46,000`

*Answer.* **Cost/tonne = $1,320; $528,000 transferred to contract cost; year-end inventory carried at
$86,000 after a $46,000 write-down** recognised as an expense in profit or loss. Check: $528,000 +
$132,000 = $660,000, the full original purchase cost accounted for. ✓

*Interpretation.* Two separate things happen to the same $660,000 of purchased steel: most of it (400 t)
simply moves from one asset category (inventory) into contract cost as it is consumed by the contract it
was bought for; the remainder, no longer needed for its original purpose, must be tested against what it can
actually realise, and written down because circumstances outside the original contract have changed its
value.

**Example 2.4.2b — the inventory/contract-cost boundary.** Falcon Marine also fabricates a standard
mooring-buoy module to stock, at a cost of $85,000, before any customer contract exists for it — this is IAS
2 finished-goods inventory. Three months later, a customer signs a $150,000 contract that specifically
allocates this module. On allocation, the $85,000 stops being IAS 2 inventory and becomes a cost of
fulfilling that IFRS 15 contract (KA 2.2) — the module itself does not move, only its accounting
classification does, at the moment it is committed to a specific customer contract.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Carrying materials at cost indefinitely without testing NRV when circumstances
change (obsolescence, specification changes, market price falls) — the write-down must be recognised as
soon as the evidence exists, not deferred to a convenient period. (2) Including **general or selling
overheads** in the cost of inventory — only directly attributable costs of purchase and conversion qualify.
(3) Treating materials already consumed into a specific contract as if they remain a separately reportable
inventory balance, rather than transferring their cost into contract fulfilment cost.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted demand-forecasting and stock-ageing tools can flag slow-moving or
potentially obsolete inventory lines for an NRV review far earlier than a purely periodic manual review —
useful for triggering the right conversation at the right time. The tool can flag *candidates*; it cannot
itself determine the realistic resale price or completion/selling costs for a write-down, which depend on
current market conditions and management's genuine intent for the stock — a judgement that remains with the
professional.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.5 (cost coding — inventory vs contract cost control accounts); KA 2.2 (IFRS 15
contract fulfilment costs); KA 2.4.5 (the historical IAS 11 boundary this scope exclusion now sits with
IFRS 15); KA 5.2 (cost control cycle: commitment → accrual → actual).

### 2.4.3 IAS 16 — Property, plant and equipment

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 16 *Property, Plant and Equipment* governs the recognition, initial
measurement, depreciation and derecognition of tangible items held for use in production or supply of goods
or services, for rental, or for administrative purposes, and expected to be used for more than one period.
For project controls, IAS 16 governs the capital plant a project *owns* — cranes, piling rigs, fabrication
structures, site facilities — as distinct from IFRS 16 (KA 2.4.4), which governs plant a project *leases*.

**The underlying principle.** An item is recognised as PP&E only if it is probable that future economic
benefits will flow to the entity and its cost can be measured reliably. Once recognised, its **depreciable
amount** (cost less residual value) is allocated systematically over its **useful life**, so that the
statement of profit or loss reflects the asset being consumed as it is used, rather than the full cost
hitting profit in the year of purchase or not at all until disposal. Crucially for project controls, the
*cost* of an asset is not just its purchase price: it includes every cost directly attributable to bringing
it to the location and condition necessary for it to operate as intended — and, where a decommissioning or
site-restoration obligation exists, the **initial estimate** of that obligation (measured under IAS 37,
KA 2.4.1) is added to the asset's cost at recognition.

**Formula.**

`Cost = purchase price (net of trade discounts, plus non-refundable import duties) + directly attributable`
`costs (delivery, installation, testing, professional fees) + initial estimate of dismantling/restoration`
`costs (per IAS 37)`

`Annual depreciation (straight-line) = (Cost − Residual value) / Useful life` (units: currency per period;
other methods — diminishing balance, units of production — allocate the same depreciable amount differently
over time but must still reflect the pattern of the asset's consumption).

**Example 2.4.3 — capitalising construction cost together with a decommissioning provision.**

*Scenario.* Falcon Marine builds a temporary fabrication support structure at a cost of $1,450,000 in
direct construction costs (materials, labour, testing). Under its offshore licence it also has a legal
obligation to remove the structure in 10 years — this is the same obligation measured in **Example 2.4.1b**
at a present value of **$558,395**. The structure has a 10-year useful life (matching the removal horizon)
and nil residual value, since it will be fully dismantled.

*Required.* The asset's initial cost and the annual straight-line depreciation charge.

*Working.*
`Initial cost = 1,450,000 (construction) + 558,395 (IAS 37 decommissioning provision) = 2,008,395`
`Annual depreciation = (2,008,395 − 0) / 10 = 200,839.50`

*Answer.* **Initial cost = $2,008,395** (SAR 7,531,481 at the illustrative rate); **annual depreciation =
$200,839.50** for 10 years.

*Interpretation.* IAS 16 and IAS 37 interlock here: the $558,395 provision recognised under IAS 37 does not
merely sit as a liability — it simultaneously becomes part of the cost of the asset it relates to under
IAS 16, and is then depreciated over the asset's life alongside the construction cost. Choosing a 10-year
useful life for the depreciation charge that matches the 10-year removal horizon is deliberate: an asset
whose useful life is shorter or longer than its associated decommissioning date needs its depreciation
schedule and its provision's unwinding period reconciled by the professional, not left inconsistent.

**Example 2.4.3b — a disposal, gain and loss.** Falcon Marine separately buys a piling rig: price
$1,000,000, import duty $50,000, delivery $40,000, installation and testing $30,000, less a $20,000 trade
discount — **total cost = $1,100,000**. Estimated residual value $100,000, useful life 8 years:
`Annual depreciation = (1,100,000 − 100,000) / 8 = $125,000`. After 5 years, accumulated depreciation =
`125,000 × 5 = $625,000`, so carrying amount = `1,100,000 − 625,000 = $475,000`. The rig is then sold for
$520,000: `Gain on disposal = 520,000 − 475,000 = $45,000`. The gain is recognised in profit or loss at
disposal — it is not revenue from the entity's ordinary construction activity, and is reported separately
(cross-reference KA 8.5, project/asset closeout).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Expensing directly attributable costs (delivery, installation, testing) as period
cost instead of capitalising them into the asset's cost. (2) Forgetting to include an IAS 37 decommissioning
estimate in the asset's initial cost where a genuine obligation exists — this understates both the asset and
the associated depreciation charge over its life. (3) Depreciating a highly specific, single-project asset
over a "standard" useful life taken from a fixed-asset register rather than the asset's *actual* expected
useful life in this deployment (which may be shorter — a temporary structure removed at year 10 is not a
50-year building).

**AI in this topic.** Fixed-asset and ERP modules increasingly use AI to reconcile a physical asset
register against ledger balances and flag assets that appear to be depreciating on a useful life
inconsistent with similar assets elsewhere in the portfolio — a genuinely useful consistency check. It
cannot determine, on its own, the *correct* useful life or residual value for a novel or highly
project-specific asset (a temporary offshore structure has no deep historical base to learn from), and it
cannot judge whether a decommissioning provision genuinely exists and should be added to cost — both remain
matters of professional and, where material, actuarial/engineering judgement.

### 2.4.4 IFRS 16 — Leases

**Definition and purpose.** IFRS 16 *Leases* requires a lessee to recognise, for almost every lease, a
**right-of-use (ROU) asset** and a corresponding **lease liability** on the statement of financial position
— a single on-balance-sheet model that replaced the earlier operating/finance lease split for lessees. A
lease exists where a contract conveys the right to control the use of an identified asset for a period of
time in exchange for consideration. Two narrow exemptions let a lessee simply expense payments instead:
**short-term leases** (term of 12 months or less, with no purchase option) and leases of **low-value
underlying assets**, applied by class or asset respectively.

**The underlying principle.** A lease is, economically, very similar to buying an asset with borrowed money
— the lessee controls and uses the asset for the lease term and owes a stream of future payments for that
right. IFRS 16 reflects that economic substance directly: the lease liability is initially measured at the
**present value** of the lease payments not yet paid, and the ROU asset is depreciated like any other
owned asset, while the liability is unwound like a loan (interest expense on the declining balance plus
principal repayment with each cash payment).

**Formula.**

`Lease liability (initial) = Σ [Payment_t / (1 + r)^t]`, t = 1 … n, where r = the rate implicit in the lease
if readily determinable, otherwise the lessee's **incremental borrowing rate**, and n = the number of
payment periods in the lease term.

`ROU asset (initial) = Lease liability + payments made at/before commencement − lease incentives received +`
`initial direct costs + estimated dismantling/restoration costs (per IAS 37, where applicable)`

**Example 2.4.4 — a 3-year heavy-lift crane lease.**

*Scenario.* Falcon Marine leases a heavy-lift crane for a 3-year project: $400,000 payable annually in
arrears; incremental borrowing rate 8% p.a. (no incentives, initial direct costs, or restoration obligation,
for simplicity).

*Required.* (a) The initial lease liability; (b) the full 3-year amortisation of the liability; (c) the
annual ROU depreciation charge; (d) the total year-1 profit-or-loss impact.

*Working.*

(a) `PV annuity factor (3 yr @ 8%) = 1/1.08 + 1/1.08² + 1/1.08³ = 0.925926 + 0.857339 + 0.793832 = 2.577097`
`Lease liability = 400,000 × 2.577097 = $1,030,839` (rounded; SAR 3,865,646 at the illustrative rate)

(b)

| Year | Opening liability ($) | Interest @ 8% ($) | Payment ($) | Principal repaid ($) | Closing liability ($) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,030,839 | 82,467 | 400,000 | 317,533 | 713,306 |
| 2 | 713,306 | 57,064 | 400,000 | 342,936 | 370,370 |
| 3 | 370,370 | 29,630 | 400,000 | 370,370 | 0 |

(c) `ROU depreciation = 1,030,839 / 3 years (straight-line over the lease term) = $343,613/year`

(d) `Year-1 total charge = interest 82,467 + depreciation 343,613 = $426,080`

*Answer.* **Initial lease liability = $1,030,839; the liability fully amortises to $0 by the end of Year 3;
Year-1 profit-or-loss charge = $426,080**, compared with the $400,000 cash paid.

*Interpretation.* The $26,080 gap between the Year-1 charge ($426,080) and the cash rental ($400,000)
illustrates a well-known IFRS 16 effect: because interest is calculated on a declining balance, the combined
interest-plus-depreciation charge is **front-loaded** relative to a level cash rental — highest in Year 1,
falling each year, even though the cash payment is flat at $400,000 throughout. Total cost over the full
three years is unchanged ($1,200,000 of cash paid; total interest $82,467+57,064+29,630 = $169,161, exactly
matching $1,200,000 − $1,030,839 the amount by which cash payments exceed the initial liability) — only its
timing across the three years differs from the cash profile.

**Example 2.4.4b — the short-term lease exemption.** Falcon Marine also hires site cabins for a 6-month
mobilisation period at $5,000/month, total $30,000. Because the term is 12 months or less and there is no
purchase option, Falcon Marine elects the short-term lease exemption: no ROU asset or lease liability is
recognised; the $30,000 is simply expensed on a straight-line basis, $5,000/month, exactly as a simple
rental always was under the pre-IFRS 16 regime.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating all leases as simple rental expense out of habit — under IFRS 16 this is
only correct for the two narrow exemptions (short-term, low value); every other lease requires the full ROU
asset/liability model. (2) Using the interest rate implicit in the lease when it is not, in fact, readily
determinable to the lessee — the incremental borrowing rate should be used instead, and getting this wrong
changes both the liability and the ROU asset. (3) Forgetting that a project cost report's "equipment rental"
line for a leased crane like this one is no longer a single, level cash-expense line — it becomes a
depreciation charge plus a declining interest charge, which a report reader unfamiliar with IFRS 16 may
misread as unrelated cost movements.

**AI in this topic.** Lease-accounting software (often AI-assisted) can extract key terms — payment amounts,
term, options — from a scanned lease contract and auto-populate the amortisation schedule, a significant
efficiency gain for entities with many equipment leases. It cannot reliably judge lease **classification
edge cases** (whether a contract truly conveys the right to control an *identified* asset, or is in
substance a service contract with no identified asset) or select an appropriate incremental borrowing rate
without human input — extraction errors or a wrongly selected discount rate flow straight through to a
materially wrong liability, so extracted terms must be reviewed against the actual signed contract before
the schedule is relied upon.

purposes); KA 5.1 (cost types — how a leased asset's cost now appears across a project cost report); KA 7.1
(contract types — a lease is itself a form of contract with its own risk allocation).

### 2.4.5 IAS 11 (withdrawn) — the legacy construction-contracts standard, and why IFRS 15 replaced it

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 11 *Construction Contracts* was the IFRS standard governing revenue and cost
recognition on construction contracts before it was withdrawn, superseded by **IFRS 15** *Revenue from
Contracts with Customers* for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2018 (issued jointly with
IAS 18 *Revenue*'s replacement, as part of a project converged with US GAAP's ASC 606). A project controls
professional will still meet IAS 11's terminology in older financial statements, in jurisdictions or
counterparties slow to fully retire the language, and in legacy contract-accounting policies — so this topic
exists to enable that translation, not to teach IAS 11 as current practice.

**The underlying principle (as it operated).** IAS 11 required contract revenue and contract costs to be
recognised by reference to the **percentage of completion** of the contract activity at the reporting date,
*where the outcome of the contract could be estimated reliably* — an input-based percentage (costs incurred
to date ÷ total estimated contract costs) was the most common measure used in practice. Where the outcome
could **not** be estimated reliably, IAS 11 required a more conservative approach: revenue recognised only
to the extent of contract costs incurred that were probably recoverable, with the corresponding costs
expensed as incurred (the "recoverable-costs" or "zero-profit" approach) — recognising no profit until the
outcome became reliably estimable.

**Why IFRS 15 replaced it.** IAS 11 was a construction-industry-specific standard, sitting alongside the
general-purpose IAS 18 (which used a risks-and-rewards transfer test for most other goods and services) —
two different recognition models for economically similar situations produced inconsistent practice across
industries. IFRS 15 replaced both with a single, comprehensive **five-step model** (KA 2.2) applied
identically across every industry: identify the contract; identify the performance obligations; determine
the transaction price; allocate it to the performance obligations; recognise revenue as (or when) each
performance obligation is satisfied. Critically, IFRS 15 does not presume that construction contracts
automatically qualify for "percentage-of-completion"-style, over-time recognition — a contract must actively
satisfy one of IFRS 15's specific over-time criteria (broadly: the customer simultaneously receives and
consumes the benefit as the entity performs; the entity's performance creates or enhances an asset the
customer controls as it is created; or the asset has no alternative use to the entity *and* the entity has
an enforceable right to payment for performance completed to date). Only where one of these is met is
revenue recognised over time (using an input or output method, KA 2.2) — otherwise revenue is recognised at
a single point in time, when control transfers.

**Example 2.4.5 — the same arithmetic, a different test.** Falcon Marine has a $5,000,000 fabrication
contract, estimated total cost $4,000,000, with $1,000,000 of cost incurred to date.
`% complete (input method) = 1,000,000 / 4,000,000 = 25%`
`Revenue recognised = 25% × 5,000,000 = $1,250,000`; `Cost recognised = $1,000,000`; `Profit recognised =
$250,000`. Under old IAS 11 practice, this arithmetic — the input-method percentage-of-completion
calculation — would have looked identical. Under IFRS 15, the same numbers are produced, but only *after*
first confirming this specific contract passes an over-time recognition criterion (here: the customer
controls the platform as it is built, and Falcon Marine has an enforceable right to payment for work
performed) and *then* choosing the input method to measure progress (KA 2.2). The number is the same; the
**test that justifies using it is not**.

**Example 2.4.5b — where the old presumption and the new test diverge.** Consider a $2,000,000 contract for
a standard, modular platform component, estimated total cost $1,600,000, with $400,000 (25%) of cost
incurred to date. Under blanket IAS 11-era practice, a contractor might simply apply percentage of
completion and recognise $500,000 of revenue (25% × 2,000,000). If, however, this module is a standardised
design sellable to any customer (it has an **alternative use** to Falcon Marine) and the contract does **not**
give Falcon Marine an enforceable right to payment for work completed to date if the customer cancels, none
of IFRS 15's over-time criteria is met — so **no revenue is recognised at this stage at all**; the full
$2,000,000 is recognised only at the single point in time when control of the completed module transfers to
the customer. The two standards can genuinely diverge, not just in terminology but in the amount and timing
of revenue recognised — which is exactly why the professional must actively test each contract, not assume
construction-contract treatment applies by default.

**Quick-reference — translating IAS 11 terminology to IFRS 15:**

| IAS 11 (withdrawn) term | IFRS 15 equivalent |
|---|---|
| Contract revenue | Transaction price allocated to the performance obligation(s) |
| Contract costs | Costs incurred / costs to fulfil the contract |
| Percentage of completion (input-based) | Input method, applied only once the over-time criteria are met |
| "Outcome reliably estimable" gate | The over-time recognition criteria, plus the ability to reasonably measure progress |
| Recoverable-costs / zero-profit approach | A constrained-estimate outcome under IFRS 15's variable-consideration guidance, where progress cannot yet be reasonably measured |

### 2.4.6 IAS 23 — Borrowing costs

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 23 *Borrowing Costs* requires borrowing costs directly attributable to the
acquisition, construction or production of a **qualifying asset** — one that necessarily takes a substantial
period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale — to be **capitalised** as part of that asset's
cost. All other borrowing costs are expensed as incurred. For major EPC and infrastructure projects financed
partly by debt, this determines whether part of the project's financing cost sits inside the capital cost
report (as part of the asset) or below the line as a period finance expense.

**The underlying principle.** While an asset is under construction and not yet generating economic benefit,
the financing cost of building it is treated as part of the cost of getting the asset ready — matching the
cost to the asset it created, in the same spirit as any other directly attributable construction cost under
IAS 16 (KA 2.4.3). Capitalisation begins only once expenditure and borrowing costs are both being incurred
*and* the activities necessary to prepare the asset are actively under way; it is suspended during extended
periods where active development is interrupted, and it **ceases** once substantially all the activities
necessary to prepare the asset for its intended use are complete — continuing to capitalise after that point
would overstate the asset and understate period finance cost. IAS 23's definition of "borrowing costs"
explicitly includes interest on lease liabilities recognised under IFRS 16 (KA 2.4.4) — so if a right-of-use
asset were itself under construction as a qualifying asset, its lease interest could in principle be
capitalised on exactly the same basis as loan interest.

**Formula.** For a borrowing taken out **specifically** to fund a qualifying asset:

`Capitalised borrowing cost = actual borrowing costs incurred on that borrowing during the period − any`
`investment income earned on the temporary investment of unspent borrowed funds`

For **general** borrowings (no specific loan, funds drawn from a general pool):

`Capitalisation rate = weighted average of the borrowing costs applicable to the general borrowings`
`outstanding during the period`

`Capitalised borrowing cost = weighted-average accumulated expenditure on the qualifying asset during the`
`period × capitalisation rate` (capped at the actual borrowing costs incurred on the general pool during the
period).

**Example 2.4.6 — a specific borrowing, with temporary investment income.**

*Scenario.* Falcon Marine draws a $6,000,000 loan specifically to fund construction of a qualifying asset,
at 7% p.a., for the full year. $1,000,000 of the drawn funds is not immediately needed and is invested
temporarily for 3 months at 4% p.a. before being spent.

*Required.* The borrowing cost to capitalise for the year.

*Working.*
`Borrowing cost incurred = 6,000,000 × 7% = 420,000`
`Investment income earned = 1,000,000 × 4% × 3/12 = 10,000`
`Capitalised borrowing cost = 420,000 − 10,000 = 410,000`

*Answer.* **Capitalised borrowing cost = $410,000**, added to the cost of the qualifying asset under
construction (and, once the asset is complete, depreciated as part of its cost under IAS 16, KA 2.4.3).

*Interpretation.* The $10,000 of investment income is netted off, not recognised separately as investment
income in profit or loss — the logic being that idle-fund investment while waiting to spend specifically
borrowed money is part of the same financing arrangement for the asset, so only the *net* cost of financing
is capitalised.

**Example 2.4.6b — general borrowings, weighted-average capitalisation rate.** Falcon Marine has no specific
loan for a second qualifying asset, but general borrowings outstanding all year: Loan A, $2,000,000 at 6%;
Loan B, $3,000,000 at 8%.

`Total borrowing cost = (2,000,000 × 6%) + (3,000,000 × 8%) = 120,000 + 240,000 = 360,000`
`Weighted-average capitalisation rate = 360,000 / (2,000,000 + 3,000,000) = 360,000 / 5,000,000 = 7.2%`

If the weighted-average accumulated expenditure on the qualifying asset during the year is $1,500,000:
`Capitalised borrowing cost = 1,500,000 × 7.2% = $108,000`

This is well below the $360,000 total borrowing cost for the period, so the cap is not binding; the
remaining $252,000 of borrowing cost is expensed in profit or loss as ordinary finance cost, since only the
portion attributable to the qualifying asset's own expenditure is capitalised.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Continuing to capitalise borrowing costs after the asset is substantially complete
but before it is formally commissioned or handed over — the test is *substantial completion of the
activities necessary to prepare the asset*, not the administrative handover date. (2) Capitalising borrowing
costs during a suspension of active construction (e.g. a lengthy dispute or funding pause) — capitalisation
should be suspended for extended interruptions, not continued by default. (3) Forgetting to net off
investment income earned on temporarily idle, specifically borrowed funds before capitalising the balance.

**AI in this topic.** Treasury and project-cost systems can increasingly automate the tracking of weighted-
average accumulated expenditure against a qualifying asset and flag the capitalisation rate to finance for
review — useful for keeping the calculation current as a project's drawdown profile changes. Judging *when*
construction activity has been substantially completed (the cessation trigger) or genuinely suspended (the
interruption trigger) depends on engineering and commercial facts an automated system cannot observe
directly; these triggers must be confirmed by the project team and finance jointly before the capitalisation
period is closed off.

### Key terms

- **Provision** — a liability of uncertain timing or amount, recognised when a present obligation, a
  probable outflow and a reliable estimate all exist (IAS 37).
- **Constructive obligation** — an obligation arising from established practice, published policy or
  specific statement, not merely a legal contract or statute.
- **Contingent liability / contingent asset** — a possible obligation/asset whose existence depends on an
  uncertain future event; disclosed, not recognised (an asset is recognised only when virtually certain).
- **Expected value method** — measuring a provision as the probability-weighted average of its possible
  outcomes.
- **Net realisable value (NRV)** — estimated selling price less estimated costs of completion and sale
  (IAS 2); inventory is carried at the lower of cost and NRV.
- **Qualifying asset** — an asset that necessarily takes a substantial period to get ready for its intended
  use or sale (IAS 23).
- **Depreciable amount / useful life** — cost less residual value, allocated systematically over the period
  the asset is expected to be used (IAS 16).
- **Right-of-use (ROU) asset / lease liability** — the two balance-sheet items a lessee recognises for
  (almost) every lease under IFRS 16.
- **Incremental borrowing rate** — the rate a lessee would pay to borrow, over a similar term and security,
  the funds necessary to obtain an asset of similar value; used to discount lease payments where the rate
  implicit in the lease is not readily determinable.
- **Short-term lease / low-value asset exemption** — the two IFRS 16 exemptions letting a lessee expense
  lease payments instead of recognising a ROU asset and liability.
- **Percentage of completion (input method)** — measuring progress as costs incurred to date over total
  estimated costs; a legacy IAS 11 term now operating, where applicable, as the IFRS 15 input method.
- **Capitalisation rate** — the weighted-average borrowing cost rate applied to a qualifying asset's
  accumulated expenditure where funded from general (not specific) borrowings (IAS 23).

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 2.4.1 [topic 2.4.1, level: application — numerical]**
A contractor's warranty exposure has three possible outcomes: 70% chance of $0 cost, 20% chance of $80,000,
and 10% chance of $500,000. Using the IAS 37 expected value method, what provision should be recognised?
A. $50,000
B. $58,000
C. $66,000 ✔
D. $80,000

*Rationale:* Expected value = (0.70×0) + (0.20×80,000) + (0.10×500,000) = 0 + 16,000 + 50,000 = $66,000
(§2.4.1). D takes only the highest single outcome, ignoring probabilities; A and B are plausible arithmetic
slips that omit or misweight one term.

**MCQ 2.4.2 [topic 2.4.2, level: application — numerical]**
A specialist valve is held at a cost of $2,500/unit, 200 units in stock. Due to obsolescence, estimated
selling price is $2,000/unit and estimated costs to sell are $150/unit. What total write-down is required
under IAS 2?
A. $100,000
B. $130,000 ✔
C. $150,000
D. $30,000

*Rationale:* NRV = 2,000 − 150 = $1,850/unit; write-down = (2,500 − 1,850) × 200 = $130,000 (§2.4.2). A
ignores the costs to sell (treats NRV as selling price alone); C uses an incorrect selling-cost figure; D
computes only the total selling costs, not the write-down.

**MCQ 2.4.3 [topic 2.4.3, level: application — numerical]**
An item of plant costs $300,000, plus $20,000 freight and $15,000 installation, less a $10,000 trade
discount; estimated residual value $25,000; useful life 6 years. What is the annual straight-line
depreciation charge?
A. $54,167
B. $50,000 ✔
C. $44,167
D. $51,667

*Rationale:* Total cost = 300,000 + 20,000 + 15,000 − 10,000 = $325,000; depreciable amount = 325,000 −
25,000 = $300,000; ÷ 6 years = $50,000 (§2.4.3). A omits the residual value; C wrongly excludes freight and
installation from cost; D fails to net off the trade discount.

**MCQ 2.4.4 [topic 2.4.4, level: recall]**
Under IFRS 16, a lessee may elect to expense lease payments without recognising a right-of-use asset or
lease liability for:
A. Any lease with fixed payments
B. Short-term leases (term of 12 months or less, no purchase option) and leases of low-value underlying
   assets ✔
C. All leases where the lessor retains legal ownership of the asset
D. Any lease with total payments under $1,000,000

*Rationale:* IFRS 16 provides exactly two recognition exemptions for lessees — short-term leases and
low-value assets (§2.4.4). C effectively describes almost every lease (lessors nearly always retain legal
title) and is not the basis of the exemption; D invents a monetary threshold that does not exist in the
standard.

**MCQ 2.4.5 [topic 2.4.5, level: analysis]**
Which statement best explains why IFRS 15 superseded IAS 11 for construction contracts?
A. IFRS 15 abolished revenue recognition for long-term contracts entirely
B. IAS 11's construction-specific percentage-of-completion approach was replaced by a single five-step model
   applied across all industries, with over-time recognition conditional on meeting specific criteria rather
   than presumed ✔
C. IFRS 15 requires all construction contracts to recognise revenue only at a single point in time
D. IAS 11 and IFRS 15 are identical in substance; IFRS 15 only renamed IAS 11's requirements

*Rationale:* IFRS 15 replaced the industry-specific IAS 11 (and IAS 18) with one comprehensive,
criteria-based model in which over-time recognition must be actively tested per contract (§2.4.5, cross-ref
KA 2.2). A and C both misstate IFRS 15's actual mechanics; D understates the genuine conditionality IFRS 15
introduced, illustrated in Example 2.4.5b.

**MCQ 2.4.6 [topic 2.4.6, level: application — numerical]**
A company borrows $4,000,000 specifically at 6% p.a. for a full year to fund a qualifying asset, and
temporarily invests $500,000 of the unused funds for 6 months at 3% p.a. What borrowing cost should be
capitalised under IAS 23?
A. $225,000
B. $232,500 ✔
C. $240,000
D. $247,500

*Rationale:* Borrowing cost = 4,000,000 × 6% = $240,000; investment income = 500,000 × 3% × 6/12 = $7,500;
capitalised amount = 240,000 − 7,500 = $232,500 (§2.4.6). C ignores the investment-income offset entirely;
D incorrectly adds the investment income instead of deducting it.

### Self-check questions

1. State the three IAS 37 recognition criteria for a provision, and explain whether an unresolved lawsuit
   against a contractor, where the outcome and eventual cost are both genuinely uncertain, would normally be
   recognised as a provision or disclosed as a contingent liability.
2. A contractor holds inventory at a cost of $90,000; its net realisable value is $75,000. What accounting
   is required under IAS 2, and what amount, if any, is recognised in profit or loss?
3. Explain, in your own words, why a lessee's combined interest-plus-depreciation charge in the early years
   of an IFRS 16 lease is typically higher than the level cash rental that used to be reported under a
   simple operating lease.
4. Explain why capitalisation of borrowing costs under IAS 23 must stop at a defined point, and describe one
   consequence for project cost reporting if that cessation point is missed.

**Answers**

1. The three criteria are: a present obligation (legal or constructive) from a past event; a probable
   outflow of economic resources; and a reliable estimate of the amount. An unresolved lawsuit where both
   the outcome and the eventual cost are genuinely uncertain typically fails the "reliable estimate" test (or
   the outflow is not yet assessed as probable), so it is normally disclosed as a **contingent liability**,
   not recognised as a provision — it would only become a provision once an outflow becomes probable and can
   be reliably estimated (§2.4.1).
2. Inventory is carried at the lower of cost and NRV. Since NRV ($75,000) is below cost ($90,000), the
   inventory is written down to $75,000, and the **$15,000 write-down is recognised as an expense in profit
   or loss** in the period it occurs (§2.4.2).
3. Interest on the lease liability is calculated on the (declining) opening balance each period, so it is
   highest in Year 1 and falls thereafter, while ROU depreciation is typically level (straight-line). Adding
   a high early interest charge to a level depreciation charge produces a combined charge that is higher than
   a flat cash rental in the early years and lower later — total cost across the full lease term is
   unchanged, only its timing shifts (§2.4.4, Example 2.4.4).
4. Capitalisation must stop once substantially all the activities necessary to prepare the qualifying asset
   for its intended use are complete — continuing beyond that point overstates the asset's cost and
   understates the period's finance expense. If missed, the capital cost baseline and future depreciation
   charge are overstated (feeding an inflated asset value into project cost/overhead reports), while true
   in-period finance cost is understated, distorting both the capital cost report and the entity's reported
   profitability (§2.4.6).
