## KA 6.4 — Integrating cost & schedule

KA 6.1 built PV, EV and AC; KA 6.2 turned them into variances and indices; KA 6.3 turned those into a

### 6.4.1 The Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB): integrating the schedule and cost baselines

**Definition & purpose.** The Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB) is the time-phased budget — PV, as used
throughout KA 6.1–6.3 — produced by integrating three separate baselines onto one common structure: the
approved **scope** (the WBS), the approved **schedule** (the Integrated Master Schedule, IMS, referenced in
PMI's Practice Standard for Scheduling), and the approved **budget**. PV is not simply "the budget spread
smoothly over the project duration"; it is, properly built, the sum, period by period, of the budgeted cost
of exactly the activities the IMS schedules to occur in that period.

**The underlying principle.** Cost data and schedule data do not naturally live in the same structure — cost
is normally organised by a Cost Breakdown Structure/chart of accounts (KA 1.5), while a schedule is a network
of activities with logic and durations (KA 10.1). Without deliberate integration on a common WBS/control-
account structure (previewed here, developed fully in 6.4.3), an organisation ends up with two disconnected
reports: a cost report showing spend by month, and a schedule report showing activity dates, with no way to
confirm that this month's spend relates to the activities the schedule actually planned for this month. The
PMB is what results once both are deliberately built on the same structure and the same data date.

**Formula.** `PMB(t) = PV(t) = Σ (budgeted cost of IMS activities scheduled to be complete, periods 1 → t)` —
the same PV already defined in KA 6.1.2, now stated explicitly as a schedule-derived quantity.

**Example 6.4.1 — Why an integrated (schedule-derived) PV spread differs from a naive, non-integrated one.**

*Scenario.* WP3 (Trenching & Earthworks), budget $200,000, is scheduled per the approved IMS to run Months
2–5 (4 months) — consistent with the resource-loaded plan developed in 6.4.2. Two ways of spreading its
budget into PV are compared at the Month-2 data date: (a) the schedule-integrated spread, which recognises
WP3 has not started until Month 2; (b) a naive spread that ignores the IMS and simply divides the $200,000
evenly across the whole 6-month project.

*Required.* Cumulative PV for WP3 at Month 2 under each approach.

*Working.*
(a) Integrated: by the end of Month 2, 1 of WP3's 4 scheduled months has elapsed →
`PV = 1/4 × 200,000 = 50,000`.
(b) Naive: `200,000 / 6 = 33,333.33/month`; by the end of Month 2 (2 months elapsed) →
`PV = 2 × 33,333.33 = 66,666.67 ≈ 66,667`.

*Answer.* Integrated PV = **$50,000**; naive PV = **$66,667** — an overstatement of **$16,667**.

*Interpretation.* The naive spread assumes trenching is already a third complete in planning terms by Month
2, when the approved schedule says it has not started at all — a purely artefactual distortion that would
make any subsequent SV reading unreliable, entirely because the cost baseline was never actually built from
the schedule. This is exactly the failure the PMB concept exists to prevent.

- Building a PV curve to *look* like a plausible S-curve (a smoothing assumption) rather than deriving it

- Updating the schedule (dates, logic) without a corresponding update to the cost baseline, so the two drift
  apart silently over time.
- Treating the PMB as fixed once built rather than subject to the same re-baselining discipline as PV
  (KA 6.1.2) whenever an approved change occurs.

cannot confirm that the underlying schedule logic or resource assumptions are themselves realistic — that
remains a scheduling and estimating judgement (KA 3.2, KA 10.1) the professional must apply before any
automated PMB is baselined.

### 6.4.2 Resource-loaded and cost-loaded schedules: building PV from the execution plan

**Definition & purpose.** A resource-loaded schedule assigns the actual resources planned to execute each
activity — labour crews, equipment, quantities, at stated productivity and cost rates — directly onto the
schedule's activities and time periods. Pricing that resource loading produces a **cost-loaded schedule**:
the period-by-period cost distribution that is summed and cumulated to produce PV (6.4.1). This is the most
defensible way to time-phase a budget, because the spread is derived from an actual execution plan rather
than assumed.

**The underlying principle.** A schedule's activities carry logic and durations (KA 10.1) but say nothing,
by themselves, about cost. Loading resources converts schedule *time* into schedule *cost*: planned output
per period (a productivity rate × the period's available working time) priced at a unit rate gives that
activity's cost contribution for the period; summing across all active activities gives the period's PV.

**Formula.** `PV(activity, period) = productivity (units/day) × working days (period) × unit rate ($/unit)`;
summed across all activities active in a period gives `PV(period)`; cumulated across periods gives `PV(t)`.

**Example 6.4.2 — Building WP3's monthly PV from its resource-loaded schedule.**

*Scenario.* WP3 (Trenching & Earthworks) has a total scope of 2,000 m at a unit rate of $100/m
($200,000 total, per Example 6.1.4). The resource-loaded schedule assigns one trenching crew to WP3 for 4
months (Months 2–5), planned at 25 m per working day, 20 working days per month.

*Required.* (a) the crew's planned monthly output; (b) the resulting monthly PV contribution from WP3; (c)
confirmation that the four months sum to WP3's full budget.

*Working.*
(a) `25 m/day × 20 days/month = 500 m/month`.
(b) `500 m/month × $100/m = $50,000/month`, for each of Months 2, 3, 4 and 5.
(c) `4 × 50,000 = 200,000` — matches WP3's budget exactly.

*Answer.* WP3 contributes **$50,000/month** to PV in Months 2–5, and $0 in Months 1 and 6, reconciling
exactly to its $200,000 budget.

*Interpretation.* This is the mechanism — not an assumption — by which a genuinely resource-loaded schedule
produces PV. If real productivity later differs from 25 m/day, that is a matter for re-planning and
re-baselining (KA 3.1, KA 6.1.2), not for silently re-spreading the same total.

**Mini-case — under-resourcing surfaces at the planning stage, not after the fact.** Suppose only half the
planned crew is mobilised, halving productivity to 12.5 m/day: `12.5 × 20 = 250 m/month`. Completing 2,000 m
at 250 m/month would take `2,000 / 250 = 8 months` — the crew cannot deliver WP3's scope within its
Months 2–5 window at all. A resource-loaded schedule surfaces this *before* execution begins; a simple
financial time-spread never would.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Never revisiting resource-loading assumptions after mobilisation actuals diverge from plan.
- Loading a generic/average rate rather than one specific to the activity's real crew composition.
- Resource-loading one work package in isolation without checking for resource contention against other,
  concurrent work packages (a resource-levelling issue, cross-reference KA 10.3).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted scheduling tools can propose resource loading and productivity rates from
historical project analogues, and can flag resource-contention conflicts across concurrent activities
automatically (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). They cannot know site-specific conditions — ground
conditions, access constraints, actual crew competency — that determine whether a historical rate genuinely
applies here; an unreviewed AI-proposed rate becomes an unreliable PV, propagating error into every EVM
calculation built on it.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2 (estimating methods, productivity rates); KA 3.3 (S-curve construction); KA
6.1.2 (PV); KA 6.4.1 (PMB); KA 10.3 (resource levelling).

### 6.4.3 The control-account / WBS–OBS intersection: the Responsibility Assignment Matrix

**Definition & purpose.** A **control account** is the point at which scope (the WBS), organisational
responsibility (the **OBS**, Organisation Breakdown Structure) and budget/schedule all meet at a single,
manageable level — the level at which EVM is actually planned and measured (cross-reference KA 5.3). The
**Responsibility Assignment Matrix (RAM)** — referenced in PMI's Practice Standard for Work Breakdown
Structures — is the standard tool that maps every WBS element against every OBS element, with control
accounts populated at the intersections where genuine management accountability exists.

**The underlying principle.** A WBS alone answers "what work"; an OBS alone answers "who." Neither alone is
sufficient for control: a control account exists so that one identifiable manager is accountable for *both*
the scope and the budget of a defined piece of work. Because a WBS element can require input from more than
one organisational unit, and one unit can execute across more than one WBS element, the RAM makes those
relationships explicit — so budget, schedule, measurement method and cost coding are each assigned exactly
once, with no gaps and no double-counting.

**Formula.** `Control account budget = Σ (work-package budget assigned to that WBS × OBS intersection)`;
`Σ (all control account budgets) = BAC`.

**Example 6.4.3 — The RAM for Pipeline Segment 4B, including a genuine cross-cutting control account.**

*Scenario.* Four OBS units execute the project: Engineering, Procurement, Construction, and Site Management/
PMO (which also holds quality assurance/HSE oversight). Most work packages map one-to-one to a department,
but $10,000 of WP3's $200,000 and $8,000 of WP4's $120,000 are ring-fenced QA/HSE effort, managed separately
by Site Management/PMO as its own control account, CA-06, rather than by Construction:

| WBS element | Engineering | Procurement | Construction | Site Mgmt/PMO | Row total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| WP1 Design & Engineering | CA-01 $80,000 | | | | $80,000 |
| WP2 Pipe & Valve Procurement | | CA-02 $150,000 | | | $150,000 |
| WP3 Trenching & Earthworks | | | CA-03 $190,000 | CA-06a $10,000 | $200,000 |
| WP4 Pipe-laying & Welding | | | CA-04 $112,000 | CA-06b $8,000 | $120,000 |
| WP5 Site Supervision | | | | CA-05 $50,000 | $50,000 |
| **Column total** | **$80,000** | **$150,000** | **$302,000** | **$68,000** | **$600,000** |

*Required.* (a) confirm the WP3 and WP4 rows reconcile to their known budgets; (b) confirm the columns sum
to BAC; (c) state CA-06's total budget and the WBS elements it draws from.

*Working.*
(a) WP3 row: `190,000 + 10,000 = 200,000` ✓. WP4 row: `112,000 + 8,000 = 120,000` ✓.
(b) `80,000 + 150,000 + 302,000 + 68,000 = 600,000 = BAC` ✓.
(c) `CA-06 = CA-06a + CA-06b = 10,000 + 8,000 = 18,000` (SAR 67,500), drawing from WP3 and WP4.

*Answer.* CA-06 (QA/HSE) = **$18,000**, spanning two WBS elements; Construction's total accountability =
**$302,000** (SAR 1,132,500) across two control accounts (CA-03, CA-04).

*Interpretation.* CA-06 is the clearest illustration of a genuine WBS × OBS intersection: it corresponds to
neither a single WBS row nor a single department's "natural" scope — it is a distinct piece of accountability
carved out of two WBS elements. CA-03/CA-04 and CA-06 must each apply their own EV measurement method
(6.1.4) and roll up correctly into aggregate project EV (6.1.5) without double-counting the $18,000 moved out
of Construction's totals.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Leaving a genuine cross-cutting scope (like QA/HSE effort) uncoded — sitting in neither the "natural" WBS
  owner's control account nor a properly established separate one, so it is invisible to both.
- Assuming a RAM is a one-off planning exercise rather than a live structure that must be updated when
  organisational responsibilities change mid-project.
- Reporting only the OBS roll-up (e.g. "Construction: $302,000") without also tracking each control account
  separately — the level EVM must actually be measured at (cross-reference KA 6.1.5).

**AI in this topic.** AI tools can auto-draft a RAM from a WBS and an organisation chart, and flag scope that
appears in the WBS but has no assigned OBS owner (a coding gap) (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). They
cannot judge whether a proposed assignment reflects genuine, sensible accountability (e.g. whether QA/HSE
really should sit with Site Management rather than Construction) — that is an organisational-design decision
for the professional.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.5, KA 5.3 (cost coding, control accounts); KA 6.1.5 (EV aggregation across the
WBS); KA 8.2 (planning, the baselines).

### 6.4.4 Measuring earned value from the schedule: deriving physical progress from the IMS

**Definition & purpose.** KA 6.1.4 defined the standard EV measurement methods (0/100, 50/50, % complete,
weighted milestones, LOE, apportioned effort) in full; this topic does not repeat that taxonomy. It covers
the schedule-specific question those methods raise once integration is taken seriously: how are the
weightings and progress inputs actually *derived from*, and *validated against*, the schedule and its
independent physical evidence — not merely asserted.

**The underlying principle.** EV measurement is only as reliable as the link between schedule-level progress
data and the control-account EV calculation. Two schedule-specific risks arise. First, weighted-milestone
splits should be derived from the schedule's own planned duration/effort profile for the activity, not
assigned arbitrarily — an arbitrary split is exactly how front-loading (KA 6.1.4) creeps in. Second, a
scheduler's own "percentage complete" field in a CPM tool, updated on the basis of elapsed *duration*, is not
the same thing as independently verified *physical* progress, and using the former as EV is a common,
material error.

**Example 6.4.4 — Deriving WP4's milestone weights from the schedule, and confirming them against KA 6.1.4.**

*Scenario.* WP4 (Pipe-laying & Welding, $120,000) uses weighted milestones: Prep, Lay, Weld, Test. The
resource-loaded schedule shows planned durations of Prep 2 days, Lay 8 days, Weld 6 days, Test 4 days
(20 days total), with effort assumed roughly proportional to duration for this activity.

*Required.* Milestone weights derived from the schedule, and comparison with those used in Example 6.1.4.

*Working.* Total duration = `2 + 8 + 6 + 4 = 20 days`. `Weight(Prep) = 2/20 = 10%`; `Weight(Lay) = 8/20 =
40%`; `Weight(Weld) = 6/20 = 30%`; `Weight(Test) = 4/20 = 20%`.

*Answer.* **10% / 40% / 30% / 20%** — identical to the weights used throughout KA 6.1.4's WP4 example.

*Interpretation.* This confirms those weights were never arbitrary — they are schedule-derived. Deriving
weights mechanically from planned duration/effort is the defensible way to set a weighted-milestone split,
directly countering the front-loading risk KA 6.1.4 flagged: weights cannot be inflated for an early
milestone without the schedule itself supporting a genuinely larger early-effort share.

**Mini-case — duration-elapsed "percent complete" versus verified quantity progress.** Per 6.4.2, WP3 is
planned over 80 working days (20 days/month × 4 months, Months 2–5) to excavate 2,000 m. By the Month-3 data
date, 2 of its 4 scheduled months have elapsed — 40 of 80 planned working days, i.e. 50% of scheduled
duration. If a scheduler's duration-elapsed "50% complete" were used directly as EV: `EV = 50% × 200,000 =
100,000`, against the correct, quantity-verified figure from Example 6.1.4 (900 m of 2,000 m excavated,
45%): `EV = 45% × 200,000 = 90,000`. The duration-based shortcut overstates EV by **$10,000** — small here in
isolation, but exactly the kind of proxy that, applied across many activities, can materially inflate a
project's reported EV with no corresponding physical progress.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a scheduling tool's own "percent complete" field (typically duration-elapsed) as EV without an
  independent physical/quantity check.
- Setting weighted-milestone splits by convention or habit rather than from the specific activity's planned
  duration/effort profile.
- Updating schedule dates/logic without a reconciled update to the EV measurement drawn from the same
  progress data and the same data date.

**AI in this topic.** Schedule-analytics tools can automatically compare a scheduler's reported percent
complete against independent physical/quantity data where such data exists, flagging divergences like the
WP3 example above (cross-reference KA 13.5). They cannot themselves verify which figure is correct — that
requires an independent physical measurement (a site walk, a quantity survey, a milestone sign-off); an AI
flag here is a prompt to re-verify, not a resolution.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.1.4 (EV measurement methods, defined in full, including the front-loading
pitfall); KA 10.1 (activity durations); KA 10.4 (schedule progress measurement).

### 6.4.5 Schedule-aware forecasting: critical path, float and the limits of a blended SPI

**Definition & purpose.** This topic bridges KA 6.3.4's EAC Method 3 (which discounts remaining budget by
`CPI × SPI`) with genuine schedule analysis. SPI is a value-weighted average across the *whole* time-phased
budget; it has no concept of a critical path (KA 10.2). A large-budget activity sitting on float can drag
SPI down just as much as an equally large activity on the critical path — even though only the latter
genuinely threatens completion, and only the latter plausibly justifies the paid-acceleration assumption
Method 3 makes.

**The underlying principle.** Before trusting a blended SPI as the basis for a schedule-adjusted EAC, a
controls professional must read it alongside a current network schedule update (float report): is the
shortfall concentrated in critical-path activities, or is it safely absorbed by float elsewhere?

**Example 6.4.5 — Blended SPI overstates schedule-driven cost pressure when float is ignored.**

*Scenario.* A control account comprises two work packages, each with PV to date of $60,000 and EV to date of
$45,000 (each individually: `SV = -15,000`, `SPI = 45,000/60,000 = 0.75`). The Month-5 schedule update
confirms WPx sits on the critical path (zero float); WPy has 8 weeks of total float and is not critical.
Combined, PV = $120,000, EV = $90,000, blended `SPI = 90,000/120,000 = 0.75` — identical to each package's
own SPI. Remaining budget for the account (BAC − EV) is $40,000, split $20,000 to each package.

*Required.* Contrast the schedule-driven ETC (Method 3 logic, `ETC = remaining / SPI`, isolating the
schedule effect by holding CPI = 1.00) applied blindly to the whole $40,000, against applying it only to the
genuinely at-risk (critical-path) portion.

*Working.*
Blanket approach: `ETC = 40,000 / 0.75 = 53,333`.
Schedule-aware approach: WPx (critical) `ETC = 20,000 / 0.75 = 26,667`; WPy (float-protected, no acceleration
assumed) `ETC = 20,000` unchanged. Total = `26,667 + 20,000 = 46,667`.

*Answer.* Blanket ETC = **$53,333**; schedule-aware ETC = **$46,667** — **$6,666** (about 12.5%) lower.

*Interpretation.* Applying Method 3's SPI-based pressure uniformly overstates the forecast because it wrongly
assumes WPy's float-protected slippage carries the same acceleration-cost risk as WPx's critical-path
slippage. This is exactly why KA 6.3.4 required "behind schedule *on or near the critical path*" as Method
3's precondition, not merely "SPI below 1.0."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Applying a project-wide blended SPI to Method 3 without checking whether the shortfall sits on critical or
  non-critical activities.
- Assuming float is a permanent buffer — other, unrelated slippage can consume the same float before this
  activity is reached, so the check must be current, not historical.
- Treating the critical path as fixed for the life of the project, when it can shift as work progresses.

**AI in this topic.** CPM engines within scheduling software can compute float and identify critical versus
non-critical behind-schedule activities far faster than a manual review, and can be paired with EVM data to
flag exactly this blended-versus-critical distinction (cross-reference KA 10.2, KA 13.5). They cannot judge
whether a logic relationship or duration estimate in the network is itself realistic — an incorrect logic
link can make a genuinely critical activity appear to have float, or vice versa; only the scheduler's review
of the network logic can catch that.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.3.4 (EAC Method 3); KA 10.2 (CPM, float, critical path); KA 6.4.6 (Earned
Schedule, the fuller time-based remedy).

### 6.4.6 Earned Schedule: ES, SV(t) and SPI(t) — a time-based complement to classic SV and SPI

**Definition & purpose.** Earned Schedule (ES) is a technique, developed by Walt Lipke as an extension to
classic EVM and now widely discussed in project controls literature — including within AACE TCM's coverage
of forecasting techniques — that re-expresses the schedule dimension of EVM performance in **time units**
rather than currency units. It answers a subtly different, and far more reliable, question near completion
than classic SV/SPI: at what point on the *original planned timeline* does the value earned so far
correspond to?

**The underlying principle.** KA 6.2.7 showed that classic SV and SPI are bounded by PV's ceiling at BAC —
as PV approaches BAC near the baselined finish, both are mechanically pulled toward zero/1.00 regardless of
true calendar lateness, because there is no more planned value left to fall behind on. Earned Schedule fixes
this structurally by comparing *time to time*, not value to value: it finds the point on the PV curve,
expressed as a time value, that corresponds to the current EV, then compares that time to the actual time
elapsed. Because actual time keeps advancing without limit after the baselined finish date, ES-based metrics
retain the ability to show genuine lateness for as long as the project actually takes to finish.

**Formula.**
`ES = C + (EV − PV(C)) / (PV(C+1) − PV(C))`
where *C* is the number of complete baseline periods whose cumulative PV is at or below current EV, PV(C)
and PV(C+1) are cumulative planned value ($) at the end of periods C and C+1, and EV is current cumulative
earned value ($). ES is expressed in the schedule's own time units (e.g. months), interpolated to a
fraction of a period.

`SV(t) = ES − AT` (months) — a signed, time-denominated schedule variance; negative is behind schedule.
`SPI(t) = ES / AT` (dimensionless) — a time-based schedule performance index, benchmarked at 1.00.

where **AT** (Actual Time) is the calendar time elapsed since project start, in the same units as ES, and
**PD** (Planned Duration) is the project's total baselined duration.

**Example 6.4.6 — ES, SV(t), SPI(t) and a schedule-adjusted forecast at Pipeline Segment 4B's Month-5 data
date (continuing KA 6.3).**

*Scenario.* Continuing KA 6.3's Month-5 data (BAC $600,000; PV $540,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250; CPI 0.80;
SPI 0.75) and the project's monthly cumulative PV table from KA 6.1: M1 $60,000; M2 $150,000; M3 $270,000;
M4 $420,000; M5 $540,000; M6 $600,000 (= BAC, PD = 6 months).

*Required.* (a) ES; (b) SV(t) and SPI(t); (c) IEAC(t) — the forecast total duration — under three
performance-factor (PF) assumptions mirroring KA 6.3's three EAC methods; (d) a schedule-adjusted cost EAC
using the **Schedule Cost Index**, `SCI(t) = CPI × SPI(t)`, in place of `CPI × SPI` in the Method 3 formula.

*Working.*
(a) EV $405,000 lies between PV(3) $270,000 and PV(4) $420,000, so C = 3:
`ES = 3 + (405,000 − 270,000)/(420,000 − 270,000) = 3 + 135,000/150,000 = 3.90 months`.
(b) `SV(t) = 3.90 − 5.00 = −1.10 months`. `SPI(t) = 3.90/5.00 = 0.78`.
(c) `IEAC(t) = AT + (PD − ES)/PF`; `PD − ES = 6.00 − 3.90 = 2.10 months`:
  - PF = 1 (remaining schedule at the planned rate, analogous to EAC Method 1): `IEAC(t) = 5.00 + 2.10/1 =
    7.10 months`.
  - PF = SPI(t) = 0.78 (current time-trend continues, analogous to Method 2): `IEAC(t) = 5.00 + 2.10/0.78 =
    7.69 months`.
  - PF = SCI(t) = CPI × SPI(t) = 0.80 × 0.78 = 0.624 (combined pressure continues, analogous to Method 3):
    `IEAC(t) = 5.00 + 2.10/0.624 = 8.37 months`.
(d) `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)/SCI(t) = 506,250 + 195,000/0.624 = 506,250 + 312,500 = 818,750`.

*Answer.* **ES = 3.90 months; SV(t) = −1.10 months; SPI(t) = 0.78.** Forecast completion ranges from **7.10**
to **8.37 months** against the planned 6 months. Schedule-adjusted EAC (SCI(t) basis) = **$818,750**
(SAR 3,070,313, rounded).

*Interpretation.* ES/SV(t)/SPI(t) express the same data as classic SV (−$135,000) and SPI (0.75) but in time
units a scheduler and a sponsor can both act on directly: earned progress corresponds to where the plan
expected the project to be 1.10 months ago. The three IEAC(t) figures mirror KA 6.3.7's EAC1 < EAC2 < EAC3
pattern exactly, for the same reason. And because SPI(t) = 0.78 is a fairer reading of true time performance
than classic SPI = 0.75 (which KA 6.2.7 showed grows structurally optimistic near completion), the SCI(t)-
based EAC of $818,750 is a more defensible schedule-adjusted cost forecast than KA 6.3.4's original
$831,250 — a $12,500 difference attributable entirely to using a time-based, rather than value-based,
schedule signal.

**Mini-case — revisiting KA 6.2.7's exact one-month-late trajectory: does ES catch what classic SPI missed?**
KA 6.2.7 walked through an alternative trajectory in which the project is consistently behind and finishes a
full calendar month late, in Month 7:

| AT (months) | PV cum ($) | EV cum ($) | ES (months) | SV(t) (months) | SPI(t) | Classic SPI (KA 6.2.7) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 420,000 | 378,000 | 3.72 | −0.28 | 0.93 | 0.90 |
| 5 | 540,000 | 486,000 | 4.55 | −0.45 | 0.91 | 0.90 |
| 6 (baselined finish) | 600,000 | 560,000 | 5.33 | −0.67 | 0.89 | 0.93 |
| 7 (actual finish) | 600,000 | 600,000 | **6.00** | **−1.00** | 0.86 | **1.00** |

*Working (Month 7 row).* EV = $600,000 = BAC = PV(6) exactly, so ES is capped at the full planned duration:
`ES = PD = 6.00`. `SV(t) = 6.00 − 7.00 = −1.00`. `SPI(t) = 6.00/7.00 = 0.86`.

*Interpretation.* At Month 7, SV(t) reads **exactly −1.00 month** — precisely the true, stated one-month
calendar slip — while classic SV (`600,000 − 600,000 = 0`) and classic SPI (1.00) both read as if the
project finished exactly on schedule. This is not a coincidence: at completion, ES always equals the planned
duration exactly, because the full baselined scope value has, by definition, now been earned, so SV(t)
always reduces to `PD − AT` — the genuine calendar slip in time units. This is the structural fix promised in
KA 6.2.7: Earned Schedule does not merely reduce the completion-point bias, it removes it, because time
(unlike value-based PV) is never capped.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Confusing ES (a point in *time*, e.g. "3.90 months") with EV (a point in *value*, e.g. "$405,000") — ES is
  read off the PV curve at the EV value, not equal to it.
- Interpolating ES against a stale, un-re-baselined PV curve (cross-reference KA 6.1.2) — ES is only as
  reliable as the curve it is read from.
- Treating SPI(t) as superior to classic SPI for every purpose — classic SV/SPI remain the simpler, standard
  metrics for routine mid-project reporting; ES's real value is specifically in the closing stages of a
  project, exactly where classic SPI's bias is worst.

**AI in this topic.** Because ES requires interpolation against the PV curve at every reporting period, it is
a natural candidate for automation — dashboards can compute ES, SV(t) and SPI(t) alongside classic SV/SPI at
every data date and flag a widening divergence between them as an early-warning signal, particularly late in
a project (cross-reference KA 13.5). The calculation is deterministic and low-risk to automate once the PV
curve and EV are correct. What remains a professional judgement is how much weight to place on IEAC(t)
versus a bottom-up forecast from the network schedule itself (the same top-down/bottom-up logic as KA 6.3.1,
applied here to time) — an ES-derived forecast is a statistical extrapolation, not a substitute for an
updated CPM network forecast.

### 6.4.7 Limitations of EVM as an integrated cost-schedule system

**Definition & purpose.** Having built a fully integrated cost-schedule EVM system across KA 6.1–6.4, this
closing topic states plainly what it still cannot do, so a controls professional deploys it as one tool
among several — never as a complete answer to project performance.

**The underlying principle.** EVM, even correctly integrated with the schedule and extended with Earned
Schedule, remains a quantitative system built from a limited set of inputs (budget, physical progress,
actual cost, time). Several structural limitations persist regardless of how well it is implemented:

- **LOE dampens the blended read.** By construction, LOE always shows EV = PV (KA 6.1.4), so a control
  account with a significant LOE share will show a blended SPI/SPI(t) that is more favourable than the true
  performance of its measurable portion — quantified below.
- **Aggregation masks offsetting positions.** A healthy project-level figure can hide materially adverse
  performance in one area offset by strong performance elsewhere (the Project Nova mini-case, KA 6.1.5).
- **Quality is invisible to EV.** EV credits the *budgeted value of physically completed work*; it says
  nothing about whether that work meets specification. A pipe section credited 100% complete under 0/100
  that later fails a hydrotest has already been "earned" — the failure surfaces later as added rework scope,
  not as a correction to the EV already booked.
- **EVM depends entirely on baseline integrity.** Every limitation and technique in KA 6.1–6.4 ultimately
  traces back to this: a poorly re-baselined PV corrupts every downstream number, however sophisticated the
  forecasting method applied to it.
- **EVM extrapolates the past; it does not anticipate the unknown.** Even Earned Schedule and predictive
  models are statistical extrapolations of historical PV/EV/AC data — they cannot foresee a not-yet-occurred
  risk event (cross-reference Domain 12) or substitute for a bottom-up, logic-driven re-estimate (KA 6.3.1).
- **Variable scope strains classical EVM.** A significant approved change requires a full re-baseline
  (KA 5.4, KA 6.1.2) or the numbers become meaningless; in adaptive/agile delivery, where scope is
  deliberately variable, classical EVM's assumptions break down further — addressed by AgileEVM in KA 9.5.

**Example 6.4.7 — Quantifying LOE dampening.**

*Scenario.* A control account has PV to date of $1,000,000, of which $200,000 (20%) is LOE scope and
$800,000 (80%) is measurable, quantity/milestone-based scope currently running at a true SPI of 0.70.

*Required.* The account's blended SPI.

*Working.* Measurable EV = `0.70 × 800,000 = 560,000`. LOE EV = `200,000` (= its own PV, by definition).
Blended EV = `560,000 + 200,000 = 760,000`. Blended `SPI = 760,000/1,000,000 = 0.76`.

*Answer.* Blended SPI = **0.76**, against the measurable portion's true SPI of **0.70**.

*Interpretation.* The LOE slice, always reading SPI = 1.00 by construction, pulls the blended figure upward
by 0.06 here — masking the true severity of the measurable shortfall. On an account with a larger LOE share
(common on PM/supervision-heavy contracts), the dampening is larger still. This persists even after full
schedule integration and even under Earned Schedule, since SPI(t) is built from the same blended EV/PV data
and inherits the identical effect.

**Mini-case — quality invisibility.** A control account credits a fabricated pipe spool 100% complete under
0/100 on delivery. Three weeks later it fails a hydrotest and must be re-fabricated. The EV already booked
is not reversed by EVM mechanics; the rework instead appears as new scope, cost and schedule impact against
the account going forward. EVM correctly measured *quantity of physical progress delivered on the date
measured* — it was never designed to measure, and cannot measure, whether that progress will hold up under
subsequent technical scrutiny. Quality assurance (KA 6.4.3's CA-06) is a necessary, separate control, not an
EVM substitute.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Abandoning EVM altogether because of a known limitation, rather than pairing it with the complementary
  control that addresses it (a bottom-up estimate, a QA process, a network schedule review, Earned Schedule).
- Treating a limitation as a footnote stated once, rather than an active check applied at every reporting
  period (e.g. checking a control account's LOE share, or confirming a scope change has been re-baselined).
- Assuming Earned Schedule "solves" EVM's limitations generally — it fixes specifically and only the
  completion-point bias in the schedule dimension; it does nothing for LOE dampening, aggregation masking,
  or quality invisibility.

**AI in this topic.** Predictive/ML-based EAC and early-warning models can incorporate leading indicators
beyond PV/EV/AC/schedule float — change-order volume, weather data, resourcing trends, historical portfolio
analogues — and can flag emerging risk earlier than a monthly EVM cycle (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5).
This genuinely extends what a formulaic EAC alone can see. It does not overcome the structural limitations
above: a model trained on blended EV/PV data inherits the same LOE-dampening and aggregation-masking effects
unless specifically built to disaggregate them, has no visibility into quality risk unless quality data is
explicitly fed to it, and remains fundamentally a pattern-extrapolation of history, not a judgement about a
genuinely novel risk. Every AI-generated forecast, however sophisticated, requires the professional's
disposal — interpreted in light of these known limitations, checked against bottom-up and network-schedule
evidence, never reported as a self-certifying number. **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Cross-references.** KA 6.1.4 (LOE); KA 6.1.5 (aggregation, Project Nova); KA 5.4, KA 6.1.2 (baseline
integrity, change control); KA 6.3.1 (bottom-up estimates); KA 6.4.6 (what Earned Schedule fixes, and does
not); KA 9.5 (AgileEVM); Domain 12 (risk management); KA 13.6 (AI governance).

### Key terms

- **Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB)** — the time-phased budget (PV) produced by integrating the
  approved scope, schedule and cost baselines.
- **Integrated Master Schedule (IMS)** — the approved project schedule from which PV is derived.
- **Resource-loaded / cost-loaded schedule** — a schedule with planned resources and productivity assigned
  to activities, priced to produce PV.
- **Organisation Breakdown Structure (OBS)** — the structure of organisational responsibility, mapped
  against the WBS to define control accounts.
- **Responsibility Assignment Matrix (RAM)** — the WBS × OBS matrix that identifies control accounts.
- **Earned Schedule (ES)** — the point on the PV curve, expressed in time units, that corresponds to current
  EV: `ES = C + (EV − PV(C))/(PV(C+1) − PV(C))`.
- **Actual Time (AT) / Planned Duration (PD)** — calendar time elapsed since project start, and the
  project's total baselined duration, both in the same time units as ES.
- **SV(t)** — `ES − AT`; time-denominated schedule variance.
- **SPI(t)** — `ES / AT`; time-based schedule performance index.
- **IEAC(t)** — the Independent Estimate at Completion (time): `AT + (PD − ES)/PF`, for a chosen performance
  factor (PF = 1, SPI(t), or SCI(t)).
- **Schedule Cost Index, SCI(t)** — `CPI × SPI(t)`; the time-based analogue of EAC Method 3's `CPI × SPI`.
- **LOE dampening** — the tendency of a control account's blended SPI/SPI(t) to read more favourably than
  its measurable portion's true performance, because LOE always shows SPI = 1.00.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 6.4.1 [topic 6.4.1, level: recall]**
The Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB) is best described as:
A. The schedule alone, independent of cost
B. **The time-phased budget resulting from integrating the approved scope, schedule and cost baselines** ✔
C. The actual cost incurred to date
D. A synonym for the Estimate at Completion
*Rationale:* the PMB is PV, built by integrating scope/schedule/cost (6.4.1). A ignores cost; C describes AC;
D confuses a baseline with a forecast (EAC).

**MCQ 6.4.2 [topic 6.4.6, level: application — numerical]**
A project's cumulative PV is $300,000 at Month 2 and $480,000 at Month 3. At the Month-4 data date,
cumulative EV is $360,000. What is the Earned Schedule (ES)?
A. 2.00
B. **2.33** ✔
C. 3.00
D. 4.00
*Rationale:* `ES = 2 + (360,000 − 300,000)/(480,000 − 300,000) = 2 + 60,000/180,000 = 2.33`. A takes the
lower bound only; C the upper bound; D confuses ES with AT.

**MCQ 6.4.3 [topic 6.4.6, level: application — numerical]**
Using the same data (ES = 2.33 months, AT = 4.00 months), what is SV(t)?
A. +1.67 months
B. **−1.67 months** ✔
C. −0.58 months
D. $120,000
*Rationale:* `SV(t) = ES − AT = 2.33 − 4.00 = −1.67` (adverse). A reverses the sign; C mistakenly divides
instead of subtracting; D is a currency figure, not a time-based SV(t).

**MCQ 6.4.4 [topic 6.4.6, level: application — numerical]**
CPI is 0.90 and SPI(t) is 0.85. What is the Schedule Cost Index, SCI(t)?
A. 1.75
B. **0.765** ✔
C. 0.05
D. 0.94
*Rationale:* `SCI(t) = CPI × SPI(t) = 0.90 × 0.85 = 0.765`. A sums instead of multiplying; C subtracts; D
averages the two — none is the defined relationship.

**MCQ 6.4.5 [topic 6.4.6, level: analysis]**
Why does SV(t) at project completion always equal (planned duration − actual time), correctly capturing the
true calendar slip, whereas classic SV does not?
A. Because AC is not used in either calculation
B. **Because ES equals the planned duration exactly once EV reaches BAC, while PV — which classic SV depends
   on — is capped at BAC and stops moving at the baselined finish** ✔
C. Because SPI and SPI(t) are always identical at completion
D. Because Level of Effort work packages dominate the calculation at completion
*Rationale:* as shown in the 6.4.6 mini-case, ES = PD exactly when EV = BAC, so SV(t) = PD − AT; classic SV
= EV − PV = 0 at that point because both terms are capped at BAC together. C and D describe mechanisms that
do not occur.

**MCQ 6.4.6 [topic 6.4.7, level: analysis]**
A control account is 20% Level of Effort (LOE) and 80% measurable, quantity-based scope. The measurable
scope is running at a true SPI of 0.70. What does EVM theory predict about the account's blended SPI?
A. It will equal 0.70 exactly, since LOE has no effect on SPI
B. **It will read higher than 0.70, because the LOE portion always shows SPI = 1.00 by construction, pulling
   the blended figure toward 1.00** ✔
C. It cannot be calculated without knowing AC
D. It will read lower than 0.70, because LOE is always adverse
*Rationale:* as in Example 6.4.7 (blended SPI 0.76 against a true measurable SPI of 0.70), LOE mechanically
dampens the true shortfall. A ignores the effect; D reverses its direction; C is irrelevant — SPI does not
require AC.

### Self-check questions

1. Using the Pipeline Segment 4B PV table (M4 cumulative PV $420,000; M5 cumulative PV $540,000) and a
   hypothetical EV of $500,000 at the Month-5 data date, calculate ES, SV(t) and SPI(t).
2. Explain, in your own words, why classic SV and SPI become unreliable indicators of true calendar lateness
   near project completion, and briefly explain how Earned Schedule's SV(t) avoids the same weakness.
3. A control account has PV to date of $500,000, of which $100,000 (20%) is Level of Effort. The measurable
   80% ($400,000 PV) is running at a true SPI of 0.75. Calculate the account's blended SPI and explain why it
   differs from 0.75.

**Answers**

1. EV $500,000 lies between PV(4) $420,000 and PV(5) $540,000: `ES = 4 + (500,000 − 420,000)/(540,000 −
   420,000) = 4 + 80,000/120,000 = 4.67`. `SV(t) = 4.67 − 5.00 = −0.33` (adverse). `SPI(t) = 4.67/5.00 =
   0.93`.
2. Classic SV and SPI compare EV against PV, and PV is capped at BAC — as the data date approaches the
   baselined finish, PV stops growing while EV can still close the remaining gap, pulling SV toward zero and
   SPI toward 1.00 regardless of genuine calendar lateness (KA 6.2.7). Earned Schedule instead compares time
   to time: ES equals the planned duration exactly once EV reaches BAC, and actual time (AT) is never capped,
   so SV(t) = ES − AT continues to reflect true calendar slip for as long as the project actually takes to
   finish.
3. Measurable EV = `0.75 × 400,000 = 300,000`. LOE EV = `100,000` (= its own PV). Blended EV = `400,000`.
   Blended `SPI = 400,000/500,000 = 0.80`. It reads 0.05 higher than the measurable portion's true 0.75
   because the LOE slice always shows SPI = 1.00 by construction, pulling the blend upward.
