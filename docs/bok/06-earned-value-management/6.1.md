# Domain 6 — Earned Value Management & Forecasting (EVM/EAC)

## KA 6.1 — EVM fundamentals

Earned Value Management (EVM) is the discipline that turns three separate streams of project data — the
approved budget, the physical progress of the work, and the money actually spent — into a single, objective
measurement system that integrates **scope, schedule and cost**. Without EVM, a project controls
professional typically has a budget-versus-actual cost report (which says nothing about how much work was
really done for that spend) and a schedule status report (which says nothing about cost). EVM closes that
gap by defining three foundational measures — Planned Value (PV), Earned Value (EV) and Actual Cost (AC) —
and a consistent set of rules for calculating EV from physical progress. This KA is the foundation for the
rest of Domain 6: KA 6.2 builds the variances and indices (CV, SV, CPI, SPI, TCPI) from PV/EV/AC; KA 6.3
uses them to forecast the Estimate at Completion (EAC); KA 6.4 integrates EVM with the schedule and
introduces Earned Schedule. Everything downstream depends on PV, EV and AC being defined, measured and
reported correctly — which is why this KA is flagged flagship and treated in full depth.

### 6.1.1 The purpose of EVM and its three foundational measures

**Definition & purpose.** Earned Value Management is an internationally recognised project performance
measurement methodology (described in the *Practice Standard for Earned Value Management* referenced by
PMI's PMBOK Guide, and in AACE International's Total Cost Management (TCM) Framework as a core performance
measurement and forecasting technique) that measures project performance and progress objectively by
combining measurements of scope, schedule and cost in a single integrated system. It rests on three
measures, each expressed in the *same currency* (never in percentages alone, though percentages are derived
from them):

- **Planned Value (PV)**, also called **Budgeted Cost of Work Scheduled (BCWS)** — the authorised,
  time-phased budget for the work scheduled to be accomplished, up to a given point in time.
- **Earned Value (EV)**, also called **Budgeted Cost of Work Performed (BCWP)** — the budgeted value of the
  work that has actually been physically completed, up to that same point in time.
- **Actual Cost (AC)**, also called **Actual Cost of Work Performed (ACWP)** — the actual cost incurred to
  accomplish the work that has been performed, up to that same point in time.

**The underlying principle.** The reason EVM exists is that cost alone is not a performance measure. Cost
only tells you what has been *spent*; it says nothing about how much of the scope that spend has actually
delivered. EV is the missing link — it re-expresses physical progress in the same monetary units as the
budget and the actual cost, so that all three numbers become directly comparable. Once PV, EV and AC exist
at the same point in time (the **data date**, also called the **status date** or **as-of date**), a
controls professional can ask two independent questions: "are we earning value at the rate we planned?"
(schedule performance, EV vs PV) and "are we spending what that earned value should have cost?" (cost
performance, EV vs AC). KA 6.2 formalises these comparisons as the Schedule Variance (SV), Cost Variance
(CV), SPI and CPI; this KA's job is only to make sure PV, EV and AC are each correctly defined, sourced and
measured before those calculations are attempted.

**Formula(e).** At this stage the "formulae" are definitions of cumulative sums rather than variances:

`PV(t) = Σ (budgeted cost of work scheduled to be completed, period 1 → t)`
`EV(t) = Σ (budgeted cost of work actually completed, period 1 → t)`
`AC(t) = Σ (actual cost incurred for work performed, period 1 → t)`

where *t* is the reporting/data date and all three are expressed cumulatively in the project's reporting
currency (USD, with an SAR illustration where useful) unless a period-only ("current period") figure is
explicitly stated.

**Illustrative mini-case — why cost alone is not enough.** Two contractors each have a $500,000, six-month
work package and each has spent exactly $250,000 (50% of budget) by the mid-point. A cost-only report would
show both "on budget." But Contractor A has physically completed 50% of the scope (EV = $250,000): on
budget and on schedule. Contractor B has physically completed only 30% of the scope (EV = $150,000): on
budget in cash-spent terms, but seriously behind on delivered scope, and — because EV ($150,000) is below AC
($250,000) — actually **over budget for the work actually done**. The spend report alone cannot distinguish
A from B; PV, EV and AC together can. This is the core justification for EVM and is worked in full once SV
and CV are formally introduced in KA 6.2.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating "spending to budget" (AC ≈ PV) as evidence of good progress — it is evidence of nothing about

- Building EVM on a baseline (PV) that was never properly resource-loaded and time-phased, or that has not

  that follows (cross-reference KA 3.1, KA 3.3).
- Forgetting that PV, EV and AC must all be measured **to the same data date** and against **the same scope
  baseline (BAC)** — comparing PV at Month 3 with AC reported a week later, or with EV measured against an
  un-updated BAC after a scope change, produces numbers that look precise but are not comparable.

**AI in this topic.** Time-series anomaly-detection tools can continuously monitor the PV/EV/AC lines and
flag an unusual divergence (a sudden widening gap) earlier than a monthly reporting cycle would surface it —
useful as an early-warning trigger (cross-reference KA 13.5). AI cannot, however, judge *why* the lines have
diverged, whether the underlying data feeding PV, EV or AC is itself correct, or whether a genuine change
in scope should have moved the baseline first. The professional must confirm data integrity and root cause
before acting on any AI-generated flag — **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

### 6.1.2 Planned Value (PV / BCWS)

**Definition & purpose.** PV (BCWS) is the authorised, time-phased budget assigned to scheduled work,
expressed cumulatively as of a given date. It answers the question "how much work, valued at budget rates,
was the project supposed to have accomplished by now?" PV is a plan artefact: it is fixed once the baseline
is approved and does not change with actual performance — it only changes when a **baseline change** (an
approved scope, budget or schedule change) is formally incorporated.

**The underlying principle.** PV exists because a single total budget figure (BAC) says nothing about
*when* that money should be earned. Spreading BAC across the schedule — building the time-phased budget, or
"S-curve" — is what makes a mid-project comparison possible at all. The full methodology for constructing
that time-phased budget from a resource-loaded schedule (including the typical S-shaped, non-linear spend
profile of most projects) is covered in depth in KA 3.3; this topic focuses on what PV means within EVM and
how it is read and used once built.

**Formula.**
`PV(t) = Σ (budgeted cost per period, period 1 → t)`, drawn from the approved, time-phased cost baseline.

**Example 6.1.2 — Reading cumulative PV from the approved baseline.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B has an approved BAC of $600,000 (SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative
SAR 3.75 = USD 1) and an approved six-month, time-phased budget (built per KA 3.3 methodology) as follows:

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Budgeted cost for the period ($) | 60,000 | 90,000 | 120,000 | 150,000 | 120,000 | 60,000 | 600,000 |

*Required.* The cumulative PV at the end of Month 3.

*Working.*
`PV(3) = 60,000 + 90,000 + 120,000 = 270,000`

*Answer.* **PV(3) = $270,000** (SAR 1,012,500 at the illustrative rate) — i.e. by the end of Month 3, the
project was scheduled to have earned $270,000 of value, 45.0% of the $600,000 BAC (270,000 / 600,000).

*Interpretation.* $270,000 is the yardstick against which EV will be compared in KA 6.2 to determine whether
the project is ahead of, on, or behind schedule in earned-value terms. It says nothing yet about what was
actually achieved or spent — those are EV (6.1.4–6.1.5) and AC (6.1.3).

**Mini-case — why a stale PV baseline misleads.** Suppose, after Month 2, an approved variation added a new
$60,000 work package to be executed in Months 4–5, but the cost baseline was never updated (re-baselined) to
reflect it. At Month 3, PV would still read $270,000 against a BAC that is now understated by $60,000; every
subsequent SV and CV calculation (KA 6.2) would be computed against the wrong yardstick, making the project
look better or worse than it really is depending on how the new scope is being executed. This is why change
control (KA 5.4, KA 8.4) must formally re-baseline PV before EVM continues to be relied upon — comparing
performance to an out-of-date plan is a common and serious EVM error, distinct from re-forecasting (KA 6.3),
which adjusts the *forecast*, not the *baseline*.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Confusing PV with the *schedule* itself (dates, logic, float) rather than the *budgeted cost* of the
  scheduled work — PV is a cost curve, not a bar chart.
- Spreading budget on a straight-line (linear) basis when the underlying work is naturally S-shaped —
  overstating early-period PV and understating late-period PV, which distorts early SV readings.
- Failing to re-baseline PV after an approved change, so EV and AC are compared against a plan that no
  longer reflects the authorised scope.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted scheduling/cost-integration tools can automatically time-phase a budget
across a resource- or cost-loaded schedule far faster than a manual spread, and can propose an S-curve shape
from historical analogues (cross-reference KA 13.5). The assumptions embedded in any such automated spread —
resource calendars, productivity curves, analogue project selection — must be reviewed and approved by the
professional before the result is baselined; an unreviewed automated PV curve becomes an unreliable
foundation for every EVM calculation built on it.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 (budget baseline, contingency & management reserve); KA 3.3 (building the
time-phased budget/S-curve in depth — do not duplicate here); KA 5.4 (change control and cost impact);
KA 6.2 (SV); KA 8.4 (baseline control within Monitoring & Controlling).

### 6.1.3 Actual Cost (AC / ACWP)

**Definition & purpose.** AC (ACWP) is the actual cost incurred in accomplishing the work performed to
date, expressed cumulatively as of the data date, drawn from the organisation's cost ledger/ERP system
(cross-reference KA 1.5, KA 5.2). Unlike PV and EV, which are both *budgeted* value expressed at standard/
budget rates, AC is the only one of the three measures that reflects what actually happened financially —
real invoices, real payroll postings, real committed costs recognised as incurred.

**The underlying principle.** AC must be captured on a **consistent, matching basis** with PV and EV — the
same scope, the same cost elements, the same data date — or the comparison in KA 6.2 (CV = EV − AC) becomes
an apples-to-oranges exercise. In accrual terms (cross-reference KA 1.3), AC should reflect costs *incurred*
for work performed, not merely cash *paid*: work completed but not yet invoiced should still be accrued into
AC, otherwise the project's true cost position is understated.

**Formula.** `AC(t) = Σ (actual costs posted/accrued against the control account, period 1 → t)`, normally
built up from cost elements such as labour, materials, equipment and subcontract charges.

**Example 6.1.3 — Building AC from the cost ledger.**

*Scenario.* At the Month-3 data date, the cost ledger extract for Pipeline Segment 4B shows the following
postings against the project's cost elements:

| Cost element | Amount ($) |
|---|---|
| Labour | 140,000 |
| Materials | 95,000 |
| Equipment | 30,000 |
| Subcontract | 15,000 |
| **Total AC** | **280,000** |

*Required.* Cumulative AC at Month 3.

*Working.* `AC(3) = 140,000 + 95,000 + 30,000 + 15,000 = 280,000`

*Answer.* **AC(3) = $280,000** (SAR 1,050,000 at the illustrative rate) — 46.7% of BAC (280,000 / 600,000).

*Interpretation.* $280,000 is what the project has actually cost so far, on a like-for-like time basis with
PV ($270,000) and EV ($330,000, derived in 6.1.4). AC by itself only says the project has spent close to
what it planned to spend by now (270,000 vs 280,000) — it cannot say whether that spend bought 45% of the
scope, 55%, or something else; only EV answers that.

**Mini-case — the accrual-lag pitfall.** At the same Month-3 data date, the subcontractor has physically
completed and had verified an additional $25,000 of welding work that has not yet been invoiced (a typical
3–4 week invoice lag). The ledger-only AC of $280,000 therefore understates the true, accrual-adjusted cost:

`Accrual-adjusted AC(3) = 280,000 + 25,000 = 305,000`

Using the un-accrued figure would make the project's cost position look $25,000 better than it really is
once compared with EV in KA 6.2 (CV would read +$50,000 rather than the true +$25,000). Accruing costs for
work verified as performed, even where not yet invoiced, is standard practice (cross-reference KA 1.3, KA
1.4) and essential for AC to be a reliable EVM input.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using a purely cash-paid basis for AC instead of an accrual basis, so recently completed but unbilled work
  is invisible in the cost position.
- Including committed cost (an issued but unconsumed purchase order) in AC — committed cost is a forward
  exposure, not yet an actual cost incurred, and belongs in commitment reporting (KA 5.2), not AC.
- Reconciliation breaks between the cost ledger's cost-element view (labour/materials/equipment/subcontract)
  and the control account/work-package view that EV is measured against (KA 1.5, KA 5.3) — without a clean
  cost-coding structure, AC cannot be reliably matched to the EV it is meant to be compared with.

**AI in this topic.** Robotic process automation (RPA) and AI-assisted reconciliation tools can extract cost
postings from an ERP/ledger, map them to control accounts via cost-coding rules, and flag postings that do
not reconcile or that look anomalous relative to historical run-rates (cross-reference KA 13.5). This speeds
up AC preparation materially, but it does not verify that the underlying ledger data is itself correctly
coded, complete, or accrued — an automated extraction of badly coded source data simply produces a fast,
wrong AC. The professional remains accountable for confirming AC is complete, accrual-consistent and
correctly mapped before it is used in KA 6.2 calculations.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.3 (accrual accounting & matching); KA 1.4 (provisions & accruals); KA 1.5 (cost
coding for projects); KA 5.2 (the cost control cycle: commitment → accrual → actual); KA 6.2 (CV).

### 6.1.4 Earned value measurement methods

**Definition & purpose.** A measurement method is the pre-agreed rule for converting the *physical* progress
of a work package into its EV in monetary terms. Because "percentage physical progress" cannot always be
observed precisely or objectively, EVM defines a small family of standard methods, each suited to a
different type of work, and each fixed *in advance* in the project's EVM/control-account plan — not chosen
retrospectively to make the numbers look better. The commonly used methods are:

- **0/100** — 0% credit until the activity is fully complete, then 100%. Best for short-duration, discrete
  activities where partial credit is not meaningful (e.g. issuing a single design deliverable).
- **50/50** (or **20/80**, or other fixed splits) — a fixed percentage credited at the *start* of the
  activity, the remainder at *completion*. Best for short-to-medium activities with two clear states
  (e.g. procurement: order placed / delivered).
- **Percentage complete (units of measure)** — EV credited in direct proportion to a physically measurable
  quantity completed (e.g. cubic metres excavated, metres of pipe welded, drawings issued out of a known
  total). Best for repetitive, quantifiable work.
- **Weighted milestones** — the activity is broken into a small number of milestones, each pre-assigned a
  percentage weight (summing to 100%); EV is credited as each milestone is achieved. Best for a single
  activity with several observable, sequential stages that are not individually a full unit of measure
  (e.g. fabricate/erect/test).
- **Level of Effort (LOE)** — EV is credited strictly in proportion to elapsed time against the planned
  duration, regardless of physical output. Reserved for support-type work with no discrete, measurable
  deliverable (e.g. site supervision, project management, HSE oversight). LOE is the one method where, by
  construction, EV always equals PV for that package — it can never itself show a schedule variance.
- **Apportioned effort** — a variant of LOE used for supporting activities (e.g. quality inspection) whose
  effort is planned to move in direct proportion to a related, discrete "base" activity, rather than to
  calendar time.

*Quick-reference — choosing a method:*

| Method | Credits value | Best suited to | Weakness if misapplied |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0/100 | 100% only on completion | Short, discrete, all-or-nothing deliverables | Understates progress on long activities; sudden late jump |
| 50/50 / 20/80 | Fixed split at start/finish | Short-to-medium, two-state activities (e.g. ordered/delivered) | Can over- or under-credit if the true midpoint effort differs from the fixed split |
| % complete (units) | Proportional to measured quantity | Repetitive, quantifiable work (m³, m, drawings) | Requires a reliable, auditable quantity count |
| Weighted milestones | Proportional to pre-weighted stages achieved | Single activities with several observable stages | Vulnerable to front-loading if weights don't match real effort |
| LOE | Proportional to elapsed time | Support work with no discrete output (supervision, PM) | Never shows a schedule variance for that package — can mask real slippage elsewhere in the account |
| Apportioned effort | Proportional to a related "base" activity's progress | Supporting effort tied to a measurable base activity | Inherits any measurement error in the base activity |

**The underlying principle.** The method chosen determines *when*, within an activity's life, its budget
value is recognised as earned. Because methods differ in how conservative or generous they are about partial
credit, the same physical reality can produce very different reported EV depending only on which method was
assigned — which is precisely why the method must be fixed in the control-account plan before work starts,
not selected after the fact.

**Example 6.1.4 — Computing EV under each method: Pipeline Segment 4B at Month 3.**

*Scenario.* The Pipeline Segment 4B control account is broken into five work packages (WPs), summing to the
full $600,000 BAC, each assigned a different measurement method appropriate to its nature of work:

| WP | Description | Budget ($) | Method | Status at Month 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WP1 | Design & engineering | 80,000 | 0/100 | Deliverable issued — complete |
| WP2 | Pipe & valve procurement | 150,000 | 50/50 | Purchase order placed (start credit only) |
| WP3 | Trenching & earthworks | 200,000 (2,000 m at $100/m) | % complete (units) | 900 m excavated |
| WP4 | Pipe-laying & welding | 120,000 | Weighted milestones: Prep 10% ($12,000), Lay 40% ($48,000), Weld 30% ($36,000), Test 20% ($24,000) | Prep and Lay complete; Weld and Test not started |
| WP5 | Site supervision (PM/QA/HSE) | 50,000 | LOE | 3 of 6 planned months elapsed |
| **Total** | | **600,000** | | |

*Required.* EV for each WP, and the total EV for the control account.

*Working.*
- WP1 (0/100): complete → `EV = 100% × 80,000 = 80,000`
- WP2 (50/50): started, not complete → `EV = 50% × 150,000 = 75,000`
- WP3 (% complete, units): `900 m / 2,000 m = 45%` → `EV = 45% × 200,000 = 90,000`
- WP4 (weighted milestones): Prep (10%) + Lay (40%) achieved = 50% → `EV = 50% × 120,000 = 60,000`
  (equivalently `12,000 + 48,000 = 60,000`)
- WP5 (LOE): `3 / 6 months = 50%` → `EV = 50% × 50,000 = 25,000`

`Total EV = 80,000 + 75,000 + 90,000 + 60,000 + 25,000 = 330,000`

*Answer.* **EV(3) = $330,000** (SAR 1,237,500 at the illustrative rate) — 55.0% of BAC.

*Interpretation.* Five different, method-appropriate treatments of physical progress roll up into one
control-account EV figure, in the same monetary terms as PV ($270,000, Example 6.1.2) and AC ($280,000,
Example 6.1.3). This is the number carried forward into 6.1.5 (aggregation) and 6.1.6 (the full EVM
data set), and into KA 6.2's SV and CV.

**Mini-case — the 0/100 vs 50/50 distortion.** A separate control account contains ten identical, discrete
activities, each budgeted at $10,000 (total budget $100,000). By the data date, all ten have started but
none is finished.

Under 0/100: `EV = 10 × (0% × 10,000) = $0` — 0% of the account reads as earned, despite real work under way
on every activity.

Under 50/50: `EV = 10 × (50% × 10,000) = $50,000` — 50% reads as earned, for the identical physical reality.

*Interpretation.* Neither answer is "wrong" — each is the correct, mechanical output of the method it uses —
but the two methods tell very different stories about the *same* physical progress. This is why method
selection is a professional judgement made once, in the control-account plan, and why mixing or changing
methods opportunistically during execution (rather than because the nature of the work genuinely changed)
is a form of data manipulation that undermines the credibility of the whole EVM system.

**Animation 6.1.4 — "Earning value under different methods" [digital-only].** A single generic activity is
shown with a physical-progress slider running 0% → 100%. Four small bars beneath the slider — labelled
0/100, 50/50, % complete and LOE — update live as the slider moves, each showing its own EV reading for the
identical physical progress. At 30% physical progress, 0/100 shows $0, 50/50 shows a flat 50% "start"
credit, % complete tracks the slider exactly at 30%, and LOE shows whatever % of planned duration has

choice — make it before you start, not to fit the answer you want."

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Front-loading**: weighting an early milestone disproportionately to its actual effort (e.g. crediting
  50% for "mobilisation") so EV runs artificially ahead of true physical progress, masking problems that
  surface later.
- Using LOE for work that is genuinely discrete and measurable — LOE can never generate a schedule variance
  for that package (EV is defined to equal PV), hiding real schedule slippage within the control account.
- Applying 0/100 to long-duration, high-value activities, which understates progress for long stretches and
  then produces a sudden, large jump in EV on completion — poor for early warning.
- Changing an activity's assigned method mid-stream without a documented, work-nature-based justification.

**AI in this topic.** A GenAI tool can suggest a plausible measurement method for a new work package by
pattern-matching against historical control-account plans (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5), which can
speed up EVM-plan drafting. It cannot verify the activity's *actual* physical percentage complete — that
requires site inspection, a quantity survey, or a verified milestone sign-off — and it has no way to detect
that a proposed weighting is a front-loading risk unless a human reviews the split against real effort
proportions. Method selection and % complete assertions remain professional judgements requiring sign-off;
an AI suggestion here is a draft, never a substitute for verification.

### 6.1.5 Earned Value (EV / BCWP): aggregation across the WBS

**Definition & purpose.** A project's total EV at a given data date is the sum of the EV of every control
account/work package in the Work Breakdown Structure (WBS), each measured by its own appropriate method
(6.1.4). Aggregation is what turns work-package-level measurement into a single, project-level (or
programme-level) performance figure that can be compared with total PV and total AC.

**The underlying principle.** Because EV is always expressed in the same monetary units regardless of the
method used to derive it at the work-package level, it aggregates cleanly by simple addition — unlike, say,
percentage-complete figures from packages of very different sizes, which cannot be averaged without
weighting. This is one of EVM's most useful properties: dissimilar work (design, procurement, earthworks,
construction, supervision) can be measured by whatever method fits it, and still roll up into one number.

**Formula.** `EV(project) = Σ EV(work package i)`, for all i in the WBS/control-account structure, at the
same data date.

**Example 6.1.5 — Rolling up EV across the WBS.**

*Scenario.* Using the five work packages already measured in Example 6.1.4:

| WP | EV ($) |
|---|---|
| WP1 | 80,000 |
| WP2 | 75,000 |
| WP3 | 90,000 |
| WP4 | 60,000 |
| WP5 | 25,000 |

*Required.* Total project EV at Month 3.

*Working.* `EV(project) = 80,000 + 75,000 + 90,000 + 60,000 + 25,000 = 330,000`

*Answer.* **EV(project) = $330,000**, matching Example 6.1.4's total exactly, because the WP-level and
project-level figures are the same aggregation — the total row in that table.

*Interpretation.* Aggregation adds no new arithmetic here beyond the sum already performed; its value is
conceptual — confirming that mixed measurement methods at the work-package level do not prevent a clean,
single, comparable EV figure at the project level.

**Mini-case — programme-level roll-up ("Project Nova").** A programme comprises three active sub-projects
(each itself an aggregation of many control accounts) plus one not-yet-started sub-project, at a common data
date:

| Sub-project | PV ($) | EV ($) | AC ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sub-A | 100,000 | 100,000 | 95,000 |
| Sub-B | 250,000 | 220,000 | 240,000 |
| Sub-C | 450,000 | 380,000 | 400,000 |
| Sub-D (not started) | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| **Programme total** | **800,000** | **700,000** | **735,000** |

with a programme BAC of $1,000,000 (Sub-A + B + C budgets already reflected in their PV-to-date, plus
Sub-D's $200,000 budget not yet started). Aggregate PV, EV and AC are each the simple sum down the column
(100,000+250,000+450,000+0 = 800,000; 100,000+220,000+380,000+0 = 700,000; 95,000+240,000+400,000+0 =
735,000). At the programme level, EV ($700,000) is below PV ($800,000) — behind schedule in aggregate — even
though Sub-A individually is exactly on schedule; the shortfall is concentrated in Sub-B and Sub-C. This
illustrates why an aggregated EVM figure is a starting point for investigation, not the end of it — the
professional must be able to drill back down to the sub-project and control-account level that is driving
the aggregate result (full variance/index interpretation in KA 6.2).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Omitting a control account from the roll-up (e.g. a newly added scope package not yet loaded into the EVM
  system) — the aggregate silently understates true project size.
- Aggregating figures captured at different data dates or in different currencies without normalising first.
- Treating an on-target aggregate figure as proof that every component is on target — an aggregate can mask
  offsetting good and bad performance in different areas (as in the Project Nova mini-case).

**AI in this topic.** Automation can aggregate EV, PV and AC across hundreds of control accounts and produce
a programme-level roll-up in seconds, and can be configured to flag which sub-project(s) are driving an
aggregate shortfall (cross-reference KA 4.4, KA 13.5) — a genuine speed and consistency benefit at scale.
The risk is that an error in a single control account's underlying data (wrong method, stale baseline,
un-accrued cost) propagates silently through an automated roll-up and is harder to spot once buried in a
large aggregate; the professional should retain the ability to drill down to source data and should never
treat an automated roll-up as self-verifying.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.5, KA 5.3 (WBS/CBS and control accounts); KA 4.3, KA 4.4 (management reporting,
dashboards); KA 6.2 (variances/indices at project and account level); KA 6.4 (limitations of EVM at
aggregate levels).

### 6.1.6 Bringing it together: the EVM data set at a status date

**Definition & purpose.** The practical output of everything in this KA is a single, small **EVM summary
table** at each reporting/data date: BAC, PV, EV and AC, for the project (and, as needed, for each control
account beneath it). This table is the standard input to every calculation in KA 6.2 (CV, SV, CPI, SPI) and
KA 6.3 (EAC, ETC, VAC) — this KA's job ends at producing it correctly; interpreting it in depth belongs to
those chapters.

**The underlying principle.** PV, EV and AC are each sourced differently — PV from the approved, time-phased
baseline (6.1.2); EV from physical progress converted via a pre-agreed measurement method (6.1.4–6.1.5); AC
from the accrual-adjusted cost ledger (6.1.3) — but all three must describe **the same scope, at the same
data date**, for the resulting comparisons to be valid. Producing the table is therefore as much a data
governance exercise (alignment of data dates, scope, and accrual basis) as it is an arithmetic one.

**Example 6.1.6 — The Month-3 EVM summary table for Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* Bringing together Examples 6.1.2, 6.1.3 and 6.1.4/6.1.5:

| Measure | Value ($) | % of BAC |
|---|---|---|
| BAC | 600,000 | 100.0% |
| PV (6.1.2) | 270,000 | 45.0% |
| EV (6.1.4/6.1.5) | 330,000 | 55.0% |
| AC (6.1.3) | 280,000 | 46.7% |

*Required.* A preview of the cost and schedule position (full formal treatment — CV, SV, CPI, SPI — is
KA 6.2; only the underlying comparison is shown here as a check that the data set is internally consistent
and usable).

*Working.*
`EV − AC = 330,000 − 280,000 = +50,000` (EV exceeds AC: more value earned than cash spent to date)
`EV − PV = 330,000 − 270,000 = +60,000` (EV exceeds PV: more value earned than was scheduled to date)

*Answer.* On this data set, Pipeline Segment 4B appears **ahead of schedule and under budget** in
earned-value terms as of Month 3. Using the accrual-adjusted AC from the 6.1.3 mini-case ($305,000 instead
of $280,000), the cost-side gap narrows to `330,000 − 305,000 = +25,000` — still favourable, but a materially
different number, underscoring why AC must be accrual-complete before this table is relied upon.

*Interpretation.* This table — not a narrative, not a single percentage — is the correct EVM output at a
status date. KA 6.2 turns the two gaps computed here into formally named, indexed metrics (CV, SV, CPI,
SPI) and interprets what "favourable" actually means for forecasting and decision-making.

**Fig 6.1.6 — EVM summary: Pipeline Segment 4B, Month-3 status date.**
Chart type: a four-bar comparison chart (BAC, PV, EV, AC), USD on the vertical axis, brand blue `#1D4ED8`
for EV, neutral tones for the other three bars, with the two preview gaps (EV−AC, EV−PV) annotated as small
bracketed callouts between the relevant bars, each labelled "see KA 6.2 for CV/SV."

*Underlying data:* BAC $600,000; PV $270,000; EV $330,000; AC $280,000 (footnote: accrual-adjusted AC
$305,000).

**Mini-case — how sensitive is this table to method choice?** Suppose, instead of the mixed, work-nature-
appropriate methods used in Example 6.1.4, the entire control account had been (wrongly) measured on a
uniform 0/100 basis. Only WP1 is actually complete; WP2–WP5 have all started but none has finished, so each
earns $0 under 0/100.

`EV (uniform 0/100) = 80,000 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 80,000` (13.3% of BAC)

against the mixed-method result of $330,000 (55.0% of BAC) computed properly in Example 6.1.4. The reported
schedule and cost position swings from strongly favourable (EV $330,000 > PV $270,000 and AC $280,000) to
strongly unfavourable (EV $80,000 far below both) purely because of a method choice, with no change
whatsoever in the physical reality on site. This is the single most important lesson of KA 6.1: **the EVM
data set is only as trustworthy as the measurement-method decisions and data governance behind it** — a
lesson KA 6.2 assumes has already been secured before its variances and indices are calculated.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assembling PV, EV and AC from sources with different, unreconciled data dates.
- Presenting the summary table as a conclusion in itself, rather than as the input to the variance and
  forecasting analysis (KA 6.2, KA 6.3) and to the root-cause investigation that must follow any material
  variance.
- Skipping the reconciliation step between EV's control-account/WBS view and AC's cost-element/ledger view
  before comparing them.

**AI in this topic.** A GenAI tool can auto-draft a narrative summary of an EVM table ("the project is
running ahead of schedule and under budget") from the four numbers, which can save drafting time in a
routine report (cross-reference KA 4.4, KA 13.3, KA 13.5). It cannot know *why* — weather, resequencing, an
optimistic measurement method, an accrual gap — and a plausible-sounding AI narrative can mask exactly the
kind of method-sensitivity issue demonstrated in the mini-case above. Every AI-drafted commentary on an EVM
table must be checked against the underlying data and the professional's own knowledge of what is actually
happening on the project before it is issued.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.2 (CV, SV, CPI, SPI — the formal analysis of this table); KA 6.3 (EAC/ETC/VAC
forecasting from this table); KA 6.4 (EVM/schedule integration and its limits); KA 8.4 (Monitoring &
Controlling); KA 4.3 (management reporting cadence).

### Key terms

- **Planned Value (PV / BCWS)** — the authorised, time-phased budget for work scheduled to date.
- **Earned Value (EV / BCWP)** — the budgeted value of work actually completed to date.
- **Actual Cost (AC / ACWP)** — the actual, accrual-adjusted cost incurred for work performed to date.
- **Budget at Completion (BAC)** — the total budgeted cost of the project or control account.
- **Data date / status date** — the common point in time to which PV, EV and AC are all measured.
- **Control account / work package** — the level at which cost, schedule and scope are integrated and EV is
  measured (cross-reference KA 5.3).
- **0/100, 50/50 (or 20/80), % complete (units of measure), weighted milestones, Level of Effort (LOE),
  apportioned effort** — the standard earned-value measurement methods (6.1.4).
- **Front-loading** — disproportionate early credit assigned to a milestone or method relative to the
  effort it represents, artificially inflating early EV.
- **Time-phased budget / S-curve** — the cumulative spread of budget across the schedule from which PV is
  read (built in depth in KA 3.3).

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 6.1.1 [topic 6.1.1, level: recall]**
In Earned Value Management, Planned Value (PV, also known as BCWS) represents:
A. The budgeted cost of work actually performed to date
B. **The budgeted cost of work scheduled to be completed by a given date** ✔
C. The actual cost incurred for work performed to date
D. The total budget at completion of the project
*Rationale:* PV/BCWS is the time-phased budget for scheduled work. A describes EV; C describes AC; D
describes BAC.

**MCQ 6.1.2 [topic 6.1.2, level: application — numerical]**
A project's approved monthly budget is: Month 1 $60,000, Month 2 $90,000, Month 3 $120,000, Month 4
$150,000. What is cumulative PV at the end of Month 4?
A. $390,000
B. **$420,000** ✔
C. $450,000
D. $500,000
*Rationale:* `60,000 + 90,000 + 120,000 + 150,000 = 420,000`. A and C are plausible arithmetic slips; D
overstates the sum.

**MCQ 6.1.3 [topic 6.1.4, level: application — numerical]**
A trenching work package has a budget of $200,000 for 2,000 metres (percentage-complete/units method). By
the data date, 900 metres have been excavated. What is the EV?
A. $45,000
B. **$90,000** ✔
C. $180,000
D. $200,000
*Rationale:* `900/2,000 = 45%`; `45% × 200,000 = 90,000`. A mistakes the percentage figure for the answer
itself; C mistakes 90% complete; D assumes the package is finished.

**MCQ 6.1.4 [topic 6.1.4, level: analysis]**
Which situation is most likely to introduce a "front-loading" distortion into earned value?
A. Using 0/100 for a short-duration procurement milestone
B. Using LOE for a discrete, physically measurable construction activity
C. **Weighting the first milestone of a weighted-milestone activity well above the effort it actually represents** ✔
D. Using percentage complete (units of measure) for a quantifiable earthworks activity
*Rationale:* Front-loading is excessive early credit relative to real effort, inflating EV ahead of true
progress. A and D are appropriate uses of those methods; B causes a different distortion (loss of schedule
sensitivity), not front-loading.

**MCQ 6.1.5 [topic 6.1.3, level: application — numerical]**
A control account's cost ledger shows AC of $280,000 at the data date. A further $25,000 of subcontractor
work is complete and verified but not yet invoiced. What is the accrual-adjusted AC?
A. $255,000
B. $280,000
C. **$305,000** ✔
D. $330,000
*Rationale:* Accrual accounting requires recognising cost for work performed even where not yet invoiced
(cross-reference KA 1.3): `280,000 + 25,000 = 305,000`. A wrongly subtracts; D confuses the figure with EV.

### Self-check questions

1. Explain, in your own words, why a project could show AC exactly equal to PV (spending precisely as
   planned) while being seriously behind on real, physical progress. What does adding EV reveal that a
   simple budget-versus-actual-cost report cannot?
2. A work package with a $90,000 budget is measured using the 20/80 method (20% credited at start, 80% at
   completion). It has started but is not yet finished. What is its EV? If the same package were instead
   measured as LOE, with 40% of its planned duration elapsed, what would its EV be?
3. Using the Pipeline Segment 4B Month-3 data (BAC $600,000; PV $270,000; EV $330,000; AC $280,000), state
   in one sentence each what the EV-versus-PV comparison and the EV-versus-AC comparison each suggest about
   performance — without using the formal terms SV or CV (those are introduced in KA 6.2).

**Answers**

1. AC only measures money spent, not scope delivered; a project can spend exactly to plan while completing
   far less of the physical work than planned, and AC = PV would not reveal this. EV — the budgeted value
   of work *actually performed* — would be well below PV and AC in that case, exposing the shortfall that a
   cost-only report hides.
2. Under 20/80: the package has started but not finished, so only the start credit applies —
   `EV = 20% × 90,000 = $18,000`. Under LOE, EV tracks elapsed time regardless of physical output:
   `EV = 40% × 90,000 = $36,000`.
3. EV ($330,000) exceeds PV ($270,000), suggesting the project is earning value faster than planned — ahead
   of schedule in earned-value terms. EV ($330,000) exceeds AC ($280,000), suggesting more value has been
   earned than has been spent — a favourable cost position to date.
