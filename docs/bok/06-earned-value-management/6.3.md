# Domain 6 — Earned Value Management & Forecasting (EVM/EAC)

## KA 6.3 — Forecasting with EVM — the EAC family

KA 6.1 established PV, EV and AC; KA 6.2 turned them into variances and indices (CV, SV, CPI, SPI, TCPI).
This KA takes the last step a project controls professional is actually paid for: turning today's
performance into a **defensible forecast of the final cost**. That forecast has a name — the **Estimate at
Completion (EAC)** — and, critically, there is no single "the" EAC formula. There are several, each resting
on a different assumption about how the *remaining* work will behave, and each therefore appropriate to a
different real-world situation. Choosing the wrong one, or presenting one number without stating its
assumption, is one of the most consequential errors a controls professional can make: an EAC feeds funding
decisions, contingency drawdown, client reporting and, ultimately, whether a project sponsor finds out about
a cost overrun in month 5 or in month 11. This KA defines the **Estimate to Complete (ETC)**, works through
the three canonical EAC formulae named in the PMI/AACE forecasting literature, defines **Variance at
Completion (VAC)**, and — because no single formula is ever automatically "correct" — gives a decision
framework for choosing between them and a fully worked, side-by-side comparison on one project. It
cross-refers throughout to KA 3.4 (forecasting methods in general), KA 6.2 (the CPI/SPI/TCPI inputs this KA
consumes) and KA 6.4 (integrating the forecast with the schedule and this methodology's limits).

### 6.3.1 Estimate to Complete (ETC): the two routes to a forecast of remaining work

**Definition & purpose.** The Estimate to Complete (ETC) is the forecast cost of the work that **remains** to
be done, from the data date to project completion. It answers a narrower question than the EAC: not "what
will the whole project cost in total?" but "what will it cost to *finish* from here?" ETC and EAC are two
views of the same forecast and convert into one another directly.

**The underlying principle.** There are two legitimate routes to an ETC, and a competent forecast usually
uses both, one as a check on the other:

1. **Formula-derived (top-down).** Once an EAC has been calculated by one of the methods in 6.3.2–6.3.4, the
   ETC is simply what is left: `ETC = EAC − AC`. This is fast, systematic and reproducible, but it inherits
   whatever assumption the chosen EAC formula makes about the remaining work.
2. **Bottom-up (independent re-estimate).** The project team re-estimates the cost of the *specific* work
   packages that remain — not by formula, but by the same estimating disciplines used to build the original
   budget (KA 3.2): updated quantities, current productivity rates, known remaining risks. This is slower and
   more resource-intensive, but it is not mechanically tied to historical CPI/SPI and can capture things a
   formula cannot (a known upcoming risk event, a renegotiated rate, a resequencing of remaining work).

**Formula.** `ETC = EAC − AC` (AC in $, EAC in $) — the general, formula-derived relationship (used identically
regardless of which EAC method produced the EAC). Where a bottom-up estimate is used instead, ETC is the
independent re-estimate itself, and `EAC(bottom-up) = AC + ETC(bottom-up)`.

**Example 6.3.1 — ETC from a formula-derived EAC, checked against a bottom-up re-estimate.**

*Scenario.* A procurement sub-package has AC (accrual-adjusted, KA 6.1.3) of $95,000 to date. A statistical
forecast (using the EAC = BAC/CPI logic of 6.3.3) produces an EAC of $150,000 for this sub-package.
Separately, the procurement lead — aware that the outstanding items are now on a confirmed supplier delivery
schedule at agreed prices — re-estimates the specific cost to complete the remaining items directly at
$60,000.

*Required.* (a) The formula-derived ETC; (b) the bottom-up EAC implied by the independent re-estimate; (c)
the size and direction of the gap between the two views.

*Working.*
(a) `ETC (formula-derived) = EAC − AC = 150,000 − 95,000 = 55,000`
(b) `EAC (bottom-up) = AC + ETC (bottom-up) = 95,000 + 60,000 = 155,000`
(c) `Gap = EAC (bottom-up) − EAC (formula-derived) = 155,000 − 150,000 = 5,000`

*Answer.* Formula-derived ETC is **$55,000**; the bottom-up re-estimate implies an ETC of **$60,000** and an
EAC of **$155,000** — $5,000 higher than the statistical forecast.

*Interpretation.* A $5,000 gap on a $150,000 forecast is not alarming, but it is not zero either, and it
should be investigated before either number is reported alone: does the bottom-up estimate know about a
confirmed price change the historical CPI trend has not yet picked up, or is the procurement lead's estimate
optimistic? Reconciling the two views — not silently picking the more convenient one — is the professional
judgement this topic exists to protect.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating `ETC = BAC − EV` (the *remaining budget*) as if it were automatically a valid ETC — it is only
  valid under EAC Method 1's specific assumption (6.3.2), not in general.
- Relying solely on a formula-derived ETC for large, late-stage or high-risk remaining scope, where a
  bottom-up re-estimate is the professional standard.
- Never reconciling a bottom-up re-estimate against the formula-derived figure — each view is a check on the
  other, not a substitute for it.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted estimating tools can draft a bottom-up ETC quickly by pattern-matching
remaining work packages against historical productivity and cost data (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5),
which materially speeds up the re-estimating cycle on large remaining scopes. It cannot know about a
supplier's specific confirmed price, a site condition observed yesterday, or a resequencing decision made in
this week's coordination meeting — the inputs that make a bottom-up estimate valuable in the first place. An
AI-drafted ETC is a starting point for the estimator's review, never a substitute for it — **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2 (cost estimation methods, applied here to the remaining scope); KA 3.4
(forecasting methods generally); KA 6.2 (CPI/SPI that feed the formula-derived route); KA 6.3.2–6.3.4 (the
three EAC formulae); KA 6.4 (schedule integration and EVM's forecasting limits).

### 6.3.2 EAC Method 1 — EAC = AC + (BAC − EV): remaining work performed at budgeted rates

**Definition & purpose.** This is the most conservative-*sounding* of the three formulae in the sense that it
assumes the *least* about the future: it takes what has actually been spent (AC), and adds the *full
remaining budget* for the work not yet done (BAC − EV), as if that remaining work will now be executed
exactly on budget, regardless of how efficiently or inefficiently the work completed so far has gone.

**The underlying principle.** The formula is only defensible when the cause of any variance to date is
**atypical** — a one-off, non-repeating event (a single defective batch of material, one unusually difficult
excavation reach, a temporary resourcing gap now resolved) that has no bearing on how the *remaining* work
will be executed. Under that assumption, past performance is not a useful predictor of future performance,
so the remaining work is costed at its original budget rate.

**Formula.** `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)`, where AC = actual cost to date ($), BAC = budget at completion ($), EV
= earned value to date ($). Equivalently, `EAC = AC + ETC`, where `ETC = BAC − EV` under this method's
specific assumption.

**Assumptions & limits.**
- **Assumes:** the variance recorded to date (favourable or adverse) was caused by a one-off, isolated
  factor that will not recur, and that the team will execute all remaining work at the *original* budgeted
  rate.
- **Limit:** if the cause of the variance is systemic (a genuinely lower productivity rate, an underpriced
  rate in the original estimate, a persistent resourcing shortfall) this formula is **optimistic** — it
  understates the true forecast cost, because it ignores a trend that is very likely to continue.
- **When to use it:** after a documented, isolated root cause has been identified and resolved (e.g. "the
  overrun was one defective delivery, now replaced under warranty"), or very early in a project where too
  little data exists yet to trust a trend (cross-reference KA 6.4 on the reliability of early-stage EVM
  signals).

**Example 6.3.2 — Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5: EAC under Method 1.**

*Scenario.* Continuing the Pipeline Segment 4B running example from KA 6.1 (BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at
an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1). At the Month-5 data date, the project's cumulative EVM figures are:

| Measure | Value ($) | % of BAC |
|---|---|---|
| BAC | 600,000 | 100.0% |
| PV | 540,000 | 90.0% |
| EV | 405,000 | 67.5% |
| AC | 506,250 | 84.4% |

From these, `CPI = EV/AC = 405,000/506,250 = 0.80` and `SPI = EV/PV = 405,000/540,000 = 0.75`: the project is
running both over budget (CPI < 1.0) and behind schedule (SPI < 1.0) — a materially worse position than the
favourable Month-3 snapshot in KA 6.1, illustrating why a forecast must be re-run at every status date rather
than assumed to hold from an earlier, more favourable reporting period.

*Required.* EAC and ETC under Method 1, assuming the site team confirms the productivity shortfall behind the
current CPI/SPI was caused by a temporary equipment failure now fully resolved, with no expected recurrence.

*Working.*
`BAC − EV = 600,000 − 405,000 = 195,000`
`EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) = 506,250 + 195,000 = 701,250`
`ETC = EAC − AC = 195,000` (identical to BAC − EV, as expected under this method)

*Answer.* **EAC = $701,250** (SAR 2,629,688, rounded) and **ETC = $195,000**.

*Interpretation.* On the stated assumption — that the shortfall was a one-off, now-resolved cause — the
project is forecast to finish $101,250 over its $600,000 BAC (the VAC is formalised in 6.3.5). This is the
*least* pessimistic of the three EAC methods worked in full in Example 6.3.7, precisely because it is the
only one that does not project the current adverse CPI/SPI forward into the remaining work.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Applying Method 1 by default because it is the simplest formula, without first establishing — and
  documenting — that the variance really is a one-off. This is the single most common EAC misapplication in
  practice, and it systematically understates risk.
- Using Method 1 late in a project when a large body of consistent, adverse performance data already exists —
  the more data points showing a stable trend, the less credible a "return to budgeted rate" assumption
  becomes.
- Presenting the Method 1 EAC alone, without disclosing the assumption it rests on, to a client or sponsor.

**AI in this topic.** Pattern-classification tools can compare a current variance against a library of
historical variance root causes (materials, weather, productivity, scope) and suggest whether it more closely
resembles a historically "isolated" or "recurring" pattern (cross-reference KA 13.5) — a genuinely useful
triage aid for deciding whether Method 1's assumption is plausible. It cannot confirm that *this specific*
root cause has actually been resolved on site; that confirmation is an engineering/site-management judgement,
not a data pattern, and remains the professional's responsibility before Method 1 is relied upon.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.4 (forecasting methods and root-cause-driven forecasting); KA 5.4 (change control
and the cost impact of variations, a common genuine one-off cause); KA 6.2 (CV/CPI as the trigger for asking
whether Method 1 applies); KA 6.4 (why a single data point is a weak basis for any EAC method).

### 6.3.3 EAC Method 2 — EAC = BAC / CPI: cumulative cost efficiency continues

**Definition & purpose.** This formula assumes the opposite of Method 1: that the cost efficiency achieved to
date (the cumulative CPI) is not a one-off but a **representative rate** that will continue to apply to *all*
remaining work, including the work already done. It is the simplest statistical extrapolation of the EAC
family and, in practice, the most widely used default.

**The underlying principle.** If a project has spent $1 to earn $0.80 of value on average across everything
done so far (CPI = 0.80), and there is no specific reason to expect that rate to change, the most defensible
assumption is that it will continue to cost roughly $1 to earn every further $0.80 of value for the rest of
the project too — which is mathematically equivalent to re-costing the *entire* project (not just the
remainder) at the current cumulative efficiency: `BAC / CPI`.

**Formula.** `EAC = BAC / CPI`, where BAC = budget at completion ($), CPI = EV/AC (dimensionless cumulative
cost performance index, to date).

**Assumptions & limits.**
- **Assumes:** cumulative cost efficiency to date is stable and representative, and will persist unchanged
  for the entire remaining scope; it takes no account of the schedule position at all.
- **Limit:** it ignores any recovery plan, corrective action, or change in the nature of remaining work
  (e.g. easier vs harder work still to come). It also ignores schedule — a project that is both over budget
  and badly behind schedule may face cost pressure from **schedule** effects (overtime, remobilisation,
  extended preliminaries) that this formula does not capture (Method 3, 6.3.4, addresses that).
- **When to use it:** once a stable CPI trend is well established over several reporting periods (not a
  single data point), for relatively homogeneous, repetitive scope where the cost driver behind the
  cumulative CPI is expected to remain constant.

**Example 6.3.3 — Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5: EAC under Method 2.**

*Scenario.* Same Month-5 data as Example 6.3.2: BAC $600,000; CPI = 0.80 (i.e. every $1 spent has, on
average, earned $0.80 of budgeted value). CPI has been tracked at 0.80–0.82 for the last three reporting
periods, supporting the "stable trend" assumption this method requires.

*Required.* EAC and ETC under Method 2.

*Working.*
`EAC = BAC / CPI = 600,000 / 0.80 = 750,000`
`ETC = EAC − AC = 750,000 − 506,250 = 243,750`

*Answer.* **EAC = $750,000** (SAR 2,812,500) and **ETC = $243,750**.

*Interpretation.* Method 2 forecasts a $150,000 overrun against the $600,000 BAC — materially worse than
Method 1's $701,250 forecast, because it projects the current 0.80 cost efficiency across *all* remaining
spend rather than assuming a return to the original budgeted rate. Because the CPI trend here is described as
stable over three periods (not a single reading), Method 2's assumption is better supported by the evidence
than Method 1's in this particular scenario — the full comparison and interpretation of the divergence is
in Example 6.3.7.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Applying Method 2 from a single, early, thin data point (e.g. after only one reporting period) — a
  cumulative CPI needs a track record to be "representative," not a snapshot.
- Ignoring the schedule position entirely: a project can have a healthy CPI while heading for a schedule-driven
  cost blow-out (acceleration, extended site overheads) that Method 2 will not see.
- Failing to re-run the calculation at each new status date — CPI drifts, and a Month-3 EAC using Month-3
  CPI is stale by Month 5 (as this KA's own running example demonstrates: KA 6.1's Month-3 CPI was
  favourable; this KA's Month-5 CPI is materially worse).

**AI in this topic.** Time-series/statistical forecasting tools can compute a rolling CPI trend automatically,
flag when it has stabilised (or is still too volatile to trust), and re-run `BAC/CPI` at every new data point
without manual recalculation (cross-reference KA 13.5) — a genuine efficiency gain for routine reporting.
These tools cannot judge whether the *reason* behind the trend (a resourcing model, a subcontractor's
performance, an underpriced rate) will actually persist into the remaining scope; that causal judgement is
the professional's, and is exactly what separates a defensible Method 2 forecast from a mechanically
generated but ungrounded one.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.2 (CPI derivation and interpretation, in depth); KA 3.4 (trend-based forecasting
generally); KA 6.4 (limits of a purely cost-based extrapolation that ignores schedule); KA 13.5 (predictive/
statistical forecasting tooling).

### 6.3.4 EAC Method 3 — EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) / (CPI × SPI): cost and schedule pressure both continue

**Definition & purpose.** This formula extends Method 2's logic by recognising that cost pressure is not the
only thing that can drive up the cost of the *remaining* work: being behind schedule (SPI < 1.0) commonly
forces recovery actions — overtime, additional shifts, resequencing, extended time-related preliminaries —
that themselves cost money. Method 3 discounts the remaining budget by the **combined** cost and schedule
performance, producing the most conservative of the three formulae whenever both indices are adverse.

**The underlying principle.** The remaining work (BAC − EV) is divided not by CPI alone but by the product
`CPI × SPI`. When both indices are below 1.0, this composite factor is smaller than either index alone,
which *increases* the forecast cost of the remaining work more sharply than Method 2 does — reflecting the
view that a project both over budget and behind schedule faces compounding, not merely additive, pressure on
the work still to come.

**Formula.** `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) / (CPI × SPI)`, where AC, BAC, EV are in $, and CPI and SPI are the
dimensionless cumulative indices from KA 6.2. Equivalently, `ETC = (BAC − EV) / (CPI × SPI)`.

**Assumptions & limits.**
- **Assumes:** both the cost inefficiency and the schedule inefficiency observed to date will continue to
  affect the *remaining* work, and that schedule pressure specifically will translate into additional cost
  (which is a real but not universal effect — some schedule slippage has no cost consequence at all, e.g.
  float being consumed on a non-critical path).
- **Limit:** it can be **overly conservative** where the remaining work is not, in fact, schedule-critical, or
  where a credible recovery plan exists that specifically targets the schedule cause without implying extra
  cost. It compounds two separate performance signals into one number, which can be harder to explain to a
  non-technical stakeholder than a single-driver formula.
- **When to use it:** where the project is behind schedule **on or near the critical path** (cross-reference
  KA 10.2) such that recovery will plausibly require paid acceleration, and where both CPI and SPI trends are
  established, not single readings.

**Example 6.3.4 — Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5: EAC under Method 3.**

*Scenario.* Same Month-5 data: BAC $600,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250; CPI = 0.80; SPI = 0.75. The Month-5
schedule update confirms the shortfall lies on activities feeding the critical path, and recovery would
require added shift work.

*Required.* EAC and ETC under Method 3.

*Working.*
`CPI × SPI = 0.80 × 0.75 = 0.60`
`BAC − EV = 600,000 − 405,000 = 195,000`
`ETC = (BAC − EV) / (CPI × SPI) = 195,000 / 0.60 = 325,000`
`EAC = AC + ETC = 506,250 + 325,000 = 831,250`

*Answer.* **EAC = $831,250** (SAR 3,117,188, rounded) and **ETC = $325,000**.

*Interpretation.* Method 3 forecasts a $231,250 overrun — the most severe of the three forecasts — because it
assumes the remaining work must absorb *both* the 20% cost inefficiency and the 25% schedule inefficiency
recorded to date. Given the Month-5 schedule update confirms the shortfall is on the critical path and
recovery will need paid acceleration, this is also the best-evidenced of the three assumptions for this
particular project at this particular data date — a conclusion drawn out fully in Example 6.3.7.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Applying Method 3 mechanically whenever SPI < 1.0, without first confirming the schedule slippage is
  actually on or near the critical path — slippage on a non-critical activity with abundant float has no cost
  consequence and Method 3 will overstate the forecast.
- Treating Method 3's output as automatically "the most accurate" simply because it is the most conservative
  — conservatism is not the same as correctness; the formula is only as good as its assumption fitting the
  facts.
- Double-counting: applying a separate, manual acceleration-cost estimate *on top of* a Method 3 EAC that
  already embeds a schedule-pressure allowance for the same cause.

**AI in this topic.** A predictive-EAC model — typically a regression or machine-learning model trained on
CPI, SPI and other leading indicators (resourcing levels, change-order volume, weather/site data) — is the
natural extension of Method 3's "combine cost and schedule signals" logic, and can incorporate more variables
than a single index product (cross-reference KA 6.4, KA 13.5). Such models can flag an emerging cost/schedule
compounding risk earlier than a monthly EVM cycle would. They remain statistical extrapolations of historical
patterns: they do not know whether the specific recovery plan on this project will work, whether the client
will approve acceleration funding, or whether the critical-path finding itself is correct. Every predictive
EAC output — AI-generated or formula-generated — requires the professional's sign-off before it is reported,
consistent with **"AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable."**

**Cross-references.** KA 6.2 (SPI derivation); KA 6.4 (integrating EVM with the schedule; Earned Schedule as
an alternative, time-based way to express schedule pressure); KA 10.2 (critical path — whether schedule
slippage actually threatens completion); KA 13.5 (predictive EAC models in practice); KA 13.6 (governance of
AI-generated forecasts).

### 6.3.5 Variance at Completion (VAC): translating the forecast into a bottom-line variance

**Definition & purpose.** Variance at Completion (VAC) restates whichever EAC has been adopted as a single,
signed number against the original budget: the forecast **surplus or shortfall** at project completion. It is
the number a sponsor or client ultimately wants to see, because it converts a forecast total cost back into
the same "over/under budget" language used for CV throughout the project (KA 6.2).

**The underlying principle.** VAC is entirely derived from whichever EAC has been chosen — it introduces no
new data and no new assumption of its own. Its value is purely one of translation and threshold-setting: a
VAC is what triggers a contingency drawdown decision, a client notification, or an escalation, so it must
always be reported **with** the EAC method and assumption behind it, never as a bare number.

**Formula.** `VAC = BAC − EAC`, where BAC = budget at completion ($) and EAC = the estimate at completion
($) under whichever method has been adopted. A **negative** VAC is an adverse (unfavourable) forecast
variance — the project is forecast to cost more than budgeted; a **positive** VAC is favourable.

**Example 6.3.5 — VAC on Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5, using the Method 2 EAC as the reporting basis.**

*Scenario.* The project controls team has adopted Method 2's EAC ($750,000, Example 6.3.3) as the figure to
report this month, on the basis of its well-supported, stable three-period CPI trend.

*Required.* VAC, and a one-line statement of what it means for the sponsor.

*Working.* `VAC = BAC − EAC = 600,000 − 750,000 = −150,000`

*Answer.* **VAC = −$150,000** (SAR (562,500)) — an **adverse** variance at completion.

*Interpretation.* On the adopted forecast, Pipeline Segment 4B is expected to finish $150,000 (25.0% of BAC)
over its original $600,000 budget. This is the figure that should trigger a formal review of available
contingency and management reserve (cross-reference KA 3.1, KA 12.3) — not the EAC number alone, which reads
less immediately as a funding decision trigger.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting VAC without stating which EAC method produced it — as Example 6.3.7 shows, the same project's
  VAC ranges from −$101,250 to −$231,250 depending only on the method chosen, a materially different funding
  conversation in each case.
- Treating a favourable VAC as licence to release contingency immediately, without confirming the favourable
  trend is durable (the same "is this a one-off or a trend?" question from 6.3.2 applies symmetrically to
  good news).
- Confusing VAC (a *forecast*, based on an EAC) with CV (cumulative cost variance *to date*, KA 6.2) — they
  answer different questions and are frequently, and wrongly, used interchangeably in informal reporting.

**AI in this topic.** Dashboard and business-intelligence tools can compute VAC automatically for every
control account or project in a portfolio and flag any that breach a pre-set adverse threshold for exception
reporting (cross-reference KA 4.3, KA 4.4, KA 13.5) — valuable at portfolio scale, where a human cannot
manually scan every line every month. The threshold itself, and the judgement of what a breach should trigger
(escalation, a deep-dive review, a contingency drawdown request), remain decisions for the professional; an
automated VAC flag is a prompt to investigate, not a decision already made.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 (contingency and management reserve, the usual funding response to an adverse
VAC); KA 6.2 (CV, the in-period equivalent metric); KA 6.4 (reporting VAC alongside schedule-based forecasts);
KA 12.3 (linking risk-based contingency to a forecast shortfall).

### 6.3.6 Choosing the right EAC method: diagnosis, professional judgement and the bottom-up check

**Definition & purpose.** Because the three formulae in 6.3.2–6.3.4 can produce materially different answers
from the *same* underlying PV/EV/AC data (Example 6.3.7 shows a $130,000 spread on a $600,000 project), the
choice of method is itself a professional judgement, not a mechanical default. This topic gives a structured
way to make — and document — that choice.

**The underlying principle.** The three formulae differ only in what they assume about the **cause and
persistence** of any variance to date. The correct question is never "which formula do I habitually use?" but
"what does the evidence say about whether today's cost and schedule performance will continue into the
remaining work?" A short diagnostic sequence answers this:

1. **Is there a specific, documented, resolved root cause** for the variance (a single event, now closed
   out)? → Method 1 (`AC + (BAC − EV)`) is defensible.
2. **Is there instead a stable, multi-period cost trend with no specific resolved cause**, and no material
   schedule concern? → Method 2 (`BAC / CPI`) is the standard default.
3. **Is the project also materially behind schedule, on or near the critical path**, such that recovery would
   plausibly cost money? → Method 3 (`AC + (BAC − EV)/(CPI × SPI)`) is the more defensible, if more
   conservative, choice.
4. **Whatever the formula result, does a bottom-up re-estimate (6.3.1) of the specific remaining scope
   materially agree or disagree?** A large, unexplained gap between the formula-derived and bottom-up EAC is
   itself a signal that neither should be reported without further investigation.

A related, complementary check is the **To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI)** (KA 6.2): the efficiency the
remaining work would need to achieve to hit a *given* target (BAC, or a chosen EAC). If the TCPI required to
hit the current Method 1 EAC is far above anything the project has achieved to date, that is independent
evidence Method 1's "return to budgeted rate" assumption is not credible — a useful cross-check that does not
require choosing a different EAC formula outright.

**Fig 6.3.6 — Decision flow for choosing an EAC method.**
Chart type: a top-to-bottom decision-tree diagram in brand blue `#1D4ED8` with clean, sans-serif labels. Four
diamond decision nodes in sequence, each leading either to a labelled outcome box (Method 1 / Method 2 /
Method 3 / "investigate the gap before reporting either") or down to the next question; a final note box at
the bottom reading "always state the assumption alongside the number."

*Underlying data (the decision logic, reproducible as a table):*

| Step | Question | If yes → | If no → |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Documented, resolved, one-off root cause for the variance? | Method 1 | Go to step 2 |
| 2 | Stable, multi-period cost trend, no material schedule concern? | Method 2 | Go to step 3 |
| 3 | Materially behind schedule on/near the critical path, recovery likely to cost money? | Method 3 | Re-examine step 1/2 evidence; default to Method 2 pending further diagnosis |
| 4 | Does a bottom-up ETC materially agree with the chosen formula's ETC? | Report the EAC, with its assumption stated | Investigate the gap before reporting either figure |

**Common pitfalls.**
- Picking whichever formula gives the "best-looking" number, rather than the formula whose assumption best
  fits the evidence — this is a form of reporting bias, not forecasting.
- Never revisiting the choice: a Method 1 assumption made in month 2 (plausibly, with little data) should be
  actively re-tested as more reporting periods accumulate, not left unchanged for the rest of the project.
- Reporting a single EAC as if it were a certainty rather than a conditional forecast — best practice is to
  disclose the method and, for material decisions, the range across methods (Example 6.3.7).

**AI in this topic.** A decision-support tool can apply this diagnostic sequence automatically — checking
whether a trend is statistically stable, whether schedule slippage sits on the critical path, and whether a
bottom-up estimate has been logged for comparison — and suggest which method's assumption the data best
supports (cross-reference KA 13.5). This can genuinely speed up and standardise the diagnostic step. It
cannot substitute for the human confirmation that a root cause is *actually* resolved, that a critical-path
finding is *actually* correct, or that a bottom-up estimate is *actually* well-founded — each of those remains
a judgement call requiring domain knowledge the model does not have. The final choice of method, and its
disclosure, is always signed off by the professional.

### 6.3.7 Multi-scenario worked example — Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5: comparing all three EAC methods side by side

**Definition & purpose.** This topic draws together 6.3.2–6.3.5 into the single exhibit a controls
professional would actually prepare for a monthly report or a funding review: one project's data, all three
EAC methods computed side by side, the resulting ETC and VAC under each, and a reasoned interpretation of why
they diverge and what a professional does about it.

**Example 6.3.7 — the full EAC comparison.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B, Month-5 data date (continuing KA 6.1's running example; BAC unchanged since
project inception):

| Measure | Value ($) | Value (SAR, illustrative 3.75 = USD 1) | % of BAC |
|---|---|---|---|
| BAC | 600,000 | 2,250,000 | 100.0% |
| PV | 540,000 | 2,025,000 | 90.0% |
| EV | 405,000 | 1,518,750 | 67.5% |
| AC | 506,250 | 1,898,438 (rounded) | 84.4% |
| CPI (= EV/AC) | 0.80 | — | — |
| SPI (= EV/PV) | 0.75 | — | — |

*Required.* EAC, ETC and VAC under each of the three formulae, and an interpretation of the spread.

*Working.*

Common inputs used by all three methods: `BAC − EV = 600,000 − 405,000 = 195,000`; `CPI × SPI = 0.80 × 0.75 =
0.60`.

| Method | Formula | EAC ($) | ETC = EAC − AC ($) | VAC = BAC − EAC ($) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — future work at budget** | `AC + (BAC − EV)` | 506,250 + 195,000 = **701,250** | 195,000 | 600,000 − 701,250 = **(101,250)** |
| **2 — past efficiency continues** | `BAC / CPI` | 600,000 / 0.80 = **750,000** | 750,000 − 506,250 = 243,750 | 600,000 − 750,000 = **(150,000)** |
| **3 — cost & schedule pressure continue** | `AC + (BAC − EV)/(CPI × SPI)` | 506,250 + (195,000/0.60) = 506,250 + 325,000 = **831,250** | 325,000 | 600,000 − 831,250 = **(231,250)** |

*(Figures in parentheses are adverse/unfavourable variances, per KA style convention.)*

*Answer.* **EAC ranges from $701,250 (Method 1) to $831,250 (Method 3)** — a spread of **$130,000**, or 21.7%
of BAC, depending only on which assumption is applied to the *same* underlying PV/EV/AC data. VAC
correspondingly ranges from an adverse **$101,250** to an adverse **$231,250**.

*Interpretation — why the three methods diverge.* All three methods share the same "sunk" AC and the same
remaining scope (BAC − EV = $195,000); they differ only in what they assume happens to that $195,000 of
remaining work:

- **Method 1** assumes the adverse CPI (0.80) and SPI (0.75) recorded to date will **not** recur — the
  remaining $195,000 of work is costed at its original budgeted rate. This produces the lowest (least
  conservative) EAC, $701,250.
- **Method 2** assumes the 0.80 cumulative cost efficiency **will** persist across all remaining spend,
  producing a higher EAC, $750,000 — $48,750 more than Method 1, reflecting the cost (but not schedule)
  pressure continuing.
- **Method 3** additionally assumes the 0.75 schedule inefficiency will translate into further cost pressure
  on the remaining work (e.g. paid acceleration to protect the critical path), compounding both indices and
  producing the highest EAC, $831,250 — $81,250 more than Method 2 and $130,000 more than Method 1.

Because both CPI and SPI are below 1.0 in this scenario, the ordering **EAC1 < EAC2 < EAC3** holds; this
pattern is typical whenever cost and schedule performance are both adverse, but it is not a universal law —
each method must always be calculated, not assumed, from the current data.

Applying the 6.3.6 diagnostic: the Month-5 schedule update (Example 6.3.4) confirms the shortfall sits on
activities feeding the critical path and that recovery will require paid acceleration — evidence that favours
Method 3's assumption over Method 1's. The three-period stable CPI trend (Example 6.3.3) rules out treating
the cost variance as a one-off. On the evidence presented, **Method 3's $831,250 EAC (VAC of an adverse
$231,250) is the best-supported single figure to report this month** — but professional practice is to
disclose the full range (Method 1 to Method 3, $701,250–$831,250) to the sponsor alongside the recommended
figure and its assumption, together with the bottom-up ETC re-estimate (6.3.1) as an independent check, rather
than presenting one number as if no judgement were involved in producing it.

**Common pitfalls (specific to comparing methods).**
- Reporting only the most convenient (typically the lowest) EAC without disclosing that two other,
  equally formula-valid figures exist for the same data.
- Averaging the three EAC figures as if that produced a more "balanced" forecast — an average of three
  different assumptions is not itself a defensible assumption; it manufactures false precision.
- Failing to re-run the comparison at the next data date — the ordering and spread here reflect Month 5 only
  and will change again as CPI and SPI move.

**AI in this topic.** A reporting-automation tool can generate exactly the comparison table and fan chart
shown here automatically from a live PV/EV/AC/CPI/SPI feed, refreshed at every data date, and can flag when
the spread between methods widens materially period-on-period (cross-reference KA 4.4, KA 13.5) — a genuine,
low-risk use of automation since the underlying arithmetic is fully deterministic. What it cannot automate is
the diagnostic judgement in 6.3.6 (is this variance one-off, trending, or schedule-critical?) or the decision
of which figure — or range — to actually put in front of a sponsor. That remains, unavoidably, the
professional's call.

### Key terms

- **Estimate to Complete (ETC)** — the forecast cost of the work remaining from the data date to completion;
  `ETC = EAC − AC` (formula-derived) or an independent bottom-up re-estimate.
- **Estimate at Completion (EAC)** — the forecast total cost of the project, calculated by one of several
  formulae, each resting on a different assumption about the remaining work.
- **EAC Method 1** — `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)`; assumes the remaining work is executed at the original budgeted
  rate (variance to date treated as atypical).
- **EAC Method 2** — `EAC = BAC / CPI`; assumes the cumulative cost efficiency to date continues unchanged
  across all remaining spend.
- **EAC Method 3** — `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) / (CPI × SPI)`; assumes both cost and schedule pressure to date
  continue into the remaining work.
- **Variance at Completion (VAC)** — `VAC = BAC − EAC`; the forecast surplus (positive) or shortfall
  (negative/adverse) at completion, always reported alongside the EAC method it derives from.
- **Bottom-up EAC/ETC** — an independent re-estimate of the remaining scope, used to validate a
  formula-derived EAC rather than replace it.
- **To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI)** — the efficiency required in the remaining work to hit a target
  (BAC or a chosen EAC); a diagnostic cross-check on whether an EAC assumption is credible (full treatment in
  KA 6.2).
- **One-off (atypical) variance vs systemic (trend) variance** — the central diagnostic distinction driving
  the choice between EAC Method 1 and Methods 2/3.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 6.3.1 [topic 6.3.1, level: recall]**
Which of the following correctly states the relationship between the Estimate to Complete (ETC) and the
Estimate at Completion (EAC)?
A. `ETC = BAC − AC`
B. **`ETC = EAC − AC`** ✔
C. `EAC = ETC − AC`
D. `ETC = BAC / CPI`
*Rationale:* ETC is what remains to be spent to reach the EAC, given what has already been spent (AC):
`ETC = EAC − AC`. A confuses ETC with a budget-remaining figure that ignores actual spend; C reverses the
relationship; D is the Method 2 EAC formula, not the ETC relationship.

**MCQ 6.3.2 [topic 6.3.2, level: application — numerical]**
A project has BAC $600,000, EV $405,000 and AC $506,250. Using **EAC Method 1** (`EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)`),
what is the EAC?
A. **$701,250** ✔
B. $750,000
C. $831,250
D. $506,250
*Rationale:* `BAC − EV = 600,000 − 405,000 = 195,000`; `EAC = 506,250 + 195,000 = 701,250`. B is the Method 2
result (`BAC/CPI`); C is the Method 3 result; D mistakes AC alone for the EAC.

**MCQ 6.3.3 [topic 6.3.3, level: application — numerical]**
Using the same project (BAC $600,000, CPI = 0.80), what is the EAC under **Method 2** (`EAC = BAC / CPI`)?
A. $701,250
B. **$750,000** ✔
C. $831,250
D. $480,000
*Rationale:* `EAC = 600,000 / 0.80 = 750,000`. A is the Method 1 result; C is the Method 3 result; D
incorrectly multiplies BAC by CPI instead of dividing.

**MCQ 6.3.4 [topic 6.3.4, level: application — numerical]**
Using the same project (BAC $600,000, EV $405,000, AC $506,250, CPI = 0.80, SPI = 0.75), what is the EAC
under **Method 3** (`EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) / (CPI × SPI)`)?
A. $701,250
B. $750,000
C. **$831,250** ✔
D. $920,000
*Rationale:* `CPI × SPI = 0.60`; `(BAC − EV)/0.60 = 195,000/0.60 = 325,000`; `EAC = 506,250 + 325,000 =
831,250`. A is the Method 1 result; B is the Method 2 result; D results from dividing by CPI alone (0.80)
instead of CPI × SPI.

**MCQ 6.3.5 [topic 6.3.6, level: analysis]**
A control account shows an adverse cost variance traced to a single defective batch of material, now
replaced under warranty with no further impact expected, and no schedule concern. Which EAC method's
assumption is most defensible for this account?
A. Method 2 (`BAC/CPI`), because CPI should always be trusted
B. **Method 1 (`AC + (BAC − EV)`), because the cause was isolated and resolved** ✔
C. Method 3 (`AC + (BAC − EV)/(CPI × SPI)`), because it is the most conservative and therefore always the
safest choice
D. No EAC can be calculated without a bottom-up re-estimate first
*Rationale:* Method 1's assumption — that the remaining work reverts to budgeted rates — fits a documented,
resolved, one-off cause with no schedule impact. A wrongly treats CPI as always representative regardless of
cause; C confuses conservatism with correctness; D overstates the need for a bottom-up estimate as a
precondition, though it remains good practice as a check (6.3.1).

**MCQ 6.3.6 [topic 6.3.5, level: application — numerical]**
A project has BAC $600,000 and, under the EAC method adopted for reporting, an EAC of $750,000. What is the
VAC, and is it favourable or adverse?
A. +$150,000, favourable
B. **−$150,000, adverse** ✔
C. −$750,000, adverse
D. $0, on budget
*Rationale:* `VAC = BAC − EAC = 600,000 − 750,000 = −150,000`; a negative VAC is adverse (the project is
forecast to overrun). A reverses the sign; C confuses VAC with the EAC itself; D ignores the calculation
entirely.

### Self-check questions

1. Using only the general relationship between the formulae (no arithmetic required): if a project's CPI is
   below 1.0 and its SPI is below 1.0, would you generally expect EAC Method 3 to produce a higher or lower
   forecast than EAC Method 2? Explain why in one or two sentences.
2. Using the Pipeline Segment 4B Month-5 data (BAC $600,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250; CPI 0.80; SPI 0.75),
   compute the ETC and VAC under **EAC Method 3** independently, then check your answer against Example
   6.3.7.
3. A colleague proposes reporting the *average* of the three EAC methods' results as "a more balanced
   forecast." Explain, using the ideas in 6.3.6 and 6.3.7, why this is not a defensible practice.

**Answers**

1. Generally higher. Method 2 discounts the remaining budget by cost efficiency (CPI) alone; Method 3
   discounts it by the *combined* cost-and-schedule factor (CPI × SPI). When both indices are below 1.0,
   `CPI × SPI` is smaller than `CPI` alone, so dividing the remaining budget by it produces a larger ETC, and
   therefore a larger EAC, than Method 2 — as confirmed numerically in Example 6.3.7 ($831,250 vs $750,000).
2. `CPI × SPI = 0.80 × 0.75 = 0.60`; `BAC − EV = 600,000 − 405,000 = 195,000`; `ETC = 195,000 / 0.60 =
   325,000`; `EAC = 506,250 + 325,000 = 831,250`; `VAC = 600,000 − 831,250 = −231,250` (adverse). This
   matches Example 6.3.7's Method 3 result exactly.
3. Each EAC method rests on a distinct, stated assumption about the cause and persistence of the current
   variance (6.3.6); an average blends three different, mutually exclusive assumptions into one number that
   corresponds to no actual assumption at all, manufacturing a false sense of precision. The defensible
   practice is to diagnose which single assumption the evidence best supports (6.3.6's decision sequence) and
   report that figure, disclosing the full range and the bottom-up check alongside it — not to average away
   the disagreement between methods.
