# Domain 7 — Contracts, Commercial Management, BoQ, Invoicing & Revenue

## KA 7.1 — Types of contract

Every other Knowledge Area in this domain — contract administration (KA 7.2), pricing a Bill of Quantities
(KA 7.3), building an interim application for payment (KA 7.4), and recognising revenue from the commercial
cycle (KA 7.5) — depends on one prior decision made before a single brick is laid or a single line of code is
written: **how is the contractor going to be paid?** That decision, the contract's **pricing mechanism**, is
not an administrative detail. It is a deliberate allocation of commercial risk between the client (the
employer, or "Owner") and the contractor, and it shapes almost everything a project controls professional
subsequently does on the contract — which cost-control tools matter most, how a change is priced, how
aggressively margin needs to be protected, and how revenue is recognised under IFRS 15 (KA 2.2, KA 7.5). This
Knowledge Area sets out the main contract types used in project and construction contracting, the risk each
one allocates to each party, the incentive mechanisms some of them build in, and the standard forms of
contract — FIDIC and NEC, described here at **awareness level, in the author's own words, never quoting
contract text verbatim** — that codify these choices in practice. The chapter continues the recurring
illustrative entity **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC** and its fixed-price **Pipeline Segment 4B** contract
(KA 1.1; KA 6.1–6.3, BAC $600,000; KA 11.1, contract price $750,000), and introduces one new illustrative
scope, **Pipeline Segment 5A** — the next, less well-surveyed phase of the same pipeline programme, not yet
contracted at the point this chapter opens — as the vehicle for comparing contract types on a single, shared
scope, at an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**.

### 7.1.1 The contract-type spectrum: pricing mechanism as a risk-allocation choice

**Definition & purpose.** A contract's **pricing mechanism** is the rule that converts work performed into
an amount owed to the contractor. Every pricing mechanism in use — from a single fixed lump sum to
open-ended reimbursement of actual cost — sits somewhere on a spectrum between two extremes: **cost-based**
mechanisms, where the contractor is paid what it actually costs (plus a fee), and **price-based** mechanisms,
where the contractor is paid a price fixed in advance regardless of what the work actually costs it. This is
distinct from, though related to, two other commercial choices this KA touches only briefly and cross-refers
elsewhere: the **procurement route** (who designs — design-bid-build, design-build, or a fully integrated
EPC/turnkey model, cross-reference KA 8.2) and the **standard form** used to document the deal (FIDIC, NEC,
or a bespoke form — 7.1.7).

**The underlying principle.** A widely applied commercial rule of thumb holds that risk should sit with the
party best placed to control, price and manage it. A contractor controls how efficiently it executes defined
work — so productivity risk sits naturally with the contractor under most mechanisms. Nobody fully controls
how much of a poorly surveyed excavation will actually be needed — so a well-drafted contract for
genuinely uncertain quantities lets that risk sit, at least partly, with the party who set the scope (often
the client). Every contract type in this KA is best understood as a different answer to the same three
underlying risk questions: **who bears quantity risk** (if more or less work turns out to be needed than
assumed at award), **who bears price/rate risk** (if input costs — labour, plant, materials — turn out
higher or lower than priced), and **who bears productivity risk** (if the contractor takes more or less
effort than costed to do the same defined amount of work). A single contract type rarely gives one party 100%
of every kind of risk; the more useful skill is naming, for any given mechanism, which of the three risks
sits where.

**Illustrative example — two contracts, two starting points.** Meridian's Pipeline Segment 4B was agreed at a
**fixed contract price of $750,000** (SAR 2,812,500) against an internal budgeted cost (BAC) of $600,000 (KA
6.1, KA 11.1, Example 11.1.3) — a lump-sum contract, developed in depth in 7.1.2. As this chapter opens,
Meridian is separately negotiating the commercial structure for **Pipeline Segment 5A**, the next phase of
the same programme for the same client. Segment 5A's ground conditions are materially less well surveyed than
4B's were at award — early boreholes suggest variable strata that could require significantly more, or less,
excavation than a first-pass estimate implies. That single fact — genuine, currently unquantifiable quantity
and ground-condition uncertainty — is what makes Segment 5A a useful vehicle for this chapter: the "right"
contract type is not obvious, and the topics that follow build, piece by piece, toward a considered
recommendation in 7.1.8.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assuming one contract type is simply "better" than another in the abstract — the right choice depends on
  how well the scope and quantities are actually known at the point of pricing, not on any type's inherent

- Treating the pricing mechanism and the standard form as the same decision — a FIDIC or NEC form can carry

  risk.
- Assuming risk allocated on paper is risk that disappears — shifting quantity risk to the client does not
  make the ground any more predictable; it only decides who pays when the prediction is wrong.

first-pass triage across a large contract portfolio. It cannot decide which allocation of quantity, price and
productivity risk is *appropriate* for a specific scope's actual risk profile — that commercial judgement,
developed through this chapter and applied in the 7.1.8 mini-case, remains with the professional.

### 7.1.2 Lump-sum / fixed-price contracts

**Definition & purpose.** A **lump-sum (fixed-price) contract** sets a single, fixed total price for a
defined scope of work, agreed before work starts and not adjusted for the contractor's actual cost of
delivering that scope (adjustments occur only for formally instructed, priced **variations** to the defined
scope itself — cross-reference KA 5.4, 7.1.5). FIDIC's *Silver Book* (Conditions of Contract for EPC/Turnkey
Projects) and, to a lesser extent, its *Yellow Book* (Plant and Design-Build) are commonly used lump-sum-style
forms (7.1.7); under NEC terminology, a broadly comparable arrangement is NEC's "priced contract with an
activity schedule."

**The underlying principle.** Under a lump-sum contract, the contractor bears **quantity, price and
productivity risk almost in full** within the defined scope: if more work turns out to be needed, input
prices rise, or the contractor is simply less efficient than it costed, the contractor's margin absorbs the
difference — the client's price does not move. This is precisely why lump-sum sits at the "highest client
price certainty" end of Fig 7.1.1's spectrum, and why contractors price a risk premium into a lump-sum bid
for scope they are less confident of.

**Formula.**

`Margin = Contract price − Cost (budgeted, forecast, or actual, as stated)`

`Margin (%) = Margin ÷ Contract price`

Because the contract price is fixed, **margin is the entire residual bearing every cost risk** — which is
why continuous EAC monitoring (KA 6.3) is the single most important cost-control discipline on a lump-sum
contract: every dollar an EAC forecast rises above BAC is, on a lump-sum contract with no client entitlement
to compensation, a dollar taken directly out of margin.

**Example 7.1.2 — Margin erosion under Pipeline Segment 4B's fixed price.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B: contract price $750,000 (SAR 2,812,500); original budgeted cost (BAC)
$600,000 (SAR 2,250,000) — a planned margin of $150,000 (20.0% of contract price). Partway through
execution, an unforeseen ground condition (soft strata requiring additional stabilisation) increases the
forecast final cost by $45,000. Under the fixed-price allocation, this condition does not entitle Meridian to
a price adjustment — it is exactly the kind of quantity/productivity risk a lump-sum contract places on the
contractor.

*Required.* (a) The revised forecast cost (EAC). (b) The revised margin, in dollars and as a percentage of
contract price.

*Working.*
(a) `Revised EAC = 600,000 + 45,000 = 645,000`
(b) `Revised margin = 750,000 − 645,000 = 105,000`
    `Revised margin (%) = 105,000 / 750,000 = 14.0%`

*Answer.* **Revised EAC $645,000; revised margin $105,000 (14.0% of contract price)** — down from the
planned $150,000 (20.0%).

*Interpretation.* The $45,000 cost increase erodes margin dollar for dollar, exactly as the fixed-price
allocation implies. This is why, on a lump-sum contract, the professional's job is not to ask "did the client
pay us enough?" (the price is fixed) but "is our forecast cost still inside the price we agreed?" — a
question EAC (KA 6.3) answers directly, and one where the earlier the answer is known, the more options
(re-sequencing, mitigating, or a genuine change-order claim if the condition is in fact compensable under the
contract, KA 7.2) remain open.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reading a lump-sum contract as risk-free for the client — a client who under-specifies scope invites either
  an inflated risk premium in the price or disputes later over what was actually included.
- Assuming every cost increase on a fixed-price contract must simply be absorbed — some conditions (e.g.,
  genuinely unforeseeable physical conditions, or client-instructed changes) may be compensable under the
  specific contract's clauses; classifying a cost increase correctly (contractor risk vs. compensable event)
  is a contract-administration judgement (KA 7.2), not an automatic assumption either way.
- Losing sight of the difference between the *cost baseline* (BAC, an internal budget) and the *contract
  price* (a revenue figure) — Example 7.1.2 only works because the two are kept distinct; conflating them
  makes margin invisible.

**AI in this topic.** Predictive EAC and early-warning tools (cross-reference KA 6.3, KA 13.5) can flag
margin erosion earlier than a monthly reporting cycle, which is genuinely valuable when margin is the entire
residual bearing risk. Such tools cannot determine whether a rising cost trend reflects contractor-side risk
that must simply be absorbed, or a compensable event the contractor is entitled to claim for — that
classification requires reading the actual contract terms and the facts on the ground, a professional
judgement no forecasting model can make on its own.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.3 (EAC forecasting); KA 5.4 (change control and cost impact); KA 11.1 (O2C billing
of Pipeline Segment 4B, which bills against this same fixed contract price); KA 2.2/7.5 (revenue is still
typically recognised over time as work is performed, regardless of pricing mechanism — the pricing mechanism
affects *how much* is billed, not whether IFRS 15's over-time criteria are met).

### 7.1.3 Admeasurement / remeasurement contracts (BoQ-based)

**Definition & purpose.** An **admeasurement (remeasurement) contract** prices work using fixed **rates**
agreed at award against **estimated (provisional) quantities**, with the final contract value determined by
the **actual quantities measured** as work is completed. FIDIC's *Red Book* (Conditions of Contract for
Construction) is the classic remeasurement form, historically used where the employer retains design
responsibility and quantities cannot be fixed with confidence at tender; under NEC terminology, this
corresponds broadly to a "priced contract with a bill of quantities." The full mechanics of pricing and
measuring a BoQ are covered in depth in KA 7.3 and are only introduced here at the level needed to place
remeasurement on the risk spectrum.

**The underlying principle.** A remeasurement contract deliberately splits the three risks differently from a
lump sum: the **rate** — dollars per unit of measured work — is fixed by the contractor's tender and does not
change, so **price and productivity risk stay with the contractor** (if it costs the contractor more per unit
to deliver the work than the rate it quoted, that shortfall is the contractor's). But the **quantity** of work
is only ever provisional at tender; the contractor is paid for what is *actually* measured, so **quantity
risk sits with the client**. This is the key distinction from lump-sum, where quantity risk sits with the
contractor.

**Formula.**

`Remeasured value (item) = Actual measured quantity × Agreed BoQ rate`

`Contract value = Σ (Remeasured value, across all bill items)`

**Example 7.1.3 — Remeasuring a trenching item on Pipeline Segment 5A.**

*Scenario.* Meridian is pricing a trenching item for Segment 5A's provisional BoQ at an agreed rate of
$145/m — a client-facing billing rate, distinct from the $100/m *internal* budgeted cost rate Meridian used
for Segment 4B's own trenching work package in its EVM baseline (KA 6.1, Example 6.1.4); the BoQ rate
includes overhead and margin the internal cost rate does not. The tender BoQ shows a provisional quantity of
1,200 m. Once trenching starts, the ground proves more variable than surveyed, and the actual measured
quantity comes to 1,380 m.

*Required.* (a) The provisional contract value for this item at tender. (b) The remeasured value once the
actual quantity is known. (c) The increase, and which party's risk it reflects.

*Working.*
(a) `Provisional value = 1,200 × 145 = 174,000`
(b) `Remeasured value = 1,380 × 145 = 200,100`
(c) `Increase = 200,100 − 174,000 = 26,100` (equivalently, `(1,380 − 1,200) × 145 = 180 × 145 = 26,100`)

*Answer.* **Provisional value $174,000; remeasured value $200,100 (SAR 750,375); increase $26,100.**

*Interpretation.* The $26,100 increase is entirely a quantity effect — 180 additional metres at the same,
unchanged $145/m rate — so it falls to the client to pay, exactly as the remeasurement allocation intends.
Had Meridian instead taken longer per metre than it costed for (a productivity shortfall), that inefficiency
would still be absorbed inside the fixed $145/m rate and would not increase the client's bill by a single
dollar — the rate, not the quantity, carries that risk.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Confusing a genuine quantity remeasurement (more of the *same defined* work, priced at the existing rate)
  with a **scope change** (different work, requiring a new or adjusted rate) — the latter goes through change
  control (KA 5.4), not simple remeasurement.
- Treating the tender BoQ quantities as a guarantee of the final contract value — they are explicitly
  provisional; a client budgeting only to the tender total, without contingency for remeasurement growth, is
  under-provisioned by design.
- Assuming remeasurement removes all client cost-control incentive on the contractor — the contractor still
  has every incentive to work efficiently *within* the fixed rate, even though it has little incentive to
  minimise the *quantity* it measures, which is exactly why independent measurement/certification (KA 11.1.4)
  matters as much here as anywhere else.

**AI in this topic.** Computer-vision, drone-survey and LiDAR-based quantity-measurement tools can produce an
independent, rapid estimate of completed quantities (cross-reference KA 11.1.4) — a useful cross-check
feeding into a remeasurement valuation. These tools do not replace the contractual measurement and
certification role (commonly an Engineer's function under FIDIC-style forms) and can themselves be affected
by survey conditions, occlusion or calibration error; a professional must reconcile any AI-derived quantity
against the physical measurement before it is agreed and paid.

**Cross-references.** KA 7.3 (BoQ structure, pricing and measurement, in full depth — not repeated here);
KA 6.1 (the internal EV cost rate contrasted with the BoQ billing rate above); KA 5.4 (change control for
genuine scope changes, as distinct from quantity remeasurement).

### 7.1.4 Cost-reimbursable contracts (CPFF, CPPC, CPIF and cost-plus with a GMP)

**Definition & purpose.** A **cost-reimbursable (cost-plus) contract** pays the contractor its actual,
allowable costs, plus a **fee** representing profit and, typically, un-recovered overhead. The definition of
"allowable cost" is set out in the contract itself and is central to administering this type — costs outside
that definition are not reimbursed. Four common fee structures, in increasing order of how tightly they
constrain the client's ultimate cost exposure:

- **Cost-plus-fixed-fee (CPFF)** — a fee agreed as a fixed dollar amount at the outset, unaffected by how
  the actual cost outturn compares with the original estimate (though it may be formally adjusted for an
  authorised scope change).
- **Cost-plus-percentage-of-cost (CPPC)** — a fee calculated as a fixed percentage of actual allowable cost,
  so the fee grows automatically as cost grows.
- **Cost-plus-incentive-fee (CPIF)** — a base fee plus (or minus) an incentive adjustment tied to performance
  against a target, using a share-ratio mechanism closely related to the target-cost pain/gain approach
  developed fully in 7.1.6.
- **Cost-plus with a Guaranteed Maximum Price (GMP)** — actual cost plus fee, but capped at an agreed maximum;
  if actual cost plus fee would exceed the GMP, the contractor absorbs the excess, functioning as a
  price ceiling closely analogous to the pain-cap mechanism in 7.1.6.

**The underlying principle — and why CPPC is a warning, not a recommendation.** Under CPFF, the contractor's
absolute fee is fixed, so it has a genuine, if mild, incentive to control cost: a shorter, cheaper job means
the same fee earned sooner, for less effort. Under CPPC, by contrast, the contractor's fee *rises* the more
it spends — the incentive runs entirely the wrong way, which is why CPPC is widely regarded as poor
commercial practice and is prohibited or restricted under many public and institutional procurement policies.

**Formula.**

`CPFF payment = Actual allowable cost + Fixed fee` (fee amount does not vary with actual cost)

`CPPC payment = Actual allowable cost + (Fee % × Actual allowable cost)` (fee grows with actual cost)

`Cost-plus with GMP payment = MIN(Actual allowable cost + Fee, GMP)`

**Example 7.1.4 — CPFF vs. CPPC on Segment 5A's ground-investigation and enabling works.**

*Scenario.* Before Segment 5A's main works are even priced, Meridian is engaged to carry out early ground
investigation and enabling works — genuinely open-ended scope where quantities and even the required methods
are not yet known, making a cost-reimbursable basis appropriate for this early package (developed further in
the 7.1.8 mini-case). At award, the estimated cost is $150,000 and a 10.0% fee rate is agreed. The actual
allowable cost, once the works are complete, comes to $180,000 — a 20.0% overrun against the original
estimate.

*Required.* (a) The total payment under a CPFF basis (fee fixed at 10.0% of the *original estimate*). (b) The
total payment under a CPPC basis (fee at 10.0% of *actual* cost). (c) Comment on the incentive each creates.

*Working.*
(a) `CPFF fixed fee = 10.0% × 150,000 = 15,000`
    `CPFF total payment = 180,000 + 15,000 = 195,000`
(b) `CPPC fee = 10.0% × 180,000 = 18,000`
    `CPPC total payment = 180,000 + 18,000 = 198,000`
(c) Fee under CPPC ($18,000) exceeds fee under CPFF ($15,000) by exactly $3,000 — the entire fee increase is
    caused by, and rewards, the cost overrun itself.

*Answer.* **CPFF: $195,000 total (fee $15,000, fixed). CPPC: $198,000 total (fee $18,000, rising with
cost).**

*Interpretation.* Under CPFF, Meridian's fee, once earned, does not grow if costs run over — indeed, its
*effective* fee percentage falls as cost rises (`15,000 / 180,000 = 8.3%`, against the 10.0% rate originally
priced against the estimate), a real, if modest, incentive to control cost. Under CPPC, Meridian would be
paid *more* for the same overrun — the fee mechanism actively rewards poor cost control, which is exactly why
a client should avoid it and, where cost-reimbursable pricing is genuinely needed (as it is here, on
open-ended early investigation scope), prefer CPFF, CPIF, or a capped GMP structure instead.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using cost-reimbursable pricing on scope that is, in fact, well enough defined for a lump sum or
  remeasurement basis — cost-reimbursable pricing gives the client the least cost-control leverage of any
  mechanism and should be reserved for genuinely uncertain, hard-to-scope work.
- Failing to define "allowable cost" precisely in the contract — vague allowability rules invite dispute over
  every reimbursement claim and undermine the audit trail a cost-reimbursable contract depends on.
- Assuming a GMP cap removes all client cost risk — it caps the client's *maximum* exposure, but the client
  still bears full cost risk for every dollar below the cap, exactly as under uncapped cost-reimbursement.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based cost-audit tools can scan a contractor's cost submissions against a contract's
allowable-cost definition far faster than manual audit, flagging items that appear non-allowable (mirroring
the P2P three-way match logic in KA 11.2) for review. They cannot make the underlying *judgement* of whether
a borderline cost genuinely meets the contract's specific allowability criteria — that remains a
contract-administration decision requiring the actual contract wording and the facts of the claim.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.4 (cost provisions and estimation, relevant to sizing allowable-cost claims);
KA 2.2/7.5 (reimbursable-cost billing aligns naturally with an input-method, percentage-of-completion revenue
approach); KA 11.2 (the P2P three-way match, the control discipline behind verifying allowable cost); 7.1.6
(CPIF's incentive mechanism, developed fully as target-cost pain/gain share).

### 7.1.5 Unit-rate and time-and-materials (T&M) contracts

**Definition & purpose.** **Unit-rate** pricing pays a fixed rate per unit of defined output (closely related
to, and often indistinguishable in practice from, a single remeasured BoQ item — 7.1.3). **Time-and-materials
(T&M)** pricing — often called **dayworks** on a construction site — pays for labour at an hourly or daily
rate, plant/equipment at a daily or weekly rate, and materials at cost plus an agreed markup percentage.
Both mechanisms share a defining trait: the *quantity of effort* (hours worked, days of plant used, units
produced) is not known with confidence at the outset, so it is measured and paid for as incurred, commonly
subject to a **not-to-exceed (NTE)** ceiling as a client cost-control device.

**The underlying principle.** T&M and unit-rate pricing sit close to remeasurement on the risk spectrum: the
**rate** (per hour, per day, per unit) is fixed by agreement, so price/productivity risk within that rate
stays with the contractor — but the **quantity of effort** needed is the client's risk, since payment simply
follows however many hours, days or units are actually recorded. In practice, T&M/dayworks pricing is used
less often as the primary mechanism for an entire contract and more often as the mechanism for pricing
individual **variations** on a contract that is otherwise lump-sum or remeasured — precisely because a
variation's scope is, by definition, not covered by the contract's existing rates.

**Formula.**

`T&M value = Σ (Hours worked × Labour rate) + Σ (Plant-days × Plant rate) + (Materials at cost × (1 + Markup %))`

**Example 7.1.5 — Pricing a dayworks variation on Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* Midway through Pipeline Segment 4B's fixed-price contract, an unforeseen utility must be
relocated — work outside the original defined scope, priced under the contract's dayworks schedule rather
than at any existing BoQ rate. The instructed work takes a five-person crew 3 days at $60/hour (8-hour days),
uses one excavator for 3 days at $450/day, and consumes $2,000 of materials at cost, marked up 15.0% per the
dayworks schedule.

*Required.* The total value of the dayworks variation.

*Working.*
`Labour = 5 workers × $60/hr × 8 hr/day × 3 days = 7,200`
`Plant = 3 days × 450/day = 1,350`
`Materials = 2,000 × (1 + 15.0%) = 2,300`
`Total = 7,200 + 1,350 + 2,300 = 10,850`

*Answer.* **Dayworks variation value: $10,850** (SAR 40,688).

*Interpretation.* Once approved through change control (KA 5.4), this $10,850 is added to Pipeline Segment
4B's contract price — a rare, contractually agreed exception to the otherwise fixed lump sum, precisely
because the relocated utility falls outside the originally priced scope entirely. Note that the T&M mechanism
here prices *only the variation*; the underlying contract remains lump-sum for everything else — a single
contract very often blends more than one pricing mechanism across its life.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Allowing dayworks/T&M claims to be prepared and approved by the same party that executed the work, with no
  independent check on hours, plant usage or material quantities actually consumed — the same
  three-point-reconciliation discipline used to control billing generally (KA 11.1.5) applies with particular
  force here, since T&M has no fixed total to sanity-check against.
- Treating a not-to-exceed ceiling as optional guidance rather than a firm control — without one, T&M pricing
  has essentially no client cost-control mechanism beyond after-the-fact audit.
- Using T&M/dayworks pricing for work that could, and should, have been competitively priced as a lump sum or
  unit rate before it started — T&M is a fallback for genuinely un-scoped work, not a convenience.

**AI in this topic.** RPA and GenAI tools can draft a dayworks sheet or T&M claim directly from field
data-capture apps (timesheets, plant logs, material dockets), speeding preparation materially (cross-reference
KA 13.5). They cannot verify that the hours, plant and materials claimed were actually authorised and
genuinely worked — independent site sign-off remains essential before any T&M claim is certified, exactly the
control gap 11.1.5's three-point reconciliation exists to close.

**Cross-references.** KA 5.4 (change control and cost impact, for variations priced this way); KA 7.4
(interim valuations, where a dayworks variation is included in an application for payment); KA 11.1.5 (the
three-point reconciliation control, applied here to T&M claims specifically).

### 7.1.6 Target-cost contracts with pain/gain share

**Definition & purpose.** A **target-cost contract** sets an agreed **target cost** for the work, pays the
contractor its **actual cost plus a fee**, and then adjusts that payment by sharing the difference between
target and actual cost — the **pain/gain** — between client and contractor according to a pre-agreed **share
ratio**. Under NEC terminology, this corresponds to "target contract" pricing options (a target with an
activity schedule, or a target with a bill of quantities where quantities are also remeasured); FIDIC forms
can be, and increasingly are, adapted to a target-cost/pain-gain structure through bespoke or particular
conditions, though (unlike NEC) it is not one of FIDIC's core published mechanisms.

**The underlying principle.** Target-cost pricing exists precisely for scope like Segment 5A's main works —
where genuine, shared uncertainty makes both a lump sum (forcing the contractor to price in a large,
possibly wasted, risk premium) and pure cost-reimbursement (giving the contractor little incentive to control
cost) unattractive. By sharing both the upside (an underrun) and the downside (an overrun) of the difference
between target and actual cost, target-cost pricing gives the contractor a genuine financial stake in
controlling cost — unlike CPPC's perverse incentive (7.1.4) — while protecting the client from unlimited
cost exposure, provided the deal also includes a **ceiling (or "pain cap")** mechanism, developed in the
worked example below.

**Formula.**

`Gain or Pain = Target cost − Actual cost` (positive = gain/underrun; negative = pain/overrun)

`Contractor's share of gain = Share % × Gain` (as negotiated — uncapped or capped, per the specific deal)

`Contractor's share of pain, up to the overrun cap = Share % × min(|Pain|, Overrun cap)`
`Contractor's share of pain, beyond the overrun cap = 100% × max(0, |Pain| − Overrun cap)`

`Final price payable to contractor = Actual cost + Fee + Contractor's share of gain − Contractor's share of pain`

`Ceiling price (client's maximum exposure) = Target cost + Fee + (Share % × Overrun cap)`

*(To avoid confusion with EVM's Actual Cost, AC/ACWP — KA 6.1 — this KA always writes a target-cost
contract's outturn cost as "actual cost" in full, never abbreviated to AC.)*

**Example 7.1.6 — Pain/gain settlement for Pipeline Segment 5A's main construction package.**

*Scenario.* Following the enabling works (Example 7.1.4), Meridian and the client agree a target-cost
structure for Segment 5A's main construction package: target cost $500,000 (SAR 1,875,000); fee 10.0% of
target cost = $50,000 (target price $550,000); a 50:50 (Meridian:client) pain/gain share ratio; and, to
protect the client, an **overrun cap of $90,000** above target cost, beyond which Meridian bears 100% of any
further overrun. Gain (underrun) in this illustrative deal is shared 50:50 with no cap.

*Required.* Compute the final price payable to Meridian, and its effective fee, under three independent
actual-cost outcomes: (a) actual cost $460,000; (b) actual cost $560,000; (c) actual cost $650,000.

*Working.*

**(a) Actual cost $460,000 (a gain).**
`Gain = 500,000 − 460,000 = 40,000`
`Meridian's share of gain = 50.0% × 40,000 = 20,000`
`Final price = 460,000 + 50,000 + 20,000 = 530,000`
`Effective fee = 50,000 + 20,000 = 70,000` (15.2% of actual cost, `70,000 / 460,000`)

**(b) Actual cost $560,000 (a pain, within the $90,000 overrun cap).**
`Pain = 560,000 − 500,000 = 60,000` (within the $90,000 cap)
`Meridian's share of pain = 50.0% × 60,000 = 30,000`
`Final price = 560,000 + 50,000 − 30,000 = 580,000`
`Effective fee = 50,000 − 30,000 = 20,000` (3.6% of actual cost, `20,000 / 560,000`)

**(c) Actual cost $650,000 (a pain, exceeding the $90,000 overrun cap).**
`Pain = 650,000 − 500,000 = 150,000` (exceeds the $90,000 cap by `150,000 − 90,000 = 60,000`)
`Meridian's share of pain, up to the cap = 50.0% × 90,000 = 45,000`
`Meridian's share of pain, beyond the cap = 100% × 60,000 = 60,000`
`Meridian's total absorption = 45,000 + 60,000 = 105,000`
`Final price = 650,000 + 50,000 − 105,000 = 595,000`
`Effective fee = 50,000 − 105,000 = (55,000)` — a **loss**, since `Final price 595,000 < Actual cost 650,000`

*Answer.* **(a) Final price $530,000 (fee $70,000, 15.2% of cost). (b) Final price $580,000 (fee $20,000,
3.6% of cost). (c) Final price $595,000 — below actual cost of $650,000, a loss of $55,000 to Meridian.**

*Interpretation.* Scenarios (a) and (b) show the pain/gain mechanism working as intended: Meridian is
rewarded for beating the target and penalised, but not ruined, for missing it moderately. Scenario (c) is the
crucial professional lesson: once actual cost breaches the $90,000 overrun cap, Meridian's final price is
capped at the **ceiling price** of $595,000 (`Target cost 500,000 + Fee 50,000 + Share % × Overrun cap
45,000`, confirmed by the formula), so every further dollar of overrun beyond the cap comes directly out of
Meridian's own margin — and can, as here, turn a positive fee into an outright loss. A target-cost contract's
"shared risk" is genuinely shared only *within* the negotiated cap; beyond it, the contractor's downside is
economically indistinguishable from a lump-sum contract's unlimited exposure. Note also, under IFRS 15, that
this pain/gain adjustment is a form of **variable consideration** (cross-reference KA 2.2), estimated and
constrained under the standard's variable-consideration rules rather than recognised only once the final
settlement is known.

- Believing the share ratio alone (e.g., "50:50") describes the contractor's real maximum risk — the *cap*,

- Applying the share ratio to the *wrong* base — pain/gain is always calculated on the difference between
  target and *actual* cost, not on the difference between target and *forecast* cost (though a forecast,
  via EAC-style methods, is exactly how the pain/gain outcome is usefully predicted in-flight — KA 6.3).
- Assuming every target-cost deal caps only the pain side — many real deals also cap the gain side
  symmetrically (a "collar"); this illustration deliberately leaves gain uncapped and states that assumption
  explicitly, because pain/gain terms are always a negotiated, contract-specific structure, not a fixed rule.

**AI in this topic.** Predictive-analytics and EAC-style forecasting tools (cross-reference KA 6.3) can

letting both parties manage a target-cost contract proactively rather than discover the settlement only at
the end. This is a genuinely valuable use of forecasting in a collaborative delivery model. It cannot decide
*how* the parties should respond to an emerging pain position (accelerate, de-scope, jointly absorb, or
otherwise) — that remains a joint commercial decision, and any AI-generated forecast is only as reliable as
the underlying actual-cost data feeding it.

(CPIF, the cost-reimbursable cousin of this mechanism).

### 7.1.7 Standard forms of contract: FIDIC, NEC, EPC and framework agreements (awareness level)

**Definition & purpose.** A **standard form of contract** is a published, pre-drafted set of contract
conditions that codifies roles, risk allocation, payment mechanics and dispute procedures, so that parties do
not have to draft every clause from scratch and so that a market of engineers, lawyers and contractors shares
a common vocabulary for administering the contract. This topic describes, **at awareness level and entirely
in the author's own words — never quoting clause text or numbering from any published edition** — the two
families of standard form most referenced internationally in infrastructure and engineering contracting:
**FIDIC** and **NEC**. Full contract administration under any of these forms (obligations, notices, claims,
liquidated damages, bonds and guarantees) is covered in KA 7.2; this topic covers only how each form relates
to the pricing mechanisms already introduced.

**The underlying principle.** Neither FIDIC nor NEC is a single contract; each is a **family** of forms or
options, and choosing among them is itself a risk-allocation decision — closely related to, but not
identical with, choosing a pricing mechanism (7.1.1).

**FIDIC** (published by the International Federation of Consulting Engineers) is best known for three
colour-named forms, distinguished chiefly by who carries design responsibility and how much risk transfers to
the contractor: the **Red Book** (Conditions of Contract for Construction) is the traditional design-bid-build
form, where the employer designs and the contractor constructs, typically priced by remeasurement against a
Bill of Quantities (7.1.3); the **Yellow Book** (Plant and Design-Build) shifts design responsibility to the
contractor, who builds to the employer's stated requirements, typically priced lump-sum against a schedule of
prices; and the **Silver Book** (EPC/Turnkey) transfers the most risk to the contractor — design, procurement,
construction and much of the site-data risk — in exchange for the greatest price and time certainty the
employer can obtain, almost always priced lump-sum.

**NEC** (published by the UK's Institution of Civil Engineers) is structured around a common core of clauses
plus a choice of six **main pricing options**, commonly labelled A through F, each corresponding directly to
one of this KA's pricing mechanisms: **Option A** (a priced contract with an activity schedule — broadly
lump-sum in character); **Option B** (a priced contract with a bill of quantities — remeasurement, 7.1.3);
**Option C** (a target contract with an activity schedule — target-cost pain/gain, 7.1.6); **Option D** (a
target contract with a bill of quantities — target-cost pain/gain combined with remeasurement); **Option E**
(a cost-reimbursable contract — 7.1.4); and **Option F** (a management contract, where the contractor is paid
a fee to manage packages of work let to others, rather than to execute the work itself).

**EPC (Engineering, Procurement and Construction) / turnkey** is not itself a standard form but a
**delivery/procurement strategy**: a single contractor takes full responsibility for design, procurement and
construction, handing the client a complete, operating facility — almost always priced lump-sum (commonly
under a FIDIC Silver Book-style form) because the client's primary objective is price and time certainty with
a single point of responsibility. A **framework (call-off) agreement** is a different kind of vehicle again:
an umbrella agreement, often covering a programme of similar work over several years, under which individual
**task orders (call-offs)** are issued and separately priced — each call-off can, in principle, use any of the
pricing mechanisms in this chapter, so a framework's existence says little about risk allocation until its
individual call-offs are examined.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assuming a form's name guarantees a specific risk allocation regardless of amendment — a heavily amended
  "Red Book" contract can, in practice, no longer allocate risk the way the unamended textbook description
  implies; the actual, amended clauses always govern, not the form's reputation.
- Confusing EPC (a delivery strategy) with any single named form — EPC/turnkey delivery is most often
  documented using a FIDIC Silver Book-style form, but the terms are not interchangeable.
- Treating a framework agreement as if it were itself a pricing mechanism — it is a governance/commercial
  vehicle for issuing task orders, each of which still needs its own pricing-mechanism decision.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-analytics/CLM tools (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 11.1.3) can classify which
standard form and pricing option a drafted contract most closely resembles and extract its key terms — useful
triage across a large contract portfolio. They cannot verify that a heavily bespoke amendment has not
materially altered the risk allocation the form's name would normally suggest; only a professional reading
the fully amended document can confirm what has actually been agreed.

### 7.1.8 Choosing a contract type: risk allocation, incentives and the cost-control regime

**Definition & purpose.** This topic draws the chapter together: given a specific scope, how should a project
controls professional recommend a contract type? The choice is driven primarily by **how well the scope and
quantities are actually known** at the point of pricing, **how much risk each party is willing and able to
carry**, and **how strongly the parties want to incentivise active cost control** during execution — not by
habit, market convention, or which form was used last time.

**The underlying principle — the contract type also determines the cost-control and change regime.** The
pricing mechanism chosen is not just about who is paid what; it determines which **cost-control tools matter
most** and how a **change** is administered:

- **Lump-sum:** EAC/margin monitoring (KA 6.3) is the primary tool, since margin absorbs all risk; changes
  must be tightly distinguished from contractor risk to avoid unentitled cost absorption or unentitled claims.
- **Remeasurement:** quantity tracking and independent measurement (KA 7.3, KA 11.1.4) are primary; changes
  in *quantity* are routine and self-adjusting, while changes in *scope* still need formal change control.
- **Cost-reimbursable:** allowable-cost audit and fee-structure discipline (7.1.4) are primary, since there is
  little automatic cost-control pressure from the pricing mechanism itself.
- **Target-cost:** joint EAC/pain-gain forecasting (7.1.6, KA 6.3) is primary, since both parties share a
  direct financial stake in the same forecast.

**Mini-case — recommending a contract type for Segment 5A's main construction package.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's commercial team must recommend a pricing mechanism for Segment 5A's main construction
package, drawing on what this chapter has already established about the scope: (1) Example 7.1.3 showed a
single trenching item's provisional quantity growing 15.0% once ground conditions were better understood; (2)
Example 7.1.4 showed the enabling works' actual cost overrunning its estimate by 20.0% for the same reason —
genuine, currently unquantifiable ground-condition uncertainty; and (3) both the client and Meridian want the
contractor genuinely incentivised to control cost once work begins, not merely reimbursed for whatever it
turns out to cost.

*Analysis.*
- **Lump-sum** would force Meridian to price a large risk premium into its bid for ground conditions neither
  party can currently quantify — inflating the client's price for risk that may never materialise — or, if
  Meridian underprices that risk to stay competitive, expose it to the kind of margin erosion quantified in
  Example 7.1.2, at a scale that could threaten the contract's viability. **Not recommended** for this
  package.
- **Pure remeasurement (BoQ)** addresses the *quantity* risk demonstrated in Example 7.1.3 well — the client
  pays for what is actually measured — but leaves cost/productivity risk on genuinely uncertain ground
  conditions almost entirely with Meridian at a rate fixed before the uncertainty is understood, and creates
  no shared incentive for the kind of active, joint cost management both parties say they want. **Partially
  addresses the risk, but not the incentive requirement.**
- **Pure cost-reimbursable (CPFF)** removes Meridian's cost risk almost entirely, leaving the client exposed
  to the full extent of the uncertainty demonstrated in Example 7.1.4, with only a mild cost-control
  incentive. Appropriate for **early, genuinely un-scoped work** (as already used for the enabling works) but
  not for the main package, where the scope is materially better defined once enabling works and further
  ground investigation are complete.
- **Target-cost with pain/gain share (Example 7.1.6)** shares the uncertain ground risk between both parties
  in a bounded, transparent way; gives Meridian a direct financial incentive to control cost (it keeps 50.0%
  of any saving); protects the client from open-ended exposure once the agreed overrun cap is reached; and
  matches the collaborative, share-the-uncertainty posture both parties have already signalled. **Recommended**
  for the main package.

*Conclusion.* Meridian's commercial team recommends **cost-reimbursable (CPFF) pricing for the remaining
ground-investigation and enabling scope** — already in use, and appropriate while quantities remain
genuinely unknown — **followed by target-cost pricing with a 50:50 pain/gain share and a $90,000 overrun
cap for the main construction package**, once enabling works and further investigation have narrowed (though
not eliminated) the ground-condition uncertainty. This sequencing — cost-reimbursable while scope is unknown,
moving to a shared-risk mechanism once it is better, but not perfectly, understood, and reserving lump-sum for
scope with genuinely low uncertainty (as Segment 4B's better-surveyed conditions supported) — illustrates the
chapter's central lesson: **the right contract type is not a fixed attribute of a "type" of project; it is a
considered match between how well the scope is known and how each mechanism allocates the risk of what is
still unknown.**

- Forgetting that the chosen contract type cascades into everything downstream: the change-control regime
  (KA 5.4), the interim-valuation mechanics (KA 7.4), and the revenue-recognition pattern (KA 2.2, KA 7.5) all
  take their cue from the pricing mechanism decided here.

### Key terms

- **Pricing mechanism** — the contractual rule converting work performed into an amount owed to the
  contractor; the central risk-allocation choice this KA addresses.
- **Lump-sum (fixed-price) contract** — a single, fixed total price for a defined scope, not adjusted for
  the contractor's actual cost.
- **Admeasurement (remeasurement) contract** — fixed rates against provisional quantities; final value set
  by actual measured quantities.
- **Bill of Quantities (BoQ)** — the priced schedule of measured items underlying a remeasurement (or,
  often, a lump-sum) contract's valuation (full treatment: KA 7.3).
- **Cost-reimbursable (cost-plus) contract** — payment of actual allowable cost plus a fee.
- **Cost-plus-fixed-fee (CPFF)** — a fee fixed in dollar terms, unaffected by the actual cost outturn.
- **Cost-plus-percentage-of-cost (CPPC)** — a fee calculated as a percentage of actual cost; creates a
  perverse cost-control incentive and is widely discouraged/prohibited.
- **Cost-plus-incentive-fee (CPIF)** — a base fee adjusted by a share-ratio incentive tied to a cost target.
- **Guaranteed Maximum Price (GMP)** — a cap on a cost-reimbursable contract's total payment.
- **Target-cost contract** — actual cost plus fee, adjusted by a shared pain/gain against an agreed target.
- **Pain/gain share** — the negotiated split, between client and contractor, of the difference between
  target and actual cost.
- **Overrun cap (pain cap) / ceiling price** — the point beyond which a target-cost contractor bears 100% of
  further overrun, capping the client's maximum exposure.
- **Time-and-materials (T&M) / dayworks** — payment for labour, plant and materials at agreed rates, for
  quantities of effort not fixed in advance.
- **Not-to-exceed (NTE)** — a ceiling placed on a T&M arrangement as a client cost-control device.
- **EPC (Engineering, Procurement and Construction) / turnkey** — a delivery strategy giving one contractor
  full responsibility for design, procurement and construction, typically priced lump-sum.
- **Framework (call-off) agreement** — an umbrella agreement under which individual task orders are issued
  and separately priced.
- **FIDIC Red/Yellow/Silver Book** — internationally used standard forms distinguished chiefly by design
  responsibility and risk transfer (Red: employer design/remeasurement; Yellow: contractor design/lump-sum;
  Silver: EPC/turnkey/lump-sum).
- **NEC Options A–F** — NEC's six main pricing options, each mapping to one of this KA's pricing mechanisms.
- **Quantity risk / price (rate) risk / productivity risk** — the three risk dimensions this KA uses to
  characterise every contract type's allocation.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 7.1.1 [topic 7.1.2, level: application — numerical]**
A fixed-price contract has a contract price of $900,000 and a budgeted cost of $700,000. An unforeseen risk
event increases the forecast cost by $50,000, with no client entitlement to a price adjustment. What is the
revised margin, in dollars and as a percentage of contract price?
A. **$150,000 (16.7%)** ✔
B. $200,000 (22.2%)
C. $100,000 (11.1%)
D. $150,000 (20.0%)
*Rationale:* Revised cost `= 700,000 + 50,000 = 750,000`; revised margin `= 900,000 − 750,000 = 150,000`;
`150,000 / 900,000 = 16.7%`. B ignores the cost increase entirely; C double-subtracts the increase; D
states the correct dollar margin but divides by the wrong base (revised cost, not contract price).

**MCQ 7.1.2 [topic 7.1.3, level: application — numerical]**
Under a remeasurement (BoQ) contract, a bill item is priced at $80/m³. The provisional bill quantity was
500 m³; the actual measured quantity is 620 m³. What is the increase in contract value attributable to this
item, and which party bears the risk that caused it?
A. **$9,600 increase; the client bears the quantity risk** ✔
B. $9,600 increase; the contractor bears the quantity risk
C. $49,600 increase; the client bears the quantity risk
D. $4,800 increase; the client bears the quantity risk
*Rationale:* `(620 − 500) × 80 = 9,600`; under remeasurement, quantity risk sits with the client, who pays
for whatever is actually measured at the fixed, unchanged rate (7.1.3). C reports the total remeasured value
(`620 × 80`) rather than the increase; D halves the correct figure; B misidentifies which party bears
quantity risk.

**MCQ 7.1.3 [topic 7.1.4, level: analysis]**
A contractor's fee is calculated as a fixed percentage of actual allowable cost (cost-plus-percentage-of-cost,
CPPC). Why is this fee structure generally regarded as poor commercial practice?
A. Because it caps the client's total cost exposure, underpaying the contractor
B. **Because the contractor's fee increases as its cost increases, removing any incentive to control cost**
   ✔
C. Because it requires a bill of quantities, which is administratively burdensome
D. Because it transfers all price and productivity risk to the contractor
*Rationale:* Under CPPC, fee rises automatically with cost (Example 7.1.4) — the incentive runs directly
against cost control. A is wrong (CPPC has no cap); C confuses CPPC with remeasurement; D is the opposite of
CPPC's actual effect, which leaves cost risk with the client.

**MCQ 7.1.4 [topic 7.1.6, level: application — numerical]**
A target-cost contract has a target cost of $400,000, a fixed fee of $40,000, and a 60:40 (contractor:client)
pain/gain share ratio with no cap on either side. Actual cost comes in at $360,000. What is the final price
payable to the contractor?
A. **$424,000** ✔
B. $400,000
C. $440,000
D. $384,000
*Rationale:* `Gain = 400,000 − 360,000 = 40,000`; `Contractor's share = 60.0% × 40,000 = 24,000`;
`Final price = 360,000 + 40,000 + 24,000 = 424,000`. B uses the target price and ignores the gain share; C
adds the full gain instead of only the contractor's share; D mistakenly subtracts a share instead of adding
it.

**MCQ 7.1.5 [topic 7.1.8, level: analysis]**
A scope has significant, genuinely unquantifiable ground-condition risk at the time of contract award, but
both client and contractor want the contractor incentivised to control cost once work begins. Which contract
type most directly balances these two conditions?
A. Cost-plus-percentage-of-cost (CPPC)
B. Lump-sum/fixed-price
C. **Target-cost with a pain/gain share** ✔
D. Pure remeasurement (BoQ), with no cost-sharing mechanism
*Rationale:* Target-cost shares the uncertain risk in a bounded, incentive-aligned way (7.1.6, 7.1.8). CPPC
removes any cost-control incentive; lump-sum forces an inflated risk premium (or unmanageable exposure) for
risk neither party can currently quantify; pure remeasurement addresses quantity risk only, not the
cost/productivity risk the question specifically raises.

**MCQ 7.1.6 [topic 7.1.7, level: recall]**
Which FIDIC form of contract is most associated with transferring the greatest design, procurement and
site-data risk to the contractor, typically priced lump-sum, and used where the employer wants maximum price
and time certainty?
A. The Red Book (Construction)
B. The Yellow Book (Plant & Design-Build)
C. **The Silver Book (EPC/Turnkey)** ✔
D. A framework agreement
*Rationale:* The Silver Book transfers the most risk to the contractor, including much site-data risk
(7.1.7). The Red Book keeps design with the employer and typically uses remeasurement; the Yellow Book sits
between the two; a framework agreement is a call-off vehicle, not a risk-allocation profile in itself.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using Example 7.1.2's method, recompute Meridian's revised margin (in dollars and as a percentage of
   contract price) on Pipeline Segment 4B if the unforeseen ground condition had instead added $90,000 to
   forecast cost.
2. Using Example 7.1.6's method, compute the final price payable to Meridian on Segment 5A's target-cost
   package if actual cost comes in at exactly $410,000.
3. Explain, in your own words, why a remeasurement (BoQ) contract leaves quantity risk with the client but
   keeps price and productivity risk with the contractor, and how this differs from a lump-sum contract's
   allocation of the same three risks.
4. Explain why, in Example 7.1.6(c), Meridian's final price ($595,000) is actually less than its actual cost
   ($650,000), and what this shows about the real financial risk a contractor retains under a "shared risk"
   contract once a cost overrun breaches the overrun cap.

**Answers**

1. `Revised EAC = 600,000 + 90,000 = 690,000`; `Revised margin = 750,000 − 690,000 = 60,000`;
   `Margin (%) = 60,000 / 750,000 = 8.0%` — down from the planned $150,000 (20.0%), confirming that a fixed
   price absorbs cost increases dollar for dollar until margin itself is exhausted.
2. `Gain = 500,000 − 410,000 = 90,000` (uncapped in this deal's gain-side terms, as stated in Example 7.1.6);
   `Meridian's share = 50.0% × 90,000 = 45,000`; `Final price = 410,000 + 50,000 + 45,000 = 505,000`.
3. Under remeasurement, the rate per unit is fixed by the contract, so if the contractor's actual cost per
   unit is higher or lower than it priced (through inefficiency, input-price changes, or difficult
   conditions), that difference is the contractor's — price and productivity risk stay with the contractor.
   The *quantity* of units needed, however, is only ever provisional at tender; final payment follows the
   quantity actually measured, so if more (or less) work is needed than assumed, the client's total payment
   moves accordingly — quantity risk stays with the client. Under a lump-sum contract, by contrast, the total
   price does not move regardless of the quantity eventually needed to complete the defined scope, so
   quantity risk shifts to the contractor as well, and the client buys a higher degree of price certainty in
   exchange for the contractor pricing a risk premium into the fixed sum.
4. Once actual cost passes the $90,000 overrun cap, Meridian's share of any further overrun is 100% — none
   of it is shared with the client — so Meridian's final price stops rising with actual cost altogether
   (it is capped at the $595,000 ceiling price), while its actual cost keeps rising. Every dollar of overrun
   beyond the cap therefore comes directly out of Meridian's own margin, and can turn what should have been a
   positive fee into an outright loss, exactly as in scenario (c) ($595,000 received against $650,000 spent,
   a $55,000 loss). This shows that a target-cost contract's risk-sharing is genuine only *within* the
   negotiated cap; beyond it, the contractor's downside exposure is economically no different from an
   unlimited, lump-sum-style exposure, and the ceiling — not the headline share ratio — is the true measure
   of the contractor's maximum retained risk.
