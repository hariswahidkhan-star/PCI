# Domain 7 — Contracts, Commercial Management, BoQ, Invoicing & Revenue

## KA 7.5 — Revenue recognition in the commercial cycle

Every number this Body of Knowledge has, until now, kept carefully separate finally has to be reconciled in
this Knowledge Area: the physical progress a site team reports (Domain 6), the amount a quantity surveyor
certifies for payment (KA 7.4, KA 11.1), and the revenue a set of financial statements actually recognises
(KA 2.2) are three different figures, measured for three different purposes — and a project controls
professional who cannot explain why they differ, and reconcile them when asked, has not fully understood any
of the three. KA 2.2 built the complete IFRS 15 five-step model in depth, using Silverline Infrastructure
LLC's Route 9 Flyover Bridge as its flagship over-time construction application; **this KA does not repeat
that model**. It takes the model as given and applies it specifically to the commercial cycle described in
KA 7.1–7.4 and KA 11.1 — the sequence of contract, measurement, certification, billing and collection that
turns a signed contract into cash — asking, at every stage, what revenue should be recognised, and how that
figure sits alongside the amount actually billed. The chapter continues Meridian Rail Contracting LLC's
**Pipeline Segment 4B** contract (KA 1.1, KA 3.2, KA 6.1–6.4, KA 11.1): a fixed-price rail-corridor
drainage/utility pipeline contract priced at **$750,000** (SAR 2,812,500 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 =
USD 1), against an internal cost budget (BAC) of **$600,000**, billed under 10.0% retention and 30-day
payment terms through the certified applications for payment already established in KA 11.1 (Application 1:
15.0% cumulative, $112,500 gross; Application 2: 40.0%, $300,000; Application 3: 55.0% at the Month-3 data
date, $412,500, net invoice $101,250; Application 4: 68.0%, $510,000), and through the Month-3 and Month-5
earned-value and forecasting data already established in KA 6.1–6.3 (Month 3: PV $270,000, EV $330,000, AC
$280,000; Month 5: EV $405,000, AC $506,250, CPI 0.80, SPI 0.75, and an **adopted Estimate at Completion of
$750,000** under EAC Method 2, KA 6.3.5). Every worked figure in this chapter has been independently
re-checked to reconcile to those already-published Application and EVM figures, and to the $150,000 (20.0%
of revenue) target margin implied at inception (KA 11.1.3) — including showing, honestly, that Meridian's
own Month-5 re-forecast has since eroded that target margin to nil, exactly consistent with the adverse
CPI/SPI trend Domain 6 already reported.

### 7.5.1 Revenue recognition patterns for construction and EPC contracts in the commercial cycle

**Definition and purpose.** Before any billed amount can be compared with recognised revenue (§7.5.3–7.5.4),
the contract's recognition *pattern* must be settled: does revenue accrue **over time**, in step with the
certified-progress cycle that generates each application for payment, or only **at a point in time**,
unconnected to the billing schedule until a single handover moment? KA 2.2.6 sets out the full test in
depth — three over-time criteria, described there in this Body of Knowledge's own words, and a fallback set
of point-in-time control-transfer indicators — and this topic does not repeat it. What it adds is the
commercial-cycle consequence: only a performance obligation satisfied **over time** generates the running
contract-asset/liability position this KA is built around (§7.5.4); a point-in-time obligation's single
invoice and single revenue entry simply coincide, and none of this KA's machinery is needed for it.

**The underlying principle.** A construction or EPC contract billed through a sequence of interim
applications for payment (KA 7.4, KA 11.1) is not, by that fact alone, an over-time contract — the billing
*pattern* is a symptom of the commercial arrangement, not proof of the accounting conclusion, which must be
tested on the contract's substance (KA 2.2.6). In practice, however, the same features of a contract that
make progressive billing commercially sensible — a bespoke asset, an enforceable right to interim payment
for work certified to date — are frequently the same features that satisfy over-time criterion (c). The two
tend to travel together, but a professional must verify this on each contract rather than assume it.

**Formula.** No new formula; the decision test from KA 2.2.6 applies unchanged: **over time if criterion (a)
OR (b) OR (c) is met; otherwise, point in time.**

**Example 7.5.1 — applying the over-time test to Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario/Required.* Assess Pipeline Segment 4B — a buried drainage/utility pipeline built into a specific
rail corridor's right-of-way — against the three over-time criteria (KA 2.2.6).

*Working.*

| Criterion | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefit as Meridian performs | No — a partially buried pipeline provides no standalone benefit to the client until substantially complete and connected |
| (b) Meridian's performance creates/enhances an asset the client controls as it is created | Arguably yes, in the loose sense that the pipeline is built into client-controlled corridor land — but, as with Silverline's Route 9 Flyover Bridge (KA 2.2.6), criterion (c) is the clearer, decisive test here |
| (c) No alternative use to Meridian, and an enforceable right to payment for performance completed to date | **Yes** — a pipeline built to this specific corridor's route, gradient and connection points has no realistic alternative use, and Meridian's contract entitles it to certified payment for progress completed even on termination for convenience — the very existence of the certified-applications-for-payment mechanism (KA 7.4, KA 11.1) is itself operational evidence of this enforceable right |

*Answer.* **Over time, on criterion (c)** — the same conclusion, and the same criterion, as Silverline's
Route 9 Flyover Bridge (KA 2.2.6).

*Interpretation.* Because Pipeline Segment 4B is satisfied over time, its progress must be measured
(§7.5.2), and the resulting revenue schedule must be reconciled against the certified billing schedule
throughout the contract's life (§7.5.3–7.5.4) — not only at final handover.

**Contrast — a bundled point-in-time item (mini-case).** Suppose Pipeline Segment 4B separately included a
factory-supplied specialist valve-chamber unit, priced at $45,000, requiring no significant site-specific
customisation and capable, before delivery, of being redirected to another buyer at modest cost, with no
enforceable right to progress payment for it specifically. This item fails all three over-time criteria and
is recognised **at a point in time**, on delivery and acceptance. Its single delivery invoice and its single
revenue entry coincide exactly — a useful reminder that even a predominantly over-time EPC contract can
bundle a genuinely point-in-time supply item, and that this KA's contract-asset/liability mechanics apply
specifically to the over-time elements, not automatically to every line on the contract.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Assuming every EPC or construction contract is automatically over-time because it
is *billed* progressively — the billing pattern is a symptom, not the test (KA 2.2.6). (2) Assuming a bundled
factory-supplied item recognised at a point in time still participates in the running contract-asset/
liability waterfall built in §7.5.4 — it does not; only the over-time obligation(s) do. (3) Treating the
industry ("infrastructure," "EPC") as determinative rather than the specific fact pattern of alternative use
and enforceable payment rights on *this* contract.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-clause classification tools can scan a portfolio of EPC and construction
contracts for "no alternative use" and "enforceable right to payment on termination" language, flagging
likely over-time candidates for review far faster than a manual first read (cross-reference KA 13.4). They
cannot themselves judge whether an asset's alternative use is *realistically* redirectable — that requires
technical/commercial understanding of the specific asset and market, which remains the professional's
judgement to confirm before the classification is relied upon.

**Cross-references.** KA 2.2.6 (the full over-time test, in depth); KA 7.1 (contract types and how they
shape enforceable payment rights); KA 7.2 (contract administration); §7.5.2–§7.5.4 (what follows once a
performance obligation is confirmed over-time).

### 7.5.2 Measuring progress in the commercial cycle: input (cost-to-cost) vs the certified billing basis

**Definition and purpose.** KA 2.2.7 sets out the two families of progress-measurement method — input
methods (most commonly cost-to-cost) and output methods (units, milestones, survey, or straight-line) — and
is not repeated here. This topic applies the input method specifically to the commercial-cycle reality that
the progress figure used to *bill* a client (an independently certified, typically output-flavoured
measurement against the Bill of Quantities, KA 7.3–7.4, KA 11.1.4) is not automatically the same figure, or
even the same *kind* of measurement, as the one Meridian's finance function uses to *recognise revenue*. Both
can be entirely correct on their own terms and still diverge.

**The underlying principle.** Cost-to-cost ties revenue to the cost ledger — auditable, but a proxy that
assumes resources are consumed in reasonable proportion to value delivered (KA 2.2.7). Certified/output
progress ties billing to independently verified physical measurement, which protects the client but need not
move in lockstep with cost efficiency. The gap between the two is not a defect in either measure; it is the
structural reason the commercial cycle produces a contract asset or liability at all (§7.5.3–7.5.4).

**Formula.** As established in KA 2.2.7, applied cumulatively:

`% complete (cost-to-cost) = Costs incurred to date / Total estimated cost (as currently forecast)`
`Revenue recognised, cumulative = % complete × Contract price`

**Example 7.5.2 — Pipeline Segment 4B: cost-to-cost revenue and margin across periods.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B: contract price $750,000; BAC $600,000. At the Month-3 data date, total
estimated cost is unrevised (still $600,000, KA 6.1) and costs incurred to date are $280,000 (KA 6.1). By the
Month-5 data date, Meridian's cost-to-complete review has re-forecast total estimated cost upward: KA 6.3.3
and KA 6.3.5 already establish that the project controls team has **adopted an EAC of $750,000** (EAC Method
2, `BAC/CPI = 600,000/0.80`) as the reporting basis, on a well-supported, stable three-period CPI trend —
this KA takes that adopted figure as the total-estimated-cost input for cost-to-cost revenue recognition from
Month 5 onward, consistent with KA 2.2.12's own guidance that the *same* re-forecast cost-to-complete figure
should drive both the EAC and the IFRS 15 percentage complete. Costs incurred to date at Month 5 are $506,250
(KA 6.2–6.3). For this chapter's clean closing illustration, the Month-5 adopted EAC of $750,000 is assumed
to prove accurate at final completion, with no further re-forecast — an explicit simplifying assumption,
flagged for SME verification.

*Required.* Prepare the revenue, cost and margin schedule across three periods (inception to Month 3; Month
3 to Month 5; Month 5 to completion), and reconcile to the contract total.

*Working — Month 3 (no revision yet).*
`% complete = 280,000 / 600,000 = 46.7%`
`Revenue, cumulative = 46.7% × 750,000 = 350,000`

*Working — Month 5 (total estimated cost re-forecast to the adopted EAC, $750,000).*
`% complete = 506,250 / 750,000 = 67.5%`
`Revenue, cumulative = 67.5% × 750,000 = 506,250`

*Working — Completion.*
`% complete = 750,000 / 750,000 = 100.0%`
`Revenue, cumulative = 750,000`

*Answer — the full schedule.*

| Period | Revenue, period ($) | Cost of revenue, period ($) | Margin, period ($) | Margin % of period revenue | Revenue, cum. ($) | Cost, cum. ($) | % complete, cum. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P1: Inception–Month 3 | 350,000 | 280,000 | 70,000 | 20.0% | 350,000 | 280,000 | 46.7% |
| P2: Month 3–Month 5 | 156,250 | 226,250 | (70,000) | (44.8%) | 506,250 | 506,250 | 67.5% |
| P3: Month 5–Completion | 243,750 | 243,750 | 0 | 0.0% | 750,000 | 750,000 | 100.0% |
| **Total** | **750,000** | **750,000** | **0** | **0.0%** | | | |

*Reconciliation check.* `Total revenue = 350,000 + 156,250 + 243,750 = 750,000 ✓ (= contract price)`.
`Total cost = 280,000 + 226,250 + 243,750 = 750,000 ✓ (= the adopted, final-actual EAC)`. `Total margin =
70,000 − 70,000 + 0 = 0 ✓`.

*Interpretation.* This is a genuine, honest margin-erosion story, not a flat one: the $150,000 (20.0%)
target margin implied at Pipeline Segment 4B's inception (KA 11.1.3) held for the first period, then was
**entirely wiped out** in the second period — Period 2 alone shows a $70,000 **loss**, driven by the same
adverse CPI/SPI trend KA 6.2–6.3 already reported (CPI falling from a favourable 1.18 at Month 3 to an
unfavourable 0.80 by Month 5) — leaving the contract, on Meridian's own adopted forecast, recognising **zero
cumulative margin overall**. This is not a separate, contradictory story from Domain 6's EAC/VAC treatment;
it is the same deterioration, viewed through the revenue-recognition lens rather than the cost-forecasting
lens, and the two must always tell a consistent story on a well-controlled contract (KA 2.2.12's own pitfall
#3, restated operationally here).

**Example 7.5.2b — why the cost-to-cost percentage and Meridian's own EV percentage converge only once
re-forecasting catches up.** At Month 3, EV (KA 6.1) is $330,000 = 55.0% of BAC, and CPI = EV/AC =
330,000/280,000 ≈ 1.18. Since `AC = EV/CPI`, and total estimated cost is still the unrevised BAC at this
date:

`% complete (cost-to-cost) = AC/BAC = (EV/CPI)/BAC = (EV/BAC)/CPI = EV% / CPI = 55.0% / 1.18 ≈ 46.7%`

— matching Example 7.5.2 exactly. At Month 5, once total estimated cost is re-forecast to the adopted EAC
(`BAC/CPI`, Method 2):

`% complete (cost-to-cost) = AC / (BAC/CPI) = (AC × CPI) / BAC = EV / BAC = EV% = 67.5%`

— the cost-to-cost percentage **collapses to exactly Meridian's own EV percentage** once the total-estimated-
cost input is kept current with the same re-forecast discipline used for the EAC. *Interpretation.* This is
precisely the integration KA 2.2.12 calls for: two "different" progress measures (cost-to-cost input method;
internal EV) converge exactly whenever the same re-forecast connects them, and diverge — as they visibly did
at Month 3 (46.7% vs 55.0%) — only when one of them is left running on a stale estimate. This is the
single clearest, most concrete illustration in this KA of why re-forecasting discipline (KA 6.3) is not
merely a cost-control housekeeping matter: it directly changes recognised revenue.

### 7.5.3 Amounts billed vs revenue recognised: why they diverge

**Definition and purpose.** For this KA, **amounts billed** means the cumulative *gross* certified value
invoiced to the client to date (before retention deduction) — the same basis KA 2.2.11 uses, and the same
figure that would be credited to "Contract revenue" under KA 11.1.6's simplified O2C journal entry.
**Revenue recognised** means the cumulative amount produced by the entity's selected IFRS 15 measurement
method (§7.5.2). The two need not be, and on Pipeline Segment 4B are not, the same number at any interim
date.

**The underlying principle.** At least five distinct drivers can separate billed amounts from recognised
revenue on a single contract, and a professional should be able to name which one (or ones) explain a given
gap rather than treat any divergence as an undifferentiated anomaly:

1. **Measurement-basis effect** — cost-to-cost (input) revenue running ahead of, or behind, the certified
   physical/output progress used for billing (§7.5.2).
2. **Timing-lag effect** — the interval between a period's progress and its certification/invoice date (KA
   11.1.1's Application-3 cycle: certified Day 0, invoiced Day 2).
3. **Unbilled variable consideration** — a constrained but not-yet-certified claim, variation or incentive
   already reflected in the transaction price (§7.5.5).
4. **Modification effect** — a contract change reflected in revenue (via a cumulative catch-up, KA 2.2.10)
   before it is reflected in the billing schedule.
5. **Advance/mobilisation billing** — a payment billed ahead of any performance (§7.5.6).

**Formula.** No new formula; the canonical identity from §7.5.4 (`Contract asset/(liability) = Revenue
recognised − Amounts billed`) is what each of the five drivers, individually or in combination, pushes away
from zero.

**Example 7.5.3 — diagnosing the Month-3 gap.**

*Scenario/Required.* At the Month-3 data date, Pipeline Segment 4B's revenue recognised (cumulative,
cost-to-cost, Example 7.5.2) is $350,000; cumulative amount billed (Application 3, gross certified, KA
11.1.5) is $412,500 — a gap of $62,500. Attribute the gap to its driver(s).

*Working.* At Month 3: no contract modification has occurred (driver 4 — nil); no mobilisation advance
exists on this contract as billed (driver 5 — nil); no claim or variable consideration has yet been raised
(driver 3 — nil, introduced only in §7.5.5's later mini-case); the timing lag between certification and
invoice (KA 11.1.1) affects *when* cash follows the invoice, not the certified/billed amount itself (driver
2 — not a revenue-vs-billing driver at this data date). By elimination, the entire $62,500 gap is
attributable to driver 1, the measurement-basis effect:

`Gap attributable to measurement basis = (Certified/billed % − Cost-to-cost %) × Contract price`
`= (55.0% − 46.7%) × 750,000 = 8.333% × 750,000 = 62,500`

*Answer.* **The full $62,500 gap is explained by the measurement-basis effect alone** — cost-to-cost revenue
recognition (46.7% complete) running behind the certified billing basis (55.0% complete) at Month 3.

*Interpretation.* This is the value of driver-by-driver diagnosis: a $62,500 gap that cannot be explained is
a control concern (a coding error, a missed modification, an unreconciled claim); a $62,500 gap that is
*fully* explained by one identified, expected driver is simply the normal operation of two different,
correctly applied measurement bases — precisely the distinction §7.5.4 formalises into the balance-sheet
treatment.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating any billed-vs-revenue gap as automatically an error requiring correction —
Example 7.5.3 shows a fully explained, entirely expected gap. (2) Failing to attribute a gap to its specific
driver(s), which then makes it impossible to explain the movement to a client, auditor or lender when asked.
(3) Conflating a contract asset/liability gap (a revenue-recognition and billing-basis question) with a cash-
collection problem (a distinct O2C matter, KA 11.1.6–11.1.7) — an unpaid but fully and correctly billed
invoice is not a contract asset/liability issue at all.

**AI in this topic.** Reconciliation/diagnostic tools can decompose a billed-vs-revenue gap into its
component drivers automatically from the underlying cost, certification, claims-register and modification-
log data, and can flag billing anomalies — an invoice raised with no matching certification, or a gap that
does not reconcile to any known driver — for review (cross-reference KA 13.5, and KA 11.1.9's continuous
controls monitoring). The decomposition is only as reliable as the certification, claims and modification
data feeding it, and a professional must confirm which drivers genuinely apply before relying on an
automated attribution — **AI proposes the decomposition; the professional disposes of which drivers are
real.**

**Cross-references.** KA 2.2.11 (the underlying accounting mechanics, not repeated here); KA 7.4, KA 11.1
(how amounts billed are generated); §7.5.4 (the formal contract asset/liability treatment); §7.5.5–§7.5.6
(drivers 3 and 5, developed in full).

### 7.5.4 Contract assets and contract liabilities in the commercial cycle: the billed-vs-recognised waterfall

**Definition and purpose.** KA 2.2.11 defines the contract asset (revenue recognised exceeds amounts billed,
conditional on further performance), the contract liability (amounts billed exceed revenue recognised,
in effect deferred revenue) and the receivable (an unconditional right, once billed) in full, and is not
repeated here. This topic builds the *operational*, multi-period waterfall for Pipeline Segment 4B, tying
directly to the Application figures already established in KA 11.1 — the same position a commercial or
project-controls team would, in everyday language, call **over-billing** (a contract liability) or
**under-billing** (a contract asset).

**The underlying principle.** As stated in this Body of Knowledge's canonical formula table:

`Contract asset/(liability), cumulative = Cumulative revenue recognised − Cumulative amount billed`

A **positive** result is a contract **asset**; a **negative** result is a contract **liability** of that
magnitude — the identical sign convention used in KA 2.2.11, applied here to the commercial-cycle data this
KA and KA 11.1 generate. Tracking this position matters operationally, not only for statutory presentation: a
growing contract liability is healthy for cash flow but must never be mistaken for margin — it is billed-but-
unearned revenue, not profit; a growing contract asset signals either a billing-cycle opportunity (the
contract may allow more aggressive certification) or a genuine cost-efficiency story that cash flow
forecasting (KA 3.5) should anticipate before the client is actually billed for it.

**Formula.** As above, computed period by period from the revenue schedule (§7.5.2) and the certified billing
schedule (KA 7.4, KA 11.1).

**Example 7.5.4 — the Pipeline Segment 4B billed-vs-recognised waterfall.**

*Scenario.* Using Example 7.5.2's revenue schedule and KA 11.1's already-published Application figures:

| Point | Revenue recognised, cum. ($) | Amount billed, cum. ($) | Contract asset/(liability), cum. ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inception | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Month 3 (Application 3, KA 11.1.5) | 350,000 | 412,500 | (62,500) |
| Month 5 (indicative only)* | 506,250 | 510,000 | (3,750) |
| Completion | 750,000 | 750,000 | 0 |

\* Application 4's cumulative certified value (68.0%, $510,000, KA 11.1.8) is shown here for indicative
comparison only — its certification date is not confirmed to coincide exactly with the Month-5 EVM data
date, unlike Application 3, which KA 11.1.1 explicitly ties to the Month-3 date. This point is flagged for
SME verification (see "For SME verification" below).

*Required.* Confirm the sign convention and interpret the position at each point.

*Working.* `Month 3: 350,000 − 412,500 = (62,500)` → contract **liability** $62,500 (SAR 234,375). `Month 5
(indicative): 506,250 − 510,000 = (3,750)` → a small indicative contract **liability** of $3,750, close to
par. `Completion: 750,000 − 750,000 = 0`.

*Answer.* **Month 3: contract liability $62,500. Month 5 (indicative): contract liability $3,750, near
breakeven. Completion: $0.**

*Interpretation.* Meridian billed ahead of cost-to-cost revenue throughout — the liability never flips to an
asset in this base scenario — but the *gap narrows sharply* from Month 3 to Month 5, because cost-to-cost
revenue caught up once the total-estimated-cost input was re-forecast (§7.5.2b): the biggest divergence
occurred earliest, when a stale cost estimate and the certified billing basis were furthest apart, and the
position all but normalises once both the cost estimate and the certification basis mature. §7.5.5 shows a
variant in which an unbilled claim pushes this position into an actual contract asset.

*The rigorous cumulative entry this implies (for illustration; in practice each application is posted
separately, as KA 11.1.6 shows for a single period).* KA 11.1.6's Example 11.1.6a posted Application 3's

process simplification, explicitly flagged there as not repeating "revenue-recognition detail per KA 2.2/7.5."
Once §7.5.2's cost-to-cost measurement is applied rigorously, the cumulative picture at Month 3 (Applications
1–3 together) differs:

| Account | Debit ($) | Credit ($) |
|---|---|---|
| Trade receivable (cumulative, Applications 1–3 net invoices: 101,250 + 168,750 + 101,250) | 371,250 | |
| Retention receivable (cumulative) | 41,250 | |
| Contract revenue (cumulative, cost-to-cost, to date) | | 350,000 |
| Contract liability | | 62,500 |

*Check:* `371,250 + 41,250 = 412,500 = 350,000 + 62,500 ✓`. Only **$350,000**, not the full $412,500 billed,
has actually been earned; the $62,500 gap is not an additional receivable — it is deferred revenue, to be
released as cost-to-cost progress catches up in later periods.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating "amount billed but not yet paid" and "revenue not yet earned relative to
billing" as the same balance — Example 7.5.4b shows three genuinely distinct balances coexisting (KA 2.2.11b
shows the same principle with two; retention adds a third here). (2) Assuming a contract liability is
"lost revenue" rather than revenue simply not yet earned — it will be recognised as cost-to-cost progress
catches up, exactly as §7.5.2 shows happening between Month 3 and Month 5. (3) Failing to reconcile the
contract asset/liability roll-forward independently to both the revenue schedule and the billing schedule
each period — an unreconciled break is a strong signal of a coding, certification-timing, or measurement
error (cross-reference KA 11.1.9's control matrix).

**AI in this topic.** Automated reconciliation tools can compute the contract asset/liability roll-forward
directly from the billing system (KA 7.4/11.1) and the cost-to-cost revenue sub-ledger, flag any period where
the two do not tie out, and produce the waterfall view in Fig 7.5.4 without manual spreadsheet work
(cross-reference KA 2.2.11, KA 13.5) — a strong automation candidate, and a natural extension of the same
billing-anomaly-detection category named for this domain (KA 13.5). It depends entirely on the underlying
revenue-recognition judgement (§7.5.1–§7.5.3) already having been correctly applied; an automated waterfall
built on a stale total-estimated-cost input is simply a fast, wrong waterfall, and the professional remains
accountable for the inputs before the roll-forward is relied upon.

**Cross-references.** KA 2.2.11 (the full accounting definitions and mechanics, not repeated here); KA 7.4,
KA 11.1 (the billing schedule feeding this waterfall, in full depth); §7.5.2 (the revenue schedule feeding
it); §7.5.5 (variable consideration as a further driver of this position).

### 7.5.5 Variable consideration in the commercial cycle: variations, claims and incentives

**Definition and purpose.** KA 2.2.4 and KA 2.2.8 set out the full mechanics of variable consideration — the
expected-value and most-likely-amount estimation methods, and the constraint — and are not repeated here.
This topic applies them to the fourth driver named in §7.5.3: an unresolved **variation, claim or incentive**
in the commercial cycle (KA 7.2, KA 5.4) that has **not yet been certified or billed** through the
applications-for-payment process (KA 7.4, KA 11.1), but that IFRS 15 nonetheless requires to be reflected,
once constrained, in the transaction price used for revenue recognition.

**The underlying principle.** An application for payment can only certify what the contract's measurement
rules currently allow (KA 11.1.4) — an unresolved claim, by definition, has not yet been agreed by the
client's engineer and therefore cannot appear on a certified application. IFRS 15 does not wait for that
agreement: once the constrained estimate of the claim passes the highly-probable-no-significant-reversal test
(KA 2.2.8), it is included in the transaction price and recognised proportionally to progress, exactly like
any other component of the price — creating a further, and often poorly understood, driver of the contract
asset position (§7.5.4): an unresolved claim can inflate a contract asset well before a single dollar of it
is ever invoiced.

**Formula.** As established in KA 2.2.8:

`Expected value = Σ (possible outcome amount × probability of that outcome)`
`Variable consideration included in transaction price = MIN(estimate under chosen method, the amount highly
probable not to result in a significant future revenue reversal)`

**Example 7.5.5 — a ground-conditions claim on Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* During trenching (work package WP2/WP3, KA 6.1's control-account breakdown), Meridian encounters
unforeseen rock requiring additional excavation beyond what was priced in the BoQ (KA 7.3), and submits a
claim under the contract's variation clause for **$60,000** of additional consideration. At the Month-5
reporting date the claim has been submitted but not yet agreed by the client's engineer. Based on outcomes on
comparable past claims with this client, Meridian's commercial team assesses three possible outcomes:

| Outcome | Probability | Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Full recovery | 65% | $60,000 |
| Partial recovery | 25% | $30,000 |
| Nil recovery | 10% | $0 |

*Required.* (a) The expected value. (b) The constrained variable consideration, given management's assessed
highly-probable-recoverable amount of $35,000. (c) The effect on Month-5 revenue recognition and on the
contract asset/liability position (§7.5.4), given the claim remains uncertified and therefore unbilled.

*Working.*
(a) `Expected value = (0.65 × 60,000) + (0.25 × 30,000) + (0.10 × 0) = 39,000 + 7,500 + 0 = 46,500`
(b) `Constrained variable consideration = 35,000` (management's documented, evidence-based judgement; the
    full $46,500 expected value is not used, consistent with the constraint's function, KA 2.2.8)
(c) `Revised transaction price = 750,000 + 35,000 = 785,000`
    `% complete (unaffected by the claim, still 67.5% per Example 7.5.2) = 67.5%`
    `Revised revenue, cumulative = 67.5% × 785,000 = 529,875`
    `Incremental revenue attributable to the claim = 529,875 − 506,250 = 23,625 (= 67.5% × 35,000 ✓)`

*Answer.* **Expected value $46,500; constrained variable consideration $35,000; incremental Month-5 revenue
from the claim, $23,625 — not the full $35,000.**

*Interpretation.* Two points matter operationally. First, the constraint is doing real work here: $11,500 of
the $46,500 expected value ($46,500 − $35,000) is judged too uncertain to include, exactly as KA 2.2.8's
Coastal Pipeline Extension example illustrated on a different contract. Second — and this is the point most
often missed in practice — even a constrained, already-approved-for-recognition claim is **not** recognised
in full immediately; it is spread across the remaining performance at the contract's own percentage complete,
exactly like any other component of the transaction price, unless it relates to a distinct obligation or
triggers a cumulative catch-up under KA 2.2.10 (neither applies here, since the claim relates to work within
the single, already-partly-satisfied over-time obligation).

**Example 7.5.5b — the effect on the contract-asset position.** Because the claim is uncertified, no
Application reflects it (§7.5.3's driver 3), so the amount billed is unchanged from Example 7.5.4's indicative
Month-5 figure ($510,000, Application 4). Revenue recognised, however, rises to $529,875:

`Contract asset/(liability), with the claim = 529,875 − 510,000 = 19,875`

The indicative Month-5 position **flips from a small liability of $(3,750) (Example 7.5.4, without the
claim) to a contract asset of $19,875** — a $23,625 swing, exactly the claim's incremental revenue effect.
*Interpretation.* This is the mechanism §7.5.4 warned about in the abstract, now worked in full: an unbilled,
constrained claim is a genuine, quantified driver of the contract-asset position, not a footnote — and a
commercial team monitoring only the certified-billing view of Pipeline Segment 4B (KA 11.1) would never see
this $19,875 asset at all, because it exists entirely on the revenue-recognition side of the ledger until the
claim is eventually agreed and certified.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Assuming an unresolved, uncertified claim has zero revenue effect until agreed —
wrong, once constrained it enters the transaction price regardless of billing status (Example 7.5.5b). (2)
Assuming a constrained claim amount is recognised in full immediately, rather than proportionally to
progress like the rest of the transaction price (the $23,625-not-$35,000 point above). (3) Failing to
reassess the constraint each period as the claim's resolution prospects change (KA 2.2.8's own pitfall,
directly applicable) — a claim initially constrained to $35,000 might reasonably be revised, up or down, as
the engineer's assessment progresses.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI and NLP tools can scan site correspondence, daily records and instructions to
identify and quantify a potential claim earlier than a manual commercial review would catch it, and
probability-modelling tools can support the expected-value calculation from a library of comparable past
claim outcomes (cross-reference KA 2.2.8, KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — the claims/variation analytics category named
specifically for this domain. Neither the underlying contractual entitlement (is this genuinely a valid
claim?) nor the constraint judgement is something these tools can determine; both remain commercial, legal
and accounting judgements that a documented AI-assisted estimate supports, but does not replace.

**Cross-references.** KA 2.2.4, KA 2.2.8 (the full variable-consideration and constraint mechanics, not
repeated here); KA 7.2 (claims and contract administration); KA 5.4 (change control and cost impact); §7.5.4
(the contract-asset impact developed here).

### 7.5.6 Significant financing components in billing structures: advances, retention and mobilisation

**Definition and purpose.** KA 2.2.9 sets out the full mechanics of a significant financing component — the
accretion of a liability or receivable at a discount rate, and the 12-month practical expedient — and is not
repeated here. This topic applies the test to the two billing structures a project controls professional
meets most often: **retention** (KA 7.2, KA 7.4, KA 11.1.7) and **advance/mobilisation payments**.

**The underlying principle.** KA 2.2.9's own pitfall (2) states the point this topic exists to reinforce for
a project controls audience specifically: **retention is normally performance-related security, not
financing.** Its purpose is to protect the client against defects, not to provide either party with the time
value of money — and applying a financing adjustment to a standard retention structure would misread the
standard's intent. An **advance or mobilisation payment**, by contrast, genuinely can be a financing
component if the gap between the advance and the performance it funds is expected to exceed roughly twelve
months — the same 12-month practical expedient KA 2.2.9 sets out generally.

**Formula.** As established in KA 2.2.9: `No adjustment required if the expected timing gap between payment
and performance is ≤ 12 months`; otherwise, the advance/liability is accreted at the applicable discount rate
until the revenue it relates to is recognised.

**Example 7.5.6 — a mobilisation advance on Pipeline Segment 4B (illustrative variant; not part of the
Application 1–4 schedule used elsewhere in this chapter).** Suppose, for this topic's illustration only,
Pipeline Segment 4B's contract had instead included a **$75,000 mobilisation advance**, paid to Meridian at
contract signing and recovered by equal deductions across the first six applications for payment — a
recovery period of roughly eight months on Meridian's typical certification cadence.

*Required.* Determine whether a significant financing component adjustment is required.

*Working.* `Expected timing gap between advance and the performance it funds ≈ 8 months ≤ 12 months`.

*Answer.* **No financing adjustment is required.** The advance is presented as a contract liability (an
even more direct over-billing position than the measurement-basis gap in §7.5.4) and is simply drawn down as
each subsequent application deducts its share, with **no separate interest income or expense** recognised.

*Interpretation.* Had the same $75,000 advance instead been scheduled for recovery only at the final
application, well beyond 12 months from signing, the practical expedient would no longer apply and the
accretion mechanics worked in full in KA 2.2.9's Example 2.2.9 (advance-payment case) would apply here in
the same direction — a genuine financing expense, separate from revenue, over the extended recovery period.
The contractual label ("mobilisation advance," not "loan") does not itself determine the answer; the expected
timing gap does.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating retention as a financing component by default — a common misconception in
project-controls practice specifically, directly addressed by KA 2.2.9's own pitfall (2) and restated here.
(2) Applying the 12-month practical expedient mechanically against the *contractual* recovery schedule
without considering the entity's *realistic* expectation — an advance nominally recoverable within 10 months
but, on the project's actual track record, more likely to run past 14 months due to slippage should be
assessed on the realistic expectation, not the paper schedule. (3) Assuming a payment structure is exempt
from the test simply because it is labelled an "advance" rather than a "loan" — substance, not the contract's
chosen vocabulary, governs.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-clause extraction tools can flag advance-payment and retention clauses across
a contract portfolio and pre-compute the expected timing gap against the actual billing/recovery schedule, to
triage which contracts warrant the financing-component test at all (cross-reference KA 2.2.9, KA 13.4) — the
contract-data-extraction category named for this domain. Judging the *realistic*, as opposed to contractual,
timing expectation remains a professional judgement an automated extraction cannot make.

**Cross-references.** KA 2.2.9 (the full financing-component mechanics, including the mirror deferred-
payment case, not repeated here); KA 7.2 (retention and advance terms within contract administration); KA
11.1.7 (retention release mechanics); KA 3.5 (cash flow forecasting, where advances and retention are
operationally significant).

### 7.5.7 Onerous contracts in the commercial cycle

**Definition and purpose.** KA 1.4.5 sets out the full IAS 37 onerous-contract test — including its formula
and a complete worked example on a different Meridian contract (a rail-signalling contract that turns onerous
at month 18) — and is not repeated here. This topic connects that test specifically to the commercial cycle:
what happens to **billing** when a contract turns onerous (nothing — certified applications continue on the
contract's own certified/BoQ-measurement basis regardless of the contractor's internal forecast position),
and how the resulting provision interacts with the revenue and contract-asset/liability figures already built
in this chapter.

**The underlying principle, restated briefly.** The ordinary cost-to-cost mechanism already reflects a
proportionate share of any total forecast loss on work done **to date**, because revenue is capped by a now-
lower (or negative) margin relationship between price and re-forecast cost. The onerous-contract provision
under IAS 37 captures only the unavoidable loss still to come on the **remaining** work; recognising the
*entire* forecast loss as a fresh provision on top of the loss already reflected through cost-to-cost revenue
would double-count it (KA 1.4.5's central pitfall).

**Formula.** As established in KA 1.4.5:

`Onerous contract provision required = MAX(0, − [Total forecast contract margin − Margin recognised to date])`

**Example 7.5.7 — testing Pipeline Segment 4B under a more conservative EAC.** Example 7.5.2 used the
Month-5 **adopted** EAC (Method 2, $750,000, KA 6.3.5) as the total-estimated-cost input, which produced
exactly nil cumulative margin — not onerous, but no remaining profit either. KA 6.3.4 also computed a more
conservative **Method 3 EAC of $831,250** for the same Month-5 data (reflecting both the adverse CPI and the
adverse SPI continuing into the remaining, schedule-critical work). This topic asks: what would the onerous-
contract position look like if Method 3, rather than Method 2, were the adopted forecast?

*Required.* Determine whether the contract would be onerous under the Method 3 EAC, and the provision
required.

*Working.*
`Total forecast contract margin = 750,000 − 831,250 = (81,250)` — onerous.
`% complete (cost-to-cost, EAC basis) = 506,250 / 831,250 = 60.9%`
`Revenue recognised to date = 60.9% × 750,000 = 456,766.92` (more precisely, `81/133 × 750,000`)
`Margin recognised to date = 456,766.92 − 506,250 = (49,483.08)`
`Remaining revenue = 750,000 − 456,766.92 = 293,233.08`
`Remaining forecast cost = 831,250 − 506,250 = 325,000.00`
`Remaining margin = 293,233.08 − 325,000.00 = (31,766.92)`
*Check:* `(49,483.08) + (31,766.92) = (81,250.00) ✓`, matching the total forecast contract margin.

*Answer.* **Under the Method 3 EAC, Pipeline Segment 4B would be onerous, requiring a provision of
$31,766.92** (SAR 119,126, rounded) — the unavoidable loss on the remaining work only; the $49,483.08 loss
on work already performed needs no separate provision, because it is already reflected through reduced
cost-to-cost revenue for the periods to date.

*Interpretation.* This is a genuinely important, if sobering, project-controls lesson: **the same contract,
on the same Month-5 data, is either exactly breakeven (Method 2, no provision) or onerous by $31,766.92
(Method 3), depending entirely on which EAC method the commercial and cost-forecasting team judges best fits
the evidence** (KA 6.3.6's own diagnostic sequence). The onerous-contract test is not a separate calculation
performed in isolation by the finance function; it inherits, directly and materially, whatever EAC judgement
Domain 6's forecasting discipline has already made. Note, too, that whichever EAC applies, Meridian's
Application 4 invoice (KA 11.1.8, certified at 68.0% under the contract's own BoQ measurement rules) is
**entirely unaffected** — the client owes what the contract and its certification process say for work
certified, irrespective of Meridian's own internal profitability forecast.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Recognising the entire $81,250 forecast loss as a fresh provision, double-counting
the $49,483.08 already reflected through reduced cost-to-cost revenue (KA 1.4.5's central, and most common,
error). (2) Assuming an onerous determination changes what can be billed — it does not; a live, cash-
generating billing cycle can sit on top of a loss-making contract right up to completion. (3) Treating the
choice between Method 2 (breakeven) and Method 3 (onerous) as a formality rather than the substantive,
evidence-based judgement KA 6.3.6 requires — the accounting consequence here (a $31,766.92 provision, or
none) turns entirely on that judgement.

**AI in this topic.** As KA 1.4.5 notes, predictive analytics scanning cost-report and EAC trends can raise
an early-warning flag when a contract's trajectory is heading toward an onerous position, often before a
formal re-forecast would surface it — genuinely valuable lead time for both the commercial mitigation route
(pursuing the ground-conditions claim in §7.5.5 is exactly this kind of mitigation) and the accounting
recognition itself. Given the direct financial-statement consequence of a false positive or negative — the
difference between no provision and a material one, as this example shows — any AI-flagged early warning is
a prompt for a professional cost-to-complete review, never a basis for booking or withholding a provision on
its own.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.4.5 (the full onerous-contract mechanics and formula, and the rail-signalling
worked example, not repeated here); KA 2.4 (IAS 37 generally); KA 6.3 (the EAC methods and their selection,
directly determinative here); KA 7.2 (claims as a mitigation route against an onerous position, §7.5.5).

### 7.5.8 Disclosure: contract balances, backlog and the link to billing data

**Definition and purpose.** KA 2.2.13 sets out IFRS 15's disclosure requirements in full — disaggregation of
revenue, the contract-balance reconciliation, the remaining-performance-obligation ("backlog") figure, and
the significant judgements made — and is not repeated here. This topic makes the commercial-cycle point KA
2.2.13 itself flags: these disclosure figures should tie **directly** to the AFP/billing schedule (KA 7.4,
KA 11.1) and the cost-to-cost schedule (§7.5.2) a project-controls function already maintains operationally,
not be reconstructed as a separate financial-reporting exercise.

**The underlying principle.** The "remaining performance obligation" disclosure is, in substance, the same
number a commercial or project-controls team already tracks internally as unrecognised contract value — but
it is a **different** number from "remaining value to be billed," and a professional preparing or reading
these disclosures must be able to distinguish the two.

**Formula.** `Remaining performance obligation (revenue-side) = Contract price − Cumulative revenue
recognised`; `Remaining value to be billed (billing-side) = Contract price − Cumulative amount billed`.

**Example 7.5.8 — Pipeline Segment 4B's two backlog figures at Month 3.**

*Required.* State both figures at the Month-3/Application-3 date, and explain why they differ.

*Working.*
`Remaining performance obligation = 750,000 − 350,000 = 400,000`
`Remaining value to be billed = 750,000 − 412,500 = 337,500`

*Answer.* **Remaining performance obligation (revenue-side): $400,000. Remaining value to be billed
(billing-side): $337,500** — a difference of $62,500, exactly Example 7.5.4's contract liability.

*Interpretation.* A commercial team reading only the billing-side figure would understate how much revenue-
recognition work remains by $62,500 relative to the statutory disclosure basis — precisely the confusion
this whole KA exists to prevent. A **contract-balance movement** disclosure for the same period would present
this as a roll-forward: opening contract liability $0 (inception) → closing contract liability $(62,500) at
Month 3, with the movement explained as "amounts billed of $412,500 during the period, against revenue
recognised of $350,000 (measurement-basis effect, §7.5.3)" — the same underlying arithmetic, presented as a
movement rather than a point-in-time balance, which is what IFRS 15's contract-balance reconciliation
disclosure specifically requires (KA 2.2.13).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Disclosing only the revenue-side backlog and omitting the distinct billing-side
remaining-to-invoice figure, leaving a reader unable to bridge the disclosure to a cash-flow forecast (KA
3.5). (2) Preparing the disclosure as a standalone financial-reporting exercise disconnected from the AFP and
cost-to-cost schedules already maintained operationally (KA 2.2.13's own pitfall, restated here). (3)
Disclosing the closing contract-balance figure without the movement explanation — a reader needs to know
*why* the position changed (a measurement-basis effect, an unbilled claim, §7.5.3–7.5.5), not merely what it
changed to.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI drafting assistants can produce a first-draft disclosure note — the
disaggregation table, the contract-balance movement, the remaining-performance-obligation figure — directly
from the AFP system and the cost-to-cost schedule, saving material drafting time in a routine reporting
cycle (cross-reference KA 2.2.13, KA 13.3, KA 13.5). Given the direct regulatory and audit significance of
disclosures, every AI-drafted note must be checked line-by-line against the underlying schedules by a
qualified accountant and formally signed off before issuance.

**Cross-references.** KA 2.2.13 (the full disclosure requirements, not repeated here); KA 2.5 (management vs
statutory reporting); KA 4.3 (management reporting cadence); KA 3.5 (cash flow forecasting, which uses the
billing-side backlog specifically).

### Key terms

- **Amounts billed (commercial-cycle sense)** — the cumulative gross certified value invoiced to the client
  to date, before retention deduction; the basis generated by the applications-for-payment process (KA 7.4,
  KA 11.1).
- **Revenue recognised** — the cumulative amount produced by the entity's selected IFRS 15 measurement
  method (input/cost-to-cost or output), applied to the transaction price (KA 2.2).
- **Cost-to-cost (input method) % complete** — `Costs incurred to date / Total estimated cost (as currently
  forecast)`; converges exactly with an entity's own EV percentage whenever the total-estimated-cost input is
  re-forecast on the same basis as the EAC (§7.5.2b).
- **Contract asset (under-billing)** — cumulative revenue recognised exceeds cumulative amounts billed;
  conditional on further performance.
- **Contract liability (over-billing)** — cumulative amounts billed exceed cumulative revenue recognised;
  deferred revenue, in effect.
- **Billed-vs-recognised gap drivers** — measurement-basis effect; timing lag; unbilled variable
  consideration; modification effect; advance/mobilisation billing (§7.5.3).
- **Sign convention** — `Contract asset/(liability), cumulative = Cumulative revenue recognised − Cumulative
  amount billed`; positive = asset, negative = liability.
- **Constrained variable consideration in the commercial cycle** — a variation, claim or incentive included
  in the transaction price once constrained (KA 2.2.8), even before it is certified or billed; recognised
  proportionally to progress, not in full immediately.
- **Significant financing component vs retention** — retention is normally performance-related security, not
  financing; an advance/mobilisation payment can be a genuine financing component if its recovery gap exceeds
  approximately 12 months (KA 2.2.9).
- **Onerous contract provision (commercial-cycle application)** — the unavoidable loss on remaining work
  only, unaffected by, and never changing, what can be certified and billed under the contract's own
  measurement rules (KA 1.4.5).
- **Remaining performance obligation vs remaining value to be billed** — two distinct backlog figures;
  their difference equals the current contract asset/(liability).

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 7.5.1 [topic 7.5.1, level: recall]**
An EPC contractor builds a bespoke asset that has no realistic alternative use to the contractor and is
entitled, under the contract, to certified payment for work completed to date even if the client terminates
for convenience. Under which criterion is this recognised over time?
A. The customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefit as the entity performs
B. The entity's performance creates or enhances an asset the customer controls as it is created
C. **No alternative use to the entity, combined with an enforceable right to payment for performance
   completed to date** ✔
D. The customer has accepted the asset and holds legal title
*Rationale:* This is criterion (c) (KA 2.2.6), the criterion this KA applies to Pipeline Segment 4B in
§7.5.1. A and B describe the other two over-time criteria, not met on these facts; D describes point-in-time
control-transfer indicators, which do not apply once an over-time criterion is met.

**MCQ 7.5.2 [topic 7.5.2, level: application — numerical]**
A contractor's total estimated cost is $2,000,000, the contract price is $2,800,000, and costs incurred to
date are $900,000. Using the cost-to-cost (input) method, what cumulative revenue should be recognised?
A. $900,000
B. **$1,260,000** ✔
C. $1,400,000
D. $2,000,000
*Rationale:* `% complete = 900,000/2,000,000 = 45.0%`; `Revenue = 45.0% × 2,800,000 = 1,260,000` (§7.5.2). A
mistakes cost incurred for revenue; C and D misapply the ratio.

**MCQ 7.5.3 [topic 7.5.4, level: application — numerical]**
Cumulative revenue recognised on a contract is $480,000 and cumulative amounts billed are $525,000. What does
this represent?
A. A contract asset of $45,000
B. **A contract liability of $45,000** ✔
C. A receivable of $45,000
D. An onerous contract provision of $45,000
*Rationale:* `Contract asset/(liability) = Revenue recognised − Billed = 480,000 − 525,000 = (45,000)`, a
negative result, which is a contract liability (§7.5.4) — billing has run ahead of revenue recognised. A
reverses the sign; C confuses this conditional balance with an unconditional receivable; D is an unrelated
concept (IAS 37, §7.5.7).

**MCQ 7.5.4 [topic 7.5.3, level: analysis]**
Which of the following would **not**, by itself, be a driver of divergence between amounts billed and
revenue recognised on an over-time construction contract?
A. The measurement basis used for billing differing from the measurement basis used for revenue recognition
B. A constrained but not-yet-certified claim already reflected in the transaction price
C. **The customer's choice of payment method (bank transfer versus cheque)** ✔
D. A mobilisation advance billed ahead of any performance
*Rationale:* Payment method affects how cash is transferred, not the certified/billed amount or the revenue
recognised — it is unrelated to any of the five drivers in §7.5.3. A, B and D are each genuine, named drivers
(measurement-basis effect, unbilled variable consideration, and advance billing respectively).

**MCQ 7.5.5 [topic 7.5.5, level: analysis — numerical]**
A contractor's unresolved claim has three possible outcomes: 50% probability of $300,000, 30% probability of
$100,000, and 20% probability of $0. Using the expected value method, what amount of variable consideration
(before applying the constraint) should be estimated?
A. $300,000
B. **$180,000** ✔
C. $150,000
D. $100,000
*Rationale:* `Expected value = (0.50 × 300,000) + (0.30 × 100,000) + (0.20 × 0) = 150,000 + 30,000 + 0 =
180,000` (§7.5.5). A takes the highest possible outcome; C and D each omit or misweight one outcome. The
constraint (KA 2.2.8) would then be applied separately and could further reduce this estimate.

**MCQ 7.5.6 [topic 7.5.7, level: analysis — numerical]**
A fixed-price contract has a contract price of $750,000. Total estimated cost is re-forecast to $900,000, and
costs incurred to date are $540,000. What onerous-contract provision, if any, should be recognised?
A. $150,000 (the full forecast loss)
B. $90,000 (the margin already recognised to date)
C. **$60,000** ✔
D. $0 — no provision is required
*Rationale:* `% complete = 540,000/900,000 = 60.0%`; `Revenue to date = 60.0% × 750,000 = 450,000`; `Margin
to date = 450,000 − 540,000 = (90,000)`; `Remaining revenue = 750,000 − 450,000 = 300,000`; `Remaining cost =
900,000 − 540,000 = 360,000`; `Remaining margin = 300,000 − 360,000 = (60,000)`. The provision is $60,000,
the loss on remaining work only (§7.5.7). A double-counts the loss already reflected through reduced revenue
recognition (the central pitfall, KA 1.4.5); B states the loss to date, not the remaining loss; D wrongly
concludes the contract is not onerous.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Suppose, at a hypothetical Month-4 data date (before the Month-5 re-forecast), Pipeline Segment 4B's
   cumulative costs incurred reach $390,000, with total estimated cost still unrevised at $600,000 (BAC).
   Compute cumulative % complete and cumulative revenue recognised.
2. KA 6.1 describes Pipeline Segment 4B at Month 3 as "ahead of schedule and under budget." Explain, in your
   own words, why this favourable EVM position coexists with a $62,500 contract **liability** (§7.5.4) at the
   same date, rather than a contract asset.
3. At Month 3, Meridian's books show a trade receivable of $101,250, a retention receivable of $41,250, and a
   contract liability of $62,500 — all for the same contract at the same date. Explain how all three can
   coexist without double-counting.
4. A fixed-price contract of $750,000 has its total estimated cost re-forecast to $800,000, with costs
   incurred to date of $680,000. Determine whether the contract is onerous and, if so, the provision
   required.

**Answers**

1. `% complete = 390,000/600,000 = 65.0%`; `Revenue recognised, cumulative = 65.0% × 750,000 = 487,500`
   (§7.5.2's method, applied to a new data point).
2. KA 6.1's "ahead of schedule and under budget" describes Pipeline Segment 4B's **internal EVM performance**
   (SPI and CPI both favourable against the *budget*) — it says nothing about the relationship between
   *billing* and *cost-to-cost revenue recognition*. At Month 3, the certified billing basis (55.0%) happened
   to run ahead of cost-to-cost revenue (46.7%) specifically because total estimated cost was still the
   unrevised original BAC (§7.5.2b) — a measurement-basis effect, not a sign of poor project performance. A
   contract liability simply means billing has outpaced *recognised revenue under this method*; it is fully
   consistent with, and not contradicted by, a favourable EVM position (§7.5.3, §7.5.4).
3. The three balances answer three different questions and are computed from three different comparisons: the
   trade receivable ($101,250) measures cash billed on the most recent application but not yet collected; the
   retention receivable ($41,250) measures value earned and billed in substance but not yet due for payment;
   the contract liability ($62,500) measures the excess of cumulative amounts billed over cumulative revenue
   recognised under cost-to-cost measurement. None of the three is derived from, or nets against, either of
   the others (§7.5.4b).
4. `Total forecast margin = 750,000 − 800,000 = (50,000)` — onerous. `% complete = 680,000/800,000 = 85.0%`;
   `Revenue to date = 85.0% × 750,000 = 637,500`; `Margin to date = 637,500 − 680,000 = (42,500)`; `Remaining
   revenue = 750,000 − 637,500 = 112,500`; `Remaining cost = 800,000 − 680,000 = 120,000`; `Remaining margin =
   112,500 − 120,000 = (7,500)`. *Check:* `(42,500) + (7,500) = (50,000) ✓`. **Provision required = $7,500**
   — the loss on remaining work only (§7.5.7).
