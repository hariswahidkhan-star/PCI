# Domain 7 — Contracts, Commercial Management, BoQ, Invoicing & Revenue

## KA 7.3 — Bills of Quantities (BoQ)

A Bill of Quantities (BoQ) is the pricing document that turns a designed scope of work into a structured
list of billable items — each with a description, a unit of measurement, a quantity and, once tendered, a
rate and an amount — so that tenderers can price competitively on a common, comparable basis and, once a
contract is awarded, so that payment can be calculated consistently as the work proceeds. KA 7.1 sets out
the family of contract types a project controls professional will encounter, including the remeasurement
(admeasurement) form this KA assumes throughout; KA 7.2 covers the wider contract-management lifecycle
within which a BoQ operates. This KA goes inside the BoQ itself: how it is structured, how standard methods
of measurement keep that structure consistent across the industry, how a single item is built and priced
from first principles, how quantities are taken off from design drawings, how the whole document is
assembled and priced for tender, how it is remeasured against as-built reality, and how — throughout
construction — it becomes the working tool for valuing progress, controlling cost and pricing variations.
The running illustration continues **Pipeline Segment 4B**, the buried pipeline within a wider rail-corridor
programme delivered by **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC** (first introduced in KA 1.1, estimated in depth in
KA 3.2, and measured for earned value in KA 6.1): here, for the first time, the same scope is seen through
its **commercial** lens — as a priced Bill of Quantities, agreed with the employer under a remeasurement
contract, with rates fixed at tender and quantities settled only once the work is actually built and
measured. Everything in this KA feeds forward directly into KA 7.4 (interim valuations and applications for
payment, which apply these same BoQ rates to work-in-progress) and KA 7.5 (revenue recognition, which
converts certified valuations into IFRS 15 terms).

### 7.3.1 The Bill of Quantities: purpose and its role in an admeasurement (remeasurement) contract

**Definition & purpose.** A Bill of Quantities (BoQ) is a schedule of the items of work comprised in a
project, each described, quantified in a stated unit of measurement, and priced by the tendering contractor
at a rate and an extended amount, compiled from the design and issued to tenderers as part of the tender
documents. Once a contract is awarded, the priced BoQ becomes a contract document: its item descriptions
define the scope each rate covers, and its rates — not its quantities — are what the contractor is
contractually bound to for the duration of the works (subject to the mechanisms in 7.3.9).

**The underlying principle.** In an **admeasurement** (also called **remeasurement** or **unit-price**)
contract, the employer cannot define final quantities precisely at tender stage — ground conditions,
detailed design development and genuine variations mean the work actually required is rarely identical to
what was estimated when the BoQ was issued. Rather than force a single fixed lump sum onto a scope that is
not yet fully known (the lump-sum approach, treated fully in KA 7.1), an admeasurement contract fixes what
*can* reliably be fixed at tender — the unit rates — and leaves what *cannot* be reliably fixed — the
quantities — open to be settled once the work is actually measured. The BoQ is the instrument that makes
this possible: it gives tenderers a common, itemised scope to price (so tenders are comparable on a
like-for-like basis) while explicitly badging its quantities as **estimated, not guaranteed**. This is the
single most important distinction to carry through the rest of this KA: **a BoQ rate is a firm contractual
commitment; a BoQ quantity is a best estimate, subject to remeasurement (7.3.8).**

**Formula(e).** No single arithmetic formula describes "purpose"; the governing relationship that recurs
through this KA, and that the remainder of it builds toward, is:
`Amount due for an item = Rate (fixed at tender) × Quantity actually measured (settled at execution, 7.3.6/
7.3.8)` — as distinct from a lump-sum contract, where `Amount due = the agreed lump sum, adjusted only by
certified variations (KA 7.1, KA 5.4)`.

**Illustrative mini-case — why Meridian and the employer chose an admeasurement contract for Pipeline
Segment 4B.** At tender stage, Pipeline Segment 4B's route was fixed by a preliminary design, but ground
investigation along the 2,000 m alignment was incomplete in several reaches, and the exact number and size
of valve chambers depended on a valve-selection study still under way. Fixing a lump sum against that
residual uncertainty would have forced tenderers either to price a large, undisclosed risk allowance into
their bids (inflating the tender price for a risk that might not occur) or to bid optimistically and seek
large variations later — a common source of disputes. An admeasurement contract let Meridian and the other
tenderers price the *rates* — which they could estimate with reasonable confidence from known productivity
and market prices (7.3.5) — while leaving the *quantities* to be confirmed once ground conditions and the
valve design were finalised (7.3.6, 7.3.8). This is the same judgement KA 3.2.1's AACE estimate-class
framework describes at the whole-estimate level — match the certainty of the commercial instrument to the
maturity of the scope — applied here to the choice of contract and BoQ mechanism.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating BoQ quantities as a promise of the final quantity, or final payment, rather than as a tendering
  estimate subject to remeasurement — a misunderstanding that surfaces, often contentiously, at the first
  interim valuation where measured quantity diverges materially from billed quantity.
- Confusing an admeasurement contract's BoQ with a lump-sum contract's priced schedule of items —
  superficially similar documents that operate under materially different payment mechanics (KA 7.1
  develops this distinction in full).
- Assuming the choice between admeasurement and lump sum is purely a matter of employer preference rather
  than a risk-allocation decision driven by how mature and certain the scope genuinely is at tender.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-analytics tools can scan a BoQ and its preamble to flag whether it is drafted
on a remeasurement or lump-sum basis, and can cross-check that the stated basis is used consistently
throughout the document (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — a genuinely useful document-consistency check
on a large tender package. Such a tool cannot judge whether an admeasurement basis is the *right* commercial
choice for a given project's actual scope maturity and risk profile — that remains a procurement and
commercial-strategy decision for the professional, informed by, but not delegated to, an AI-generated
document review.

### 7.3.2 Standard methods of measurement (awareness): RICS NRM, CESMM, POMI and shared principles

**Definition & purpose.** A **standard method of measurement (SMM)** is a published rulebook that defines,
for a given sector, how quantities of work are to be measured and described so that a BoQ compiled under it
is consistent — in item structure, units and coverage — across different consultants, contractors and
projects. Without a shared SMM, every BoQ author would invent their own item descriptions and coverage
assumptions, making tenders impossible to compare fairly and rates impossible to reuse defensibly from one
project to the next.

**The underlying principle.** A rate is only meaningful if everyone pricing it, and everyone later paying
against it, agrees on exactly what it is deemed to cover. An SMM achieves this by fixing, in advance and in
general terms, how each type of work is to be measured (net or gross, in what unit, over what range bands)
and what a rate for it is "deemed to include" without those things being separately billed. At **awareness
level**, a project controls professional should recognise the major named families, in their own words:

- **RICS New Rules of Measurement (NRM)**, particularly **NRM2**, developed for building works and widely
  used internationally; it sets out default work sections and measurement rules for building-type
  construction, and largely superseded the older UK Standard Method of Measurement (SMM7) for that purpose.
- **CESMM (Civil Engineering Standard Method of Measurement)**, now in its fourth edition (CESMM4),
  developed specifically for civil engineering and infrastructure works — earthworks, concrete, pipework and
  the like — historically originating in the UK civil engineering sector and used internationally, including
  on many Gulf-region infrastructure contracts; it organises measured work into standard classes, each with
  its own rules for how that class of work is to be quantified and described.
- **POMI (RICS Principles of Measurement (International))**, a shorter, principles-based, sector- and
  jurisdiction-neutral framework RICS publishes for international use, particularly where no detailed local
  SMM exists, or as an overarching set of principles beneath a more detailed local method.

Whichever family is adopted, several principles recur across essentially all of them: work is generally
measured **net**, as fixed in its final position, with no allowance for the contractor's own wastage or
working space folded into the *billed quantity* itself (that allowance belongs in the *rate*, 7.3.5); items
are described and quantified in standard units (m, m², m³, kg, tonne, No., item/sum); items are grouped and
coded by defined work sections for consistency; and each rate carries implicit **coverage rules** — what it
is deemed to include, so that (for example) ordinary compaction is not separately billed if it is deemed
included within an excavation or backfill item, unless the specification calls for something beyond the
ordinary and the SMM requires it to be billed separately.

**Formula(e).** No arithmetic formula defines "which SMM applies" — that is a contractual choice, stated in
the BoQ preamble. What *is* directly calculable is the practical consequence of the net-measurement
principle for a contractor's own procurement, illustrated below.

**Example 7.3.2 — net billed quantity vs gross procurement quantity for the pipeline.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's BoQ item 2.02 bills 2,000 m of pipe **net** — the length of pipe actually
installed along the alignment, per the net-measurement principle above. HDPE pipe is supplied to Meridian in
standard 6 m lengths, and cutting losses at joints and valve-chamber connections require an allowance of 2%
more pipe stock than the net installed length.

*Required.* The quantity of pipe stock Meridian must actually order, and the number of standard 6 m lengths
this represents.

*Working.*
`Order length = Net billed length × (1 + wastage allowance) = 2,000 × 1.02 = 2,040 m`
`Number of 6 m lengths = 2,040 / 6 = 340 lengths`

*Answer.* **Meridian must order 2,040 m of pipe stock (340 standard 6 m lengths)**, even though the BoQ
bills, and item 2.02 is priced and paid against, a **net** quantity of 2,000 m.

*Interpretation.* The 40 m (2%) difference between what is bought and what is billed is never itself a
billed quantity — it is a cost the contractor's own rate must absorb, which is exactly why item 2.02's rate
build-up (Example 7.3.5) includes a wastage allowance inside its material cost component rather than
appearing as extra billed metres here. Confusing net billed quantity with gross procurement quantity is a
common source of confusion for less experienced estimators reading a BoQ for the first time.

**Mini-case — a coverage rule in practice.** Item 2.03 in Pipeline Segment 4B's BoQ (Fig 7.3.7) is described
as "Backfill **and compact** trench with selected excavated material," priced and billed as a single item,
rather than as two separate items for backfilling and for compaction. This reflects a normal SMM coverage
rule: ordinary compaction of backfill to a standard specification is deemed included within the backfilling
rate, and is not separately measured or billed, unless the specification calls for a special compaction
standard (for example, mechanical proof-rolling to a stated density) that the SMM requires to be
individually described and billed. A BoQ that instead billed "compaction" as its own, separate item without
such a special requirement would be inconsistent with normal coverage-rule practice and could confuse
tenderers about whether ordinary compaction is being priced once, or twice.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assuming units and coverage are self-evident and skipping the BoQ preamble/coverage rules altogether,
  leading to items priced with unintended gaps or unintended double-counted coverage.
- Mixing SMM conventions within one BoQ — for example, applying a building-oriented work-section structure
  to what is fundamentally civil engineering scope — without adapting definitions consistently.
- Treating "net measurement" as meaning "however the contractor chooses to interpret it," rather than the
  specific, stated rule of the adopted SMM.

**AI in this topic.** Document-comparison and retrieval-augmented (RAG) tools can check a draft BoQ against
the structure and typical coverage rules of a chosen SMM and flag items that appear misclassified or
potentially missing expected coverage (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — a useful consistency check on a
large tender document. Such a tool cannot determine which SMM a given project *should* adopt, nor resolve a
genuine judgement call at the boundary of two coverage rules — those remain quantity-surveying decisions for
the professional.

### 7.3.3 Anatomy of a BoQ item: description, unit, quantity, rate and amount

**Definition & purpose.** A BoQ item is the atomic line of a Bill of Quantities: a description, a unit of
measurement and a quantity (all fixed at tender issue), together with, once priced, a rate and an amount —
the same basic six-field structure repeated, item after item, throughout the document.

**The underlying principle.** The **description** defines the exact scope the rate must cover, consistent
with whichever SMM's coverage rules apply (7.3.2); the **unit** and **quantity**, together, tell a tenderer
how much of that described scope to price; the **rate** is the tenderer's own priced judgement of cost per
unit (built up as in 7.3.5); and the **amount** is a simple extension, `Rate × Quantity`. The whole document's
commercial integrity rests on every one of these fields being internally consistent — a description that
matches what was actually taken off (7.3.6), a unit that matches how the rate was built up, and an amount
that is arithmetically correct.

**Formula.** `Amount (item) = Rate × Quantity`; `Bill/Section total = Σ Amount, across the items within it`
(7.3.7 develops the full document roll-up).

**Example 7.3.3 — reading and verifying a single BoQ line.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's BoQ, Bill 2 (Measured Work), item 2.02, reads: Item ref **2.02**;
Description **"Supply, lay and joint 300 mm dia. HDPE pipe, including granular bedding and surround, in
trench excavated and backfilled elsewhere in this Bill"**; Unit **m**; Quantity **2,000**; Rate **$121.00**;
Amount **$242,000**.

*Required.* Verify the item's arithmetic, and identify what each field is doing.

*Working.* `Amount = Rate × Quantity = 121.00 × 2,000 = 242,000` ✓, matching the stated amount exactly. The
description defines the scope (pipe supply, laying, jointing, bedding and surround — but *not* excavation or
backfill, which the description explicitly signposts as billed under separate items) and, together with
7.3.2's coverage-rule discipline, tells a tenderer precisely what the $121.00/m rate must recover; the unit
(m) matches how the rate was itself built up (7.3.5); the quantity (2,000) is the net billed length from
7.3.2's take-off; the amount is the item's contribution to the Bill 2 total (7.3.7).

*Answer.* **Amount = $242,000**, correctly extended and internally consistent across all six fields.

*Interpretation.* A single verified line looks trivial in isolation, but a priced BoQ with hundreds of items
depends on every line passing exactly this same check — description, unit, quantity, rate and amount all
mutually consistent — before the whole document can be relied upon (7.3.7's roll-up assumes this has already
been done, item by item).

**Mini-case — why a vague description creates risk.** Compare item 2.02's actual description above with a
poorly written alternative: "Pipework — supply and install." The vague version states no pipe diameter, no
material, and no explicit statement of what is or is not included (bedding? jointing? excavation?) — leaving
the rate's true scope genuinely ambiguous. If a dispute later arises over whether item 2.02's $121.00/m rate
was meant to include, say, jointing consumables, a well-drafted description (as Pipeline Segment 4B's
actually is) settles the question by simply being read; a vague one invites a costly argument. This is
precisely the discipline an SMM's default work-section wording and coverage rules (7.3.2) exist to enforce
across an entire document, not just item by item at the drafter's discretion.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Ambiguous or incomplete item descriptions that leave genuine doubt about what a rate is meant to cover —
  the single most common source of BoQ-related commercial disputes.
- Unit mismatches between how an item is billed and how its rate was actually built up (e.g., pricing per
  m³ against an item billed per tonne).
- Silent extension errors (`Rate × Quantity` miscalculated) that a full document audit (7.3.7) must catch
  before a BoQ is issued or relied upon.

**AI in this topic.** Document-extraction and spreadsheet-AI tools can lift BoQ line items from a scanned or
PDF tender document into a structured spreadsheet and automatically re-check that every item's Amount equals
Rate × Quantity, flagging extension errors across thousands of lines far faster than a manual audit
(cross-reference KA 13.4). Such tools cannot verify that a description is genuinely unambiguous, or that a
quantity was correctly taken off from the drawings in the first place — those remain measurement and
quantity-surveying judgements for the professional to confirm.

### 7.3.4 Preliminaries, measured work, provisional sums, PC sums, dayworks and contingencies

**Definition & purpose.** A BoQ is not one undifferentiated list of items; it is organised into distinct
categories of cost, each governed by a different payment logic. A project controls professional must be able
to tell these categories apart at a glance, because each behaves differently under remeasurement, variation
and final-account rules — treating them as interchangeable is a routine and consequential error.

**The underlying principle.** Every category answers a different question about *when and how much* is
actually paid:

- **Preliminaries** — the cost of running the site and the project itself, rather than building any specific
  item of permanent work: site establishment, management staff, temporary facilities, insurances, security
  and the like. Usually priced as a lump sum, or split between fixed (mobilisation/demobilisation) and
  time-related components, and not normally remeasured against physical quantity — though a time-related
  preliminary may be adjusted if the contract period itself changes (an extension of time, KA 7.2).
- **Measured work** — the substantive, physically quantifiable permanent-work items (excavation, pipe,
  concrete, and so on): each billed at a rate against an estimated quantity, and paid on the quantity
  actually measured (7.3.8) — normally the bulk of a BoQ's value.
- **Provisional sums** — sums included for work or cost that is not sufficiently defined at tender to be
  measured and described as a normal item. A provisional sum is included in the tender total for competition
  and comparability, but only paid to the extent it is actually instructed and valued — it is not a
  guaranteed entitlement.
- **Prime Cost (PC) sums** — a known, specific sum (typically a nominated supplier's or subcontractor's
  quoted price) included in the BoQ, to which the main contractor adds a stated percentage for **attendance**
  (facilities, coordination, unloading and protection of the nominated goods or works) and **profit**, since
  the PC sum itself is a pass-through cost the main contractor does not itself price.
- **Dayworks** — a mechanism, and an accompanying schedule of rates, for valuing additional or varied work
  that genuinely cannot be fairly measured and valued under normal BoQ rates (typically minor, disrupted or
  emergent work), priced on a cost-plus-percentage basis for actual labour, plant and material used, verified
  by daywork sheets or vouchers. It is a valuation method of last resort, not a default (7.3.9).
- **Contingencies (BoQ-level)** — a general allowance in the BoQ (or in the employer's parallel budget) for
  items not identifiable at all at tender, governed like a provisional sum (drawn down only against
  instructed, valued work). This is distinct from the *contractor's own* pricing-risk allowance folded
  invisibly into rates (7.3.7), and distinct again from the *project-level*, risk-register-derived
  contingency of KA 3.1.6 and KA 12.3, which typically sits in the employer's budget rather than in the
  contractor's priced BoQ at all.

**Formula(e).** No single formula distinguishes the categories; the PC sum's own arithmetic is:
`PC sum total = PC sum (base) + (PC sum × Attendance %) + (PC sum × Profit %)`.

*Quick reference — how each category behaves:*

| Category | Payment trigger | Remeasured against actual quantity? | Pipeline Segment 4B example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preliminaries | Time/lump sum, largely independent of measured quantity | No (adjusted only for an extension of time, KA 7.2) | $45,000 site establishment & management |
| Measured work | Rate × quantity actually measured | Yes (7.3.8) | Excavation, pipe, concrete, testing, reinstatement |
| Provisional sum | Only to the extent instructed and valued | Not applicable — not a rate × quantity item | $40,000 allowance, utility diversions |
| PC sum | The stated sum, plus attendance % and profit % | No — a fixed pass-through sum unless the underlying cost itself changes | $25,000 valve supply + $1,250 attendance + $2,500 profit |
| Dayworks | Cost-plus-% for verified actual resources used | Not applicable — priced on vouchers, not rate × quantity | $15,000 provisional allowance |
| Contingency (BoQ-level) | Drawn down only against instructed, valued items | Not applicable | $24,618 (5% of measured-work value) |

**Example 7.3.4 — pricing the PC sum for the isolation valves.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's BoQ includes a Prime Cost sum for specialist isolation valves to be
supplied by a nominated supplier, quoted at **$25,000**. Meridian's standard tender terms add 5% attendance
and 10% profit on PC sums.

*Required.* The total amount for this PC sum item as it appears in the priced BoQ.

*Working.*
`Attendance = 25,000 × 5% = 1,250`
`Profit = 25,000 × 10% = 2,500`
`PC sum total = 25,000 + 1,250 + 2,500 = 28,750`

*Answer.* **PC sum total = $28,750.**

*Interpretation.* Only $1,250 + $2,500 = $3,750 of this figure is genuinely Meridian's own priced work
(coordinating and incorporating the nominated supplier's valves); the remaining $25,000 is simply the
nominated supplier's own price, passed through the main contract without mark-up beyond the stated
attendance and profit additions. This is why PC sums are billed and reported separately from measured work —
conflating the two would misstate how much of the contract sum reflects Meridian's own priced scope.

**Mini-case — how each category actually settles at final account.** Anticipating 7.3.8's remeasurement and
7.3.9's closing exhibit, it is useful to preview how differently these categories behave once the project is
complete: Preliminaries is paid in full at its tendered $45,000 (the programme runs to its planned duration);
the utility-diversion provisional sum is drawn down for only $38,500 of instructed work (a smaller diversion
than the $40,000 allowed for), so only $38,500 is paid and the balance simply lapses; the PC sum is paid
unchanged at $28,750, the valves having been procured at exactly the quoted sum; dayworks sees only $9,200 of
verified vouchers certified against the $15,000 allowance; and the $24,618 contingency is not itself paid as
a line at all — its role, as 7.3.9 shows in full, is to have provided headroom against exactly this kind of
net movement across the other categories, not to be drawn down as a category in its own right.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a provisional sum or BoQ-level contingency as money the contractor is automatically entitled to,
  regardless of whether it is ever instructed — a common and consequential misunderstanding.
- Omitting the contractor's attendance/profit addition on a PC sum, understating what the main contractor is
  properly entitled to for coordinating nominated work.
- Defaulting to dayworks for straightforward additional quantities that could, and should, be measured and
  valued using existing BoQ rates or a fair pro-rata/star rate instead (7.3.9) — dayworks should be the
  valuation method of last resort, because it removes the competitive-pricing discipline the rest of the BoQ
  provides.

**AI in this topic.** An AI-assisted cost-control tool can track drawdown of each provisional sum, PC sum,
dayworks allowance and contingency in real time against instructed variations, flagging when an allowance is
approaching exhaustion (cross-reference KA 5.4, KA 13.5) — a genuinely useful early-warning capability. Such
a tool cannot determine whether a given instruction genuinely falls within a provisional sum's defined scope,
or whether dayworks is properly the appropriate valuation method for a piece of additional work rather than a
measured or star rate — those remain contract-administration judgements for the professional.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1.6, KA 12.3 (project-level, risk-register contingency — distinct from this
topic's BoQ-level contingency); KA 5.4 (change control, instructing expenditure against these allowances);
KA 7.2 (contract administration and who has authority to instruct); 7.3.9 (the full final-account roll-up
this topic's category behaviour feeds into); KA 7.4 (interim valuation of these same categories).

### 7.3.5 Rate build-up: from labour, plant and material to a priced unit rate

**Definition & purpose.** Rate build-up (or "rate analysis") is the process by which an estimator constructs
a defensible unit rate for a BoQ item from its constituent resource costs, rather than guessing at a figure
or copying a rate from an unrelated source. It is the pricing-side counterpart to taking off (7.3.6), which
establishes the quantity a rate will be multiplied by.

**The underlying principle.** A unit rate must recover every genuine cost of producing one unit of the
described work — labour, plant and material — plus a fair share of the business's indirect overhead costs,
plus an intended margin (profit), built up in a defined and traceable sequence so that the rate can be
explained, defended and, if needed, adjusted later (7.3.9).

**Formula(e).**
`Labour rate (per unit) = Crew cost per period / Output per period`
`Plant rate (per unit) = Plant cost per period / Output per period`
`Material rate (per unit) = Σ material components, including any wastage allowance (7.3.2)`
`Direct cost (per unit) = Labour rate + Plant rate + Material rate`
`Rate after overhead = Direct cost × (1 + Overhead %)`
`Unit rate = Rate after overhead × (1 + Profit %)`

**Example 7.3.5 — building the unit rate for BoQ item 2.02 (supply, lay and joint 300 mm HDPE pipe).**

*Scenario.* Meridian's estimator builds the rate for item 2.02 from first principles, before the tender BoQ
(Fig 7.3.7) is finalised:

| Resource | Basis | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Labour (pipe-laying gang: 1 ganger + 4 pipelayers) | $1,000/day; output 50 m/day | — |
| Plant (mobile crane, compressor, electrofusion welding set) | $500/day; output 50 m/day | — |
| Material — pipe supplied to site | — | $60.00/m |
| Material — bedding & surround (incl. wastage) | — | $8.00/m |
| Material — jointing consumables | — | $2.00/m |
| Overhead | 10% of direct cost | — |
| Profit | 10% of cost-plus-overhead subtotal | — |

*Required.* The unit rate per metre for item 2.02.

*Working.*
`Labour rate = 1,000 / 50 = $20.00/m`
`Plant rate = 500 / 50 = $10.00/m`
`Material rate = 60.00 + 8.00 + 2.00 = $70.00/m`
`Direct cost = 20.00 + 10.00 + 70.00 = $100.00/m`
`Overhead (10%) = 100.00 × 0.10 = $10.00/m; Rate after overhead = 100.00 + 10.00 = $110.00/m`
`Profit (10%) = 110.00 × 0.10 = $11.00/m; Unit rate = 110.00 + 11.00 = $121.00/m`

*Answer.* **Unit rate for item 2.02 = $121.00/m** (SAR 453.75/m at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1). At the
billed quantity of 2,000 m (7.3.2), this gives a BoQ amount of `121.00 × 2,000 = $242,000` for item 2.02.

*Interpretation.* Material dominates the direct cost (70% of the $100.00/m direct cost, i.e. $70 of $100),
which is typical for a supply-and-lay pipe item, in contrast to a labour-dominated item such as trenching
excavation (cross-reference KA 3.2.4's separate build-up for that scope). Overhead and profit together add
21% on top of direct cost ($21.00 of $121.00) — a transparent, two-step loading that a reviewer, or a later
star-rate build-up (7.3.9), can trace and challenge stage by stage, rather than accepting a single
unexplained mark-up.

**Mini-case — applying the same method to a very different item: the concrete valve chamber (item 2.04).**
The identical five-stage method applies equally to item 2.04 ("Reinforced concrete valve chamber, Grade C30,
cast in situ, complete with cover slab," billed per m³):

| Component | Basis | Rate ($/m³) |
|---|---|---|
| Labour (chamber gang) | $600/day ÷ 6 m³/day output | 100.00 |
| Plant (pump, vibrators, power) | $150/day ÷ 6 m³/day output | 25.00 |
| Material — ready-mix C30 concrete | — | 150.00 |
| Material — reinforcement, 50 kg/m³ @ $1.50/kg | — | 75.00 |
| Material — formwork (amortised) | — | 50.00 |
| **Direct cost** | | **400.00** |
| Overhead (10%) | | 40.00 |
| **Subtotal** | | **440.00** |
| Profit (10%) | | 44.00 |
| **Unit rate** | | **484.00** |

*Interpretation.* Despite an entirely different unit (m³ vs m), scope (structural concrete vs pipe) and cost
composition (labour and material more evenly balanced here than in item 2.02), the same five-stage
method — labour, plant, material, overhead, profit — produces a rate of **$484.00/m³**, used unchanged in the
priced BoQ (Fig 7.3.7) and, at a billed quantity of 40 m³ (Example 7.3.6), an amount of $19,360.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Applying overhead and profit as a single, blended mark-up percentage without transparency, making the rate

- Omitting a genuine cost element — most often wastage, or a shared plant cost not obviously "direct" to a

- Relying on stale or unverified productivity and material-price assumptions; the whole-project sensitivity
  KA 3.2.4 demonstrated for the trenching rate applies equally to any single BoQ item's build-up.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted estimating and spreadsheet tools can auto-populate a rate build-up template

(cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — genuinely useful for speed and as a first-pass sanity check across a BoQ
with hundreds of rates to build. Such tools cannot confirm that an assumed productivity is achievable on this
specific site with this specific crew and ground conditions, nor that a quoted material price is genuinely
current — that verification remains the estimator's responsibility before a rate is submitted.

### 7.3.6 Taking off quantities: from drawings to the dimension sheet to the bill

**Definition & purpose.** **Taking off** is the disciplined process of measuring quantities of work directly
from design drawings and specifications, recording each measured dimension on a dimension sheet (or its
digital/BIM equivalent), and working those dimensions up — through an abstract, or a direct digital
roll-up — into the net billed quantities that appear in the BoQ.

**The underlying principle.** A billed quantity is only as reliable as the take-off behind it, and — because
it will, in an admeasurement contract, ultimately be checked against what is actually built (7.3.8) — a
take-off that follows the adopted SMM's measurement rules consistently (7.3.2) both produces a fair tender
document and creates a clear, auditable basis for explaining any later remeasurement variance, rather than
leaving "why did the quantity change" as an unanswerable question.

**Formula/method.** No single formula; the general working method is: a **dimension** is read from the
drawing, **timesed** (multiplied) by the number of similar occurrences, **squared** (its component
dimensions multiplied together, e.g. length × width × height for a volume), **totalled** in an abstract by
BoQ item, and **transferred** as the item's billed quantity. The recurring relationships are:
`Length (m) = Σ measured lengths`; `Area (m²) = Length × Width`; `Volume (m³) = Length × Width × Height ×
Number of occurrences`.

**Example 7.3.6 — taking off the concrete valve chamber quantity (item 2.04).**

*Scenario.* The tender drawings show five identical in-situ reinforced-concrete isolation-valve chambers
along Pipeline Segment 4B's alignment, each measuring 2.0 m long × 2.0 m wide × 2.0 m high as a single
measured volume for billing purposes.

*Required.* The billed quantity for BoQ item 2.04.

*Working.*
`Volume per chamber = 2.0 × 2.0 × 2.0 = 8.0 m³`
`Timesed by 5 identical chambers: 8.0 × 5 = 40.0 m³`

*Answer.* **Billed quantity, item 2.04 = 40 m³** — the same figure used, apparently as a given, in Examples
7.3.3–7.3.5 and the priced BoQ (Fig 7.3.7).

*Interpretation.* Take-off is where that "40" actually comes from, and it is fully reconstructable from the
drawing: five chambers of 8 m³ each. A quantity surveyor auditing the BoQ, or investigating a later
remeasurement variance (7.3.8), starts here — at the dimensioned drawing and the take-off logic behind the
billed figure — not at the total alone.

**Mini-case — taking off an area, not a volume: the reinstatement item (2.06).** The specification defines a
3.0 m wide working corridor of disturbed surface along the full 2,000 m alignment requiring reinstatement
(topsoil and seeding) once construction is complete. `Area = Width × Length = 3.0 × 2,000 = 6,000 m²` — the
same 6,000 m² billed in item 2.06 (Fig 7.3.7), taken off using the same underlying method as the chamber
volume above, applied to an area rather than a volume.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Taking off from a drawing that is not the latest issued/approved revision, so the billed quantity does not
  reflect the actual current design (cross-reference KA 5.4 on document control).
- Failing to follow the adopted SMM's specific measurement rule for a given work type (for example, billing
  gross excavated volume including working space where the rule requires net, or vice versa), producing a
  quantity systematically biased against one party.
- Transcription errors moving a dimension from drawing to dimension sheet, or from abstract to bill — a
  manual step historically vulnerable to simple slips, increasingly mitigated by digital take-off tools.

**AI in this topic.** BIM-integrated and AI-assisted take-off software can measure quantities directly from a
3D design model with fewer transcription errors than a fully manual dimension-sheet process, and can
automatically re-run the take-off whenever the design model is revised, flagging exactly which billed
quantities have changed and why (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — a genuine, high-value application of
automation in this specific task. It cannot judge whether the model itself is complete, correctly modelled,
or represents the current approved design revision, nor resolve a genuine SMM measurement-rule judgement
call at an irregular boundary condition — the take-off professional remains responsible for reviewing and
approving the tool's output before it is billed.

**Cross-references.** 7.3.2 (the SMM rules governing how take-off is done); 7.3.3 (the quantity field this
process populates); 7.3.8 (remeasurement — the same take-off discipline applied to as-built condition); KA
5.4 (design and document change control).

### 7.3.7 Pricing the BoQ: the priced bill, pricing strategy and rate loading

**Definition & purpose.** Pricing the BoQ is the tenderer's act of inserting a rate against every measured
item (built up per 7.3.5), extending each to an amount, and totalling the whole document — with its
preliminaries, provisional sums, PC sums, dayworks and contingency additions (7.3.4) — into a single tender
sum. The resulting **priced BoQ** becomes, once the contract is signed, the primary reference for valuing
work, pricing variations and administering the remeasurement contract for the rest of the project.

**The underlying principle.** Every rate a tenderer inserts must simultaneously (a) genuinely recover that
item's real cost plus a fair margin (7.3.5), and (b) sum, across the whole document, to a competitive total
tender price — two objectives that, taken together, create room for a tenderer to distribute its intended
overall margin *unevenly* across items rather than uniformly, without changing the total tender sum at all.
This is legitimate commercial pricing strategy within limits, but it is also the mechanism behind **rate
loading**, discussed below as a pitfall specific to this topic.

**Formula(e).** `Bill/Section total = Σ Amount, across items within it`; `Tender sum = Σ (Bill/Section
totals) + Provisional sums + PC sums (incl. attendance & profit) + Dayworks allowance + Contingency`.

**Example 7.3.7 — assembling and totalling Pipeline Segment 4B's priced BoQ.**

*Scenario.* Bringing together every item and category priced in 7.3.4–7.3.6:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| 2 | 2.01 | Excavate trench, average depth ≤1.5 m, incl. disposal | m | 2,000 | 65.00 | 130,000 |
| 2 | 2.02 | Supply, lay & joint 300 mm dia. HDPE pipe, incl. bedding & surround | m | 2,000 | 121.00 | 242,000 |
| 2 | 2.03 | Backfill and compact trench with selected excavated material | m | 2,000 | 28.00 | 56,000 |

| 2 | 2.05 | Hydrostatic testing & commissioning of completed pipeline | item | 1 | 18,000.00 | 18,000 |

`Contingency = 492,360 × 5% = 24,618`

*Interpretation.* This figure sits only 0.27% above KA 3.2.2's Class 5 analogous estimate for the same scope
($644,000) — an expected, reassuring degree of convergence between an early analogous estimate and this
fully itemised, definitive-stage priced BoQ, though the two are not required to match exactly, since they are
built by entirely different methods at different stages of design maturity (cross-reference KA 3.2.1's
accuracy-funnel logic). A materially larger gap would warrant investigating whether the priced BoQ, the
earlier estimate, or both, contain an error.

**Mini-case — rate loading (front-loading), illustrated numerically.** A rival tenderer submits the *same*
overall tender sum ($645,728) as Meridian, but prices item 2.01 (excavation — executed early in the
programme) at $72.50/m instead of $65.00/m (+$7.50/m), and correspondingly prices item 2.06 (reinstatement —
executed at the very end) at $2.00/m² instead of $4.50/m² (−$2.50/m²):

`Item 2.01 (rival): 72.50 × 2,000 = 145,000 (+15,000 vs Meridian's 130,000)`
`Item 2.06 (rival): 2.00 × 6,000 = 12,000 (−15,000 vs Meridian's 27,000)`
`Net effect on the tender sum: +15,000 − 15,000 = 0`

Both tenders therefore total exactly $645,728, and a purely total-price comparison shows them as commercially
identical — yet their cash-flow and remeasurement-risk profiles differ materially. If the project proceeds
broadly as billed, the rival's loaded pricing extracts more cash earlier (benefiting the contractor's cash
flow), without affecting the tender's apparent competitiveness. If quantities are then remeasured *upward* on
the now-overpriced early item, or *downward* on the underpriced late item — exactly the kind of change an
admeasurement contract exists to accommodate (7.3.8) — the rival's final account diverges from what appeared,
at tender, to be an equivalent price, in the rival's favour. This is precisely why employers and their
quantity surveyors routinely check individual rates for reasonableness against expected rate build-ups
(7.3.5) during tender evaluation, not just the tender total.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Rate loading/front-loading items expected to be executed early, or where quantities are likely to increase,
  at the expense of items expected late or likely to decrease — commercially legitimate in form, but a real
  source of employer risk under an admeasurement contract.
- Pricing every item at an identical, blanket mark-up without recognising that overhead recovery and genuine
  risk differ item by item.
- Failing to reconcile the priced BoQ total against an independent estimate before submission, missing a
  material pricing or extension error.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted tender-evaluation tools can scan a priced BoQ across hundreds of items,
benchmark each rate against a market-rate database or the employer's own cost model, and flag statistical
outliers consistent with rate loading — unusually high rates on early or likely-to-increase items, unusually
low rates on late or likely-to-decrease items — far faster than a manual, line-by-line review (cross-
reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). Such a tool cannot determine with certainty that a flagged outlier is deliberate
loading rather than a genuine, defensible pricing decision (for example, a rate that is high because of a
real, disclosed site constraint) — a flagged rate requires the quantity surveyor's own judgement, typically
including a query back to the tenderer, before any conclusion is drawn.

**Cross-references.** 7.3.5 (the rate build-up a reasonableness check compares a submitted rate against);
7.3.4 (the categories rolled into the tender sum here); KA 3.2.1 (the Class 5 estimate this total is
cross-checked against); 7.3.8, 7.3.9 (where a loaded rate's commercial consequences are actually realised).

### 7.3.8 Remeasurement: comparing billed and measured quantities

**Definition & purpose.** Remeasurement is the process — inherent to an admeasurement contract (7.3.1) — of
establishing the actual quantity of each measured-work item as executed, by measurement on site or from
as-built records, and applying the contract's already-fixed rates to that actual quantity to determine the
amount properly due, in place of the BoQ's original estimated quantity.

**The underlying principle.** Because the rate was fixed at tender (7.3.1, 7.3.7) and only the quantity is
being reconsidered, remeasurement is, mechanically, a simple substitution — but its administration depends
on both parties measuring the same as-built scope on the same basis (the same SMM rules used to compile the
original BoQ, 7.3.2, 7.3.6), so that a remeasured quantity is a genuine like-for-like update, not an
opportunity to renegotiate what the rate itself covers.

**Formula(e).**
`Remeasured amount (item) = Rate (fixed) × Quantity (measured, as-built)`
`Variance (item) = Remeasured amount − Billed (BoQ) amount = Rate × (Measured quantity − Billed quantity)`
`Total remeasured value = Σ Remeasured amounts, across all measured-work items`

**Example 7.3.8 — remeasuring Pipeline Segment 4B's measured work at final measurement.**

*Scenario.* At completion, Meridian's site team and the employer's engineer carry out a joint final
measurement of Bill 2, comparing billed (tender) quantities against as-built measured quantities. Ground
conditions required a local reroute of the alignment around an unforeseen obstruction, adding 80 m to the
excavation, pipe-laying and backfill items alike; a design-coordination change removed one of the five
inspection chambers (partly offset by increased wall thickness on the remainder), reducing the concrete
volume from 40 m³ to 36 m³; and the final working-corridor width was confirmed narrower than assumed,
reducing the reinstated area from 6,000 m² to 5,850 m². Testing and commissioning (item 2.05) is billed as a
lump-sum item and is not subject to quantity remeasurement, since its scope is either performed in full or
not at all.

*Required.* The remeasured amount and variance for each item, and the total remeasured value of Bill 2.

*Working.*

| Item | Description | Unit | Billed (BoQ) qty | Rate ($) | Billed amount ($) | Measured (as-built) qty | Remeasured amount ($) | Variance ($) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.01 | Excavate trench | m | 2,000 | 65.00 | 130,000 | 2,080 | 135,200 | +5,200 |
| 2.02 | Supply, lay & joint pipe | m | 2,000 | 121.00 | 242,000 | 2,080 | 251,680 | +9,680 |
| 2.03 | Backfill & compact | m | 2,000 | 28.00 | 56,000 | 2,080 | 58,240 | +2,240 |
| 2.04 | RC valve chamber | m³ | 40 | 484.00 | 19,360 | 36 | 17,424 | (1,936) |
| 2.05 | Testing & commissioning | item | 1 | 18,000.00 | 18,000 | 1 | 18,000 | 0 |
| 2.06 | Reinstatement | m² | 6,000 | 4.50 | 27,000 | 5,850 | 26,325 | (675) |
| **Total** | | | | | **492,360** | | **506,869** | **+14,509** |

Each variance follows directly from `Rate × (Measured − Billed)` — for example, item 2.01:
`65.00 × (2,080 − 2,000) = 65.00 × 80 = +5,200`; item 2.04: `484.00 × (36 − 40) = 484.00 × (−4) = (1,936)`.

*Answer.* **Total remeasured value of Bill 2 = $506,869**, a net **increase of $14,509 (2.9%)** over the
billed $492,360.

*Interpretation.* Three genuinely separate causes drive this net figure — a real, as-built quantity increase
(the alignment reroute, adding a consistent 80 m across three linear items), a real quantity decrease (one
fewer inspection chamber), and a further, smaller decrease (a narrower reinstated corridor) — each traceable
back to a specific site or design event, not a blanket adjustment. The net $14,509 increase sits comfortably
within the $24,618 BoQ-level contingency allowed at tender (7.3.7) — a useful cross-check that the tendered
contingency was reasonably, though not precisely, sized for the kind of quantity movement an admeasurement
contract is designed to absorb (7.3.9 develops this observation in full).

**Mini-case — interim (progressive) remeasurement, not only final.** The Example above represents a single
final measurement, but in practice an admeasurement contract typically remeasures **progressively**, at each
interim valuation, using the quantity of each item executed *to date* rather than waiting until completion —
the identical `Rate × Quantity` mechanic applied to a smaller, partial, and periodically updated quantity.
This progressive approach is what links a BoQ directly to interim payment; its full worked mechanics —
including how quantity-to-date is measured and certified period by period — are developed in KA 7.4, and are
not repeated here.

one traceable back to what actually happened on site."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a genuine lump-sum "item" unit (2.05) as remeasurable by some implied quantity — remeasurement
  applies to items billed in measurable units (m, m², m³, kg, No., and the like), not to true lump-sum items,
  unless the *scope itself* is varied (a variation, 7.3.9, not ordinary remeasurement).
- Allowing quantity variance alone to reopen the *rate* — remeasurement changes quantity only; where a
  quantity change is so large that applying the original rate unchanged becomes genuinely unreasonable, that
  is a distinct, separately governed contractual question, not something to assume automatically follows from
  ordinary remeasurement.
- Leaving all quantity verification to a single, disputed final measurement at project end, rather than
  recording and agreeing quantities progressively — exactly when site evidence and records are hardest to
  reconstruct.

**AI in this topic.** Automated survey and as-built data capture — GPS/GNSS-tracked excavation and pipe-laying
records, drone or laser-scan survey of completed reinstatement areas — increasingly feeds quantity data
directly into remeasurement records with less manual survey effort, and AI-assisted reconciliation tools can
compare as-built survey data against the original design/take-off model and flag the resulting variance
automatically, item by item (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — a genuine, growing efficiency gain over
wholly manual remeasurement. Such tools cannot determine whether a measured variance is *fairly attributable*
to a genuine site condition, as in the reroute above, as opposed to uninstructed extra work or a measurement
methodology error — that determination, and its commercial consequence, remains a contract-administration
judgement for the professional.

a quantity change reflects an instructed variation rather than ordinary as-built variance).

### 7.3.9 Using the BoQ for valuation, cost control and variation pricing

**Definition & purpose.** Beyond compiling and remeasuring the contract, the priced BoQ is the working
commercial tool used throughout construction for three closely related purposes: valuing work completed to
date (interim valuation), monitoring cost performance against the tendered allowance (cost control), and
pricing instructed changes to the work (variation pricing) — each drawing directly on the rates and structure
established earlier in this KA.

**The underlying principle.** All three uses share one idea: the priced BoQ is a *rate book*, not merely a
one-off tender total, and its rates remain the fair, agreed reference price for the described scope
throughout the contract — to be applied directly, by analogy, or as the starting point for a fresh
build-up — to whatever quantity of that scope, or genuinely new scope, actually needs to be valued.

**Formula(e).**
`Interim valuation (item, to date) = Rate × Quantity executed to date` (the same mechanic as remeasurement,
7.3.8, applied progressively — full treatment in KA 7.4).
Valuing an instructed variation, in order of preference:
(i) the **existing billed rate**, where the varied work is the same as, or reasonably similar in kind,
quantity and condition to, a billed item;
(ii) a **pro-rata/adjusted rate**, where the billed rate needs a stated, justified adjustment for a genuine
difference;
(iii) a **star rate**, built up from first principles (7.3.5's method), where no reasonably analogous billed
rate exists at all;
(iv) **dayworks** (7.3.4), only where the work genuinely cannot be fairly measured and valued by any of the
first three routes.

**Example 7.3.9 — pricing a variation with no comparable billed rate (a "star rate").**

*Scenario.* An instructed variation requires a 25 m crossing of 450 mm dia. HDPE pipe at a road crossing not
shown in the tender design — a larger diameter than any pipe billed in the original BoQ (which bills only
300 mm pipe, item 2.02), so no existing rate reasonably covers it.

*Required.* A fair star rate for the new item, using the same method as Example 7.3.5, and the resulting
variation value.

*Working.*
`Labour rate = $20.00/m (same crew and productivity reasonably assumed for a short crossing)`
`Plant rate = $10.00/m (unchanged)`
`Material: 450 mm pipe $95.00/m + bedding & surround $10.00/m + jointing consumables $3.00/m = $108.00/m`
`Direct cost = 20.00 + 10.00 + 108.00 = $138.00/m`
`Overhead (10%) = 13.80; Subtotal = 151.80/m`
`Profit (10%) = 15.18; Star rate = $166.98/m`
`Variation value = 166.98 × 25 = $4,174.50`

*Answer.* **Star rate = $166.98/m; variation value = $4,174.50** (SAR 15,654.38 illustrative).

*Interpretation.* The star rate is built exactly like item 2.02's billed rate — same method, same overhead
and profit percentages, same labour and plant assumptions — with only the genuinely different cost element
(the larger pipe's higher material cost) changed and stated separately. The $45.98/m difference from the
billed 300 mm rate ($121.00/m) is therefore entirely traceable: $38.00/m of higher material cost, plus its
own share of overhead and profit. This transparency is what makes a star rate defensible, in the same way a
fully documented Basis of Estimate is defensible (cross-reference KA 3.2.7) — an undocumented, asserted new
rate is not.

**Mini-case — the closing final account for Pipeline Segment 4B.** Bringing together this KA's remeasurement
(7.3.8) and category behaviour (7.3.4):

| Category | Tender BoQ ($) | Final account ($) |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminaries | 45,000 | 45,000 |
| Measured work | 492,360 | 506,869 |
| Provisional sums | 40,000 | 38,500 |
| PC sums (incl. attendance & profit) | 28,750 | 28,750 |
| Dayworks | 15,000 | 9,200 |
| Contingency | 24,618 | 0 |
| **Total** | **645,728** | **628,319** |

`Final account total = 45,000 + 506,869 + 38,500 + 28,750 + 9,200 + 0 = 628,319`
`Variance vs tender = 628,319 − 645,728 = (17,409)`, an adverse (to the tender total) 2.7% movement.

Despite measured work itself running **$14,509 over** its billed value (7.3.8), the final account settles
**$17,409 below** the original tender sum, because the provisional-sum, dayworks and — especially —
contingency allowances were not fully drawn upon. This is exactly the point 7.3.4 introduced: not every BoQ
category behaves the same way under remeasurement and final account, and a professional reporting "the
project came in over or under" must look at category composition, not the measured-work variance in
isolation. (A complete final account would also add Example 7.3.9's variation, $4,174.50, omitted from this
summary table for clarity of the six established categories — bringing the fully complete illustrative total
to $632,493.50.)

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reaching for dayworks, or an arbitrary new rate, before genuinely testing whether an existing billed rate
  (or a fair pro-rata adjustment of one) already covers the varied work — skipping past the preferred, more
  competitively priced routes in the hierarchy.
- Using a billed rate unadjusted where the *condition* of the work has genuinely changed (for example, the
  same pipe diameter but in significantly harder ground, or an isolated, uneconomically small quantity)
  without recognising that a rate priced for the original bulk quantity and condition may no longer be fair.
- Treating cost control as complete once a variance report shows favourable headline numbers, without
  checking which BoQ categories are actually driving the result — as the mini-case shows, an unfavourable
  measured-work variance can be quietly and misleadingly offset by unrelated, undrawn provisional/dayworks/
  contingency allowances rather than by genuine cost performance.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI and contract-analytics tools can draft a first-cut variation valuation — proposing
whether an existing rate, a pro-rata adjustment or a star rate is the appropriate route, and even drafting the
star-rate build-up itself from a resource-rate library — materially speeding up the commercial response to an
instruction (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). Dashboard tools can track BoQ-category drawdown and flag a
final-account trend in real time, as 7.3.4's AI box also noted. None of this removes the contract-
administration judgement of which valuation route is genuinely fair for a specific variation, nor the sign-
off — typically by the employer's contract administrator or engineer — that a proposed valuation is accepted,
consistent throughout this KA, and this book, with **"AI proposes; the professional disposes."**

### Key terms

- **Bill of Quantities (BoQ)** — an itemised schedule of the work in a project, each item described,
  quantified and (once priced) rated and extended to an amount.
- **Admeasurement (remeasurement) contract** — a contract in which unit rates are fixed at tender and the
  amount payable is determined by applying those rates to quantities actually measured, not to the
  quantities originally billed.
- **Standard method of measurement (SMM)** — a published rulebook (e.g. RICS NRM2, CESMM4, RICS POMI) defining
  how quantities are measured, described and coded, and what a rate is deemed to include.
- **Net measurement** — measuring work as fixed in its final position, excluding wastage or working space,
  which is instead priced within the rate.
- **Preliminaries** — the priced cost of running the site/project itself, distinct from measured permanent
  work.
- **Provisional sum** — a tender-stage allowance for work not yet sufficiently defined, paid only to the
  extent actually instructed.
- **Prime Cost (PC) sum** — a known, specific cost (typically a nominated supplier/subcontractor price)
  included in the BoQ, to which the contractor adds attendance and profit.
- **Dayworks** — a cost-plus-percentage valuation method, using a schedule of rates, for work that cannot be
  fairly measured under normal BoQ rates; a method of last resort.
- **Rate build-up (rate analysis)** — constructing a unit rate from labour, plant and material costs, plus
  overhead and profit, in a traceable sequence.
- **Taking off** — measuring quantities from design drawings (dimension, timesing, squaring, abstracting)
  into billed BoQ quantities.
- **Remeasurement variance** — the difference between an item's billed (BoQ) amount and its remeasured
  amount, arising from a change in measured quantity at the fixed billed rate.
- **Star rate** — a fair rate built from first principles for an item with no reasonably analogous billed
  rate, used to value a variation.
- **Rate loading (front-loading)** — pricing strategy that shifts margin toward items expected early or
  likely to increase in quantity, at the expense of items expected late or likely to decrease, without
  changing the tender total.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 7.3.1 [topic 7.3.1, level: recall]**
What best describes the role of a Bill of Quantities in an admeasurement (remeasurement) contract?
A. It fixes the total contract sum regardless of the quantities executed
B. **It lists priced items with estimated quantities; payment is based on the fixed rates applied to
   quantities actually measured** ✔
C. It replaces the need for any schedule of rates
D. It is used only for cost estimating, never for payment
*Rationale:* Under admeasurement, the rate is the fixed contractual commitment and the quantity is a
tender-stage estimate, settled only by remeasurement (7.3.1, 7.3.8). A describes a lump-sum contract; C and D
misstate the BoQ's ongoing role as a payment instrument.

**MCQ 7.3.2 [topic 7.3.5, level: application — numerical]**
A BoQ item's direct cost, built up from labour, plant and material, is $100.00/m. Overhead is applied at 10%
of direct cost, and profit at 10% of the resulting subtotal. What is the unit rate?
A. $100.00
B. $110.00
C. **$121.00** ✔
D. $130.00
*Rationale:* `100.00 × 1.10 = 110.00`; `110.00 × 1.10 = 121.00` (Example 7.3.5). B stops after applying
overhead only, omitting profit; D incorrectly adds a flat 30% in one step rather than compounding the two
10% additions sequentially.

**MCQ 7.3.3 [topic 7.3.8, level: application — numerical]**
A BoQ item is billed at 40 m³ at a rate of $484.00/m³. At final measurement, the actual quantity is found to
be 36 m³. What is the remeasured amount, and the variance against the billed amount?
A. $19,360; variance $0
B. **$17,424; variance (1,936)** ✔
C. $17,424; variance +1,936
D. $20,328; variance +968
*Rationale:* `Remeasured amount = 484.00 × 36 = 17,424`; billed amount `= 484.00 × 40 = 19,360`; variance
`= 17,424 − 19,360 = (1,936)`, adverse (a reduction), per Example 7.3.8. C states the correct amount but the
wrong sign; D incorrectly increases, rather than decreases, the quantity.

**MCQ 7.3.4 [topic 7.3.4, level: analysis]**
A BoQ contains both a provisional sum and a Prime Cost (PC) sum. What is the key difference between them?
A. There is no real difference; the terms are interchangeable
B. **A provisional sum covers work or cost not yet fully defined at tender, paid only as instructed; a PC
   sum is a known, specific cost (often a nominated supplier/subcontractor) to which the contractor adds
   attendance and profit** ✔
C. A PC sum is always larger in value than a provisional sum
D. Only a provisional sum requires the contractor to add profit
*Rationale:* The two categories differ in how well-defined the underlying cost is at tender, and in how the
contractor is compensated for its own role (7.3.4). C is an unsupported generalisation; D reverses which
category attracts the attendance/profit addition.

**MCQ 7.3.5 [topic 7.3.9, level: analysis]**
An instructed variation requires an item of work with no reasonably comparable billed rate in the BoQ. What
is the recognised, defensible way to price it?
A. Apply the nearest billed rate unchanged, regardless of genuine differences in the work
B. **Build a fair "star rate" from first principles — labour, plant and material, plus overhead and
   profit — adjusted for the genuine differences from any comparable billed item** ✔
C. Refuse to price the variation until a wholly new BoQ is issued
D. Use the Preliminaries lump sum to absorb the cost
*Rationale:* Where no existing rate reasonably applies, a transparently built star rate (Example 7.3.9) is
the standard route, applied only after the existing-rate and pro-rata-adjustment routes have been tested and
found unsuitable (7.3.9's decision hierarchy). A ignores genuine cost differences; C and D are not recognised
valuation routes.

**MCQ 7.3.6 [topic 7.3.3, level: recall]**
In a standard BoQ item line, what does the "Amount" column represent?
A. The unit of measurement used for the item
B. The quantity of work only
C. **Rate multiplied by quantity** ✔
D. The contractor's profit margin on the item alone
*Rationale:* `Amount = Rate × Quantity` (7.3.3). A and B each describe a different single field on the same
line; D confuses the extended amount with the profit component folded into the rate itself (7.3.5).

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. A new BoQ item's direct cost components are: labour $24.00/m, plant $12.00/m, material $74.00/m.
   Overhead is applied at 10% of direct cost, and profit at 10% of the resulting subtotal, applied
   sequentially as in Example 7.3.5. Calculate the unit rate.
2. A BoQ item is billed at 500 m² at a rate of $12.00/m². At final measurement, the actual area is found to
   be 540 m². Calculate the billed amount, the remeasured amount, and the variance.
3. Explain, in one or two sentences, why a Bill of Quantities under an admeasurement contract still needs
   estimated quantities at tender stage, even though the final amount due is based on quantities actually
   measured rather than the quantities originally billed.
4. A contractor prices one BoQ item — expected to be executed early, where quantities are likely to
   increase — well above its genuine build-up cost, while pricing another item — expected late, where
   quantities are likely to decrease — correspondingly below cost, leaving the total tender sum unchanged.
   Name this practice, and explain in one or two sentences why it specifically matters under an admeasurement
   contract.

**Answers**

1. `Direct cost = 24.00 + 12.00 + 74.00 = 110.00`; `after overhead: 110.00 × 1.10 = 121.00`; `after profit:
   121.00 × 1.10 = 133.10`. **Unit rate = $133.10/m.**
2. `Billed amount = 500 × 12.00 = 6,000`; `Remeasured amount = 540 × 12.00 = 6,480`; `Variance = 6,480 −
   6,000 = +480` (favourable to the contractor, since the rate is fixed and the measured quantity increased).
3. Estimated quantities give tenderers a common, itemised scope of comparable size to price on a like-for-
   like basis, giving both parties a consistent reference for tender evaluation and early cash-flow planning;
   without them, tenderers would have no consistent basis on which to compare rates or bids, even though
   those same quantities are explicitly not guaranteed and are superseded by actual measurement (7.3.1,
   7.3.8).
4. This is **rate loading** (a form of front-loading, 7.3.7). Because payment is rate × quantity actually
   measured, the practice is invisible at tender — the total tender sum is unchanged and appears equally
   competitive — but becomes financially consequential once real quantities are measured: if the loaded
   item's quantity does increase and the underpriced item's quantity does decrease, as the contractor
   anticipated, the contractor recovers more than a fairly and evenly priced tender would have, at the
   employer's expense, purely through the ordinary remeasurement mechanism.
