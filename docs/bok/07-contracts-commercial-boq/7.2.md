# Domain 7 — Contracts, Commercial Management, BoQ, Invoicing & Revenue

## KA 7.2 — Contract management

KA 7.1 established that a contract's pricing mechanism is a deliberate allocation of risk, and that Meridian
Rail Contracting LLC's **Pipeline Segment 4B** is priced **lump-sum (fixed-price)**: a contract price of
**$750,000** (SAR 2,812,500 at an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**) against an internal budgeted cost (BAC)
of $600,000 (KA 6.1), an implied margin of $150,000 (20.0% of contract price) (KA 7.1.2, KA 11.1.3). This KA
picks up once that contract is signed and asks the question a project controls professional lives with every
day of execution: **how is the contract actually administered, once work is under way?** Contract
management (or contract administration) is the discipline of converting the document signed at award into a
monitored, evidenced, day-to-day discipline: knowing what each party owes the other (section 7.2.1); pricing and
instructing changes to that scope (section 7.2.2); establishing and substantiating entitlement to more time and
money when an event outside the contractor's own risk occurs (section 7.2.3–section 7.2.4); keeping the records that make
any of that possible (section 7.2.5); analysing delay at a level a professional must recognise, even without running
the analysis personally (section 7.2.6); avoiding and, when unavoidable, resolving disagreement (section 7.2.7);
administering the securities — retention, bonds, guarantees, insurances — that protect each party against the
other's default (section 7.2.8); and bringing the contract to a clean, reconciled close (section 7.2.9). Two standard-form
families are named throughout, at the awareness level KA 7.1.7 set out and never repeated here in clause-level
detail: **FIDIC**, whose colour-named books this KA's terminology follows loosely (the client as "the
Employer," its appointed certifier as "the Engineer"); and **NEC**, whose "compensation event" language covers
broadly the same administrative ground under different names. This book never quotes or cites a specific
clause number from either family — contract administration always requires reading the specific, possibly
amended, document actually in force, not a textbook description of one.

This KA does not repeat KA 5.4's project-controls mechanics of change (the identify→assess→approve→
implement→re-baseline process, the change register, the CCB approval ladder) or KA 12.2–12.3's risk process
and contingency mechanics — both are cross-referenced by number throughout, not re-derived. What this KA adds
is the layer those two leave out: the **contractual** question of entitlement — what the contract itself
says a party is owed, on what evidence, within what time limit, and how a disagreement about any of that is
resolved. The two closed items this KA carries forward and resolves are **VO-01** (KA 5.4.4's isolation valve
chamber addition, cost-assessed at $38,000 but never converted to its contract-price value) and **CL-01**
(KA 5.4.2's claim — 10 working days' extension of time plus $18,000 of prolongation cost for a late tie-in
permit release — logged in KA 5.4's change register as "under review, pending substantiation"). This KA
substantiates and resolves CL-01 in full (section 7.2.3–section 7.2.5) and prices a new variation, **VO-02** (section 7.2.2), so
that section 7.2.9 can close Pipeline Segment 4B's final account against real, traceable figures.

### 7.2.1 The contract as the performance baseline: obligations, roles and administration

**Definition & purpose.** **Contract administration** (PMBOK's Control Procurements process; AACE's TCM
Framework treats it as a continuing commercial-management activity threaded through execution) is the set of
activities that monitor performance against the contract's obligations, action the mechanisms it provides for
change and relief, and keep the record that lets either party demonstrate compliance. The contract itself —
not the cost baseline, not the schedule baseline — is the **primary obligation baseline**: it states what each
party must do, by when, for how much, and what happens if either fails.

**The underlying principle.** A contract is a risk-allocation instrument first and a payment mechanism second
(KA 7.1.1). Administering it well means routinely asking, of every event that departs from plan, a single
question: **whose risk was this, under this specific contract, and does the contract's machinery for
responding to it — variation, claim, or simple absorption — actually apply?** A professional who skips this
question and defaults to "we'll sort the money out later" is not managing the contract; they are hoping it
manages itself. A commonly stated professional heuristic captures the discipline this whole KA builds toward:
**entitlement = a genuine contractual right + a compliant notice + evidence that substantiates it.** All
three are necessary; any one missing and an otherwise real entitlement can be lost or reduced — the theme
section 7.2.3–section 7.2.5 develop in full.

**Roles and authority.** Three parties administer the contract day to day: **the Employer** (the client),
**the Engineer** (the Employer's appointed certifier and contract administrator — under NEC-style terms, a
comparable role is often called the Project Manager), and **Meridian**, the Contractor. Their respective
authority to sign or approve is distinct from, and should not be confused with, two other authority tables
already established in this book: KA 11.1.3's contract-signing DOA (Sales Executive to $100,000; Commercial
Manager $100,001–$500,000; Commercial Director above $500,000 — the authority to *commit* Meridian to a
contract) and KA 5.4.6's change-approval ladder (Project Controls Manager to $10,000; Project Manager to
$25,000; the Change Control Board up to available management reserve; the Client/Sponsor beyond — the
authority to fund a *cost impact* internally). This KA's roles govern a third, separate question: who, on the
contract itself, may instruct, value, certify or determine.

**Not every cost-impacting event needs this machinery.** KA 12.3.3's R1 (adverse ground conditions on the

all. CL-01 (section 7.2.3–section 7.2.5, below), by contrast, asserts that the Employer caused a delay and must compensate

The first question a professional asks of any adverse event is exactly this one: does it stay inside the
organisation (Domain 12's risk process) or does it cross the contract boundary (this KA)?

**Mini-case — an unconfirmed verbal instruction.** Early in Pipeline Segment 4B's execution, a site engineer
accepts a verbal request from the Engineer's site representative to relocate a small section of temporary
fencing "while you're there," and Meridian proceeds without a written instruction. The work is minor and
uncontroversial, but three weeks later nobody can point to a document establishing that it was properly
instructed, at what rate, or with the Employer's authority behind it. This is the same failure mode KA 5.4.1's
mini-case describes from the cost-control side (a $34,000 unlogged verbal instruction); this KA's version of
the lesson is contractual, not just financial — a verbal instruction, however well-intentioned, creates no
reliable evidentiary trail if either party later disputes that it happened at all.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Filing the contract away after signature and administering the project purely from
the cost/schedule baselines, forgetting that the contract itself is the primary source of entitlement and
obligation. (2) Proceeding with instructed or claimed work before a written instruction or notice exists (the
mini-case above), leaving no record for a later determination. (3) Confusing the three separate authority
tables in this topic — contract-signing DOA, internal change-funding DOA, and contract-administration roles —
which answer different questions and are not interchangeable.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-analytics/CLM tools (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 11.1.3) can extract an

checklist, giving a controls team a fast first read of a long document. Such extraction cannot verify that a
specific site event actually falls within, or outside, a given clause's scope, nor substitute for reading the
full, possibly amended, document before a real entitlement decision is made — that remains the contract
administrator's judgement, informed by, never delegated to, the tool.

### 7.2.2 Variations and change orders: instruction and the valuation of varied work

**Definition & purpose.** A **variation** (or **variation order, VO**) is the contractual instrument through
which the Employer, via the Engineer, formally instructs a change to the contracted scope (KA 5.4.3 already
distinguishes a variation from a scope change, a trend and a claim — not repeated here). Once instructed, a
variation is not normally disputed as to whether it happened, only as to its **value** — and valuing varied
work correctly is this topic's job. This is distinct from **claim** valuation (section 7.2.4), which compensates a
loss the contractor asserts it has suffered, not new work the Employer has asked for.

**The underlying principle — the valuation hierarchy.** Even on a lump-sum contract, the tender documents
commonly include a priced **Schedule of Rates** (or, where the contract is remeasured, a full Bill of
Quantities, KA 7.1.3, KA 7.3), used specifically to value instructed changes to the base scope. A widely used
valuation hierarchy, common across standard forms without needing a specific clause number to state it,
applies in descending order of preference: (1) at the contract's own priced rate, where the varied work is of
**similar character and executed under similar conditions** to work already priced; (2) at that same rate,
**adjusted** where conditions differ materially but a base rate still exists; (3) at a **fair valuation** (a
"star" or "new" rate) built up from first principles — labour, material and plant cost, plus the contractor's
normal overhead and profit (O&P) — where the contract's rates offer no reasonable basis at all. Because a
priced rate already embeds the contractor's normal margin, valuing a variation at rate (steps 1–2) or at a
properly loaded star rate (step 3) is not the same exercise as pricing an internal cost impact — a
distinction this topic now resolves for VO-01.

**Reconciling KA 5.4's cost view with the contract-price view.** KA 5.4.4 priced VO-01's total **cost impact**
to Meridian at $38,000 (direct scope $32,000 plus $6,000 of schedule-driven indirect cost) — the figure that
correctly drove KA 5.4.6's internal reserve mechanics (BAC, management reserve). That $38,000 is Meridian's
own cost; it is not, by itself, what the Employer owes for the variation. Because Meridian's established
target margin on this contract is 20.0% **of contract price** (KA 7.1.2) — equivalently, cost is 80.0% of
price — the **contract-price value** of a cost-based variation valuation is obtained by dividing, not
multiplying, by the cost share:

`Contract-price value = Cost impact ÷ (1 − Margin % of price) = 38,000 ÷ 0.80 = 47,500`

VO-01 therefore adds **$47,500** (SAR 178,125) to Pipeline Segment 4B's contract price, not $38,000 — the
$9,500 difference is Meridian's normal margin on the new scope, exactly as it would be priced into any other
instructed work under this lump-sum contract.

**Formula(e).**

`Valuation at contract rate (similar work) = Actual measured quantity × Existing priced rate`

`Fair/star rate = (Labour cost + Material cost + Plant cost) × (1 + O&P %)`, where O&P% is set so that margin
equals the contract's established percentage of price (here, cost × 1.25 recovers a 20.0% margin of price,
since 1 ÷ 0.80 = 1.25)

**Example 7.2.2 — valuing VO-02: a route deviation on Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* At Month 6, the Engineer instructs a variation: reroute a short section of the pipeline around a
newly discovered utility crossing. Two elements of varied work result. (a) An additional **120 m** of
trenching in the same normal-ground conditions already priced elsewhere in the contract — work of identical
character, valued at the existing rate. Meridian's internal budgeted cost rate for this trenching, already
established at $100/m (KA 7.1.3, citing KA 6.1's Example 6.1.4), grosses up to a **priced rate of $125/m**
under the same 20.0%-of-price margin convention just applied to VO-01 (`100 ÷ 0.80 = 125`). (b) **25 m** of
thrust-bored steel casing pipe under the adjacent road, to protect the rerouted line — work of a materially
different character and method, with no applicable rate in the priced schedule, requiring a fair (star) rate
built up from first principles: labour $40/m, materials $220/m, plant $140/m (direct cost $400/m), loaded at
25.0% on cost (equivalent to the same 20.0% margin on price) to give a **star rate of $500/m**.

*Required.* (a) The value of the additional trenching. (b) The star rate and the value of the casing-pipe
work. (c) VO-02's total value.

*Working.*

(a) `120 m × 125/m = 15,000`

(b) `Direct cost = 40 + 220 + 140 = 400/m`; `O&P = 25.0% × 400 = 100/m`; `Star rate = 400 + 100 = 500/m`;
`25 m × 500/m = 12,500`

(c) `VO-02 total = 15,000 + 12,500 = 27,500`

*Answer.* **VO-02 = $27,500 (SAR 103,125)** — $15,000 for the additional trenching at the existing priced
rate, $12,500 for the road-crossing casing pipe at a newly built star rate.

*Interpretation.* Both valuation routes protect Meridian's normal margin without over- or under-pricing the
work: the trenching extension uses no new judgement at all (quantity × existing rate), while the casing pipe
— genuinely new in kind, not just in quantity — needs a first-principles build precisely because no existing
rate reasonably covers thrust-boring under a live road. Confusing the two routes is the commonest valuation
error this topic addresses next.

**Mini-case — valuing the wrong kind of work at the wrong rate.** A different estimator on Meridian's team,
asked to value the casing-pipe element quickly, instead applies the existing $125/m trenching rate to all
145 m of VO-02 (120 m trenching + 25 m casing), reasoning that "it's still pipeline work." This undervalues
the casing-pipe element by `500 − 125 = 375/m`, or `25 × 375 = 9,375` — work requiring specialist thrust-boring
plant and a different crew, priced as if it were routine open-cut trenching. The valuation hierarchy exists
precisely to stop this: an existing rate only applies to work of **similar character executed under similar
conditions**; the moment either condition fails, the professional must move to the next step of the
hierarchy, not stretch a convenient but inapplicable rate to cover unfamiliar scope.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Applying an existing rate to work of a materially different character or method

different questions and are not interchangeable, as this topic's VO-01 reconciliation shows. (3) Proceeding
with varied work before a written instruction exists (section 7.2.1's mini-case) — valuation cannot begin on work
that was never properly instructed in the first place.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-analytics and quantity-extraction tools can propose which existing rate a new
item of varied work most closely resembles, and can draft a first-pass star-rate build-up from historical
labour/material/plant cost data (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — a genuine time saving on a valuation
that would otherwise start from a blank page. Such tools cannot judge whether the "similar character, similar
conditions" test is actually met for a specific piece of work — the mini-case's misvaluation is exactly the
kind of error a plausible-looking but unverified AI suggestion could reproduce at speed; the valuation basis
itself remains a professional determination.

### 7.2.3 Claims I — extension of time: entitlement, notice and time-bars

**Definition & purpose.** An **extension of time (EOT)** is the contractual mechanism that moves the Time for
Completion later, in response to an event the contract recognises as **excusable** (not the contractor's own
risk). An excusable delay may be **compensable** (the contractor is also entitled to money, typically because
the Employer caused it) or **non-compensable** (time only — e.g. an agreed neutral event such as exceptionally
adverse weather beyond what was allowed for, depending on the specific contract). KA 7.1.2 already showed the
mirror case: an unforeseen ground condition on Pipeline Segment 4B that the contract did **not** treat as
compensable, leaving Meridian to absorb a $45,000 cost increase entirely within margin — a reminder that not
every adverse event on a lump-sum contract crosses into this topic's territory at all; classifying an event
correctly, before reaching for EOT machinery, is itself the first professional judgement KA 7.1.2 flagged for
this KA to develop.

**The underlying principle.** Two separate tests must both be passed before an EOT is granted. First, **is
the delay to the critical path, net of any float already available** at the point the event occurs — a
delay that lands entirely within existing float changes nothing about the finish date and earns no EOT (the
same net-schedule-impact logic KA 5.4.4 already applied to VO-01, now applied to a claim rather than an
instructed variation). Second, **was formal notice given within the time the contract allows** — a genuine
entitlement can still be lost, in whole or in part, if notice is late or missing. This is the **time-bar**:
many standard forms require notice within a stated period from when the contractor became aware, or should
have become aware, of the delaying event — commonly of the order of 28 days under forms in the FIDIC family,
with NEC's compensation-event mechanism requiring an early warning followed by formal notification within a
comparably short window. The exact period, and precisely what triggers it, vary by form, edition and any
bespoke amendment; this book states an **illustrative ~28-day period** for teaching purposes only, and a
professional must always read the actual period from the specific contract in force, never assume it.

**Formula.**

`Net critical delay = Gross duration of the delaying event − Available float at the point it occurs`
(floored at zero)

`Notice validity: (Notice date − Awareness date) ≤ Contractual notice period`

**Example 7.2.3 — CL-01's extension-of-time entitlement.**

*Scenario.* KA 5.4.2's register logged CL-01 at Month 5: "10 days EOT + prolongation, late tie-in permit
release," originated by Meridian's commercial team, status "under review, pending substantiation." The
underlying event: the Employer's permit for the WP4 tie-in (the same tie-in scope VO-01 also touches, KA
5.4.4) was due for release on Month 4, Day 5. It was not released until Month 4, Day 17 — a **gross idle
period of 12 working days** during which Meridian's tie-in crew and specialist plant could not proceed. A
further Level-3 schedule review at this specific point in the tie-in sequence — the same finer granularity KA
5.4.4 used for VO-01, reconciled there against KA 10.2's coarser eight-activity network, where the
corresponding activity carries zero total float overall — finds **2 working days of float** available before
this particular delay reached the critical path. Meridian became aware the permit would be late on Month 4,
Day 8 (three working days after the due date passed with no release); a **notice** of intention to claim was
issued to the Engineer on Month 4, Day 22, with the fully particularised claim (CL-01, quantified in
section 7.2.4–section 7.2.5) submitted the following month, once the delay's full effect could be properly assessed — a
common, and here entirely proper, distinction between a prompt notice and a later, fuller substantiation.

*Required.* (a) The net critical delay. (b) Whether notice was given within the illustrative 28-day time-bar.
(c) The EOT to be determined.

*Working.*

(a) `Net critical delay = 12 − 2 = 10 working days`

(b) `Notice date (Day 22) − Awareness date (Day 8) = 14 working days ≤ 28-day illustrative bar` → **valid**

(c) Both tests pass: the delay is genuinely net-critical, and notice was timely.

*Answer.* **Net critical delay = 10 working days, matching CL-01's claimed figure exactly; notice is valid
(14 days, well inside the illustrative 28-day bar); an EOT of 10 working days is properly founded.**

*Interpretation.* The 10-day figure was not simply asserted by Meridian's commercial team — it is the same
methodical net-schedule-impact calculation this book has already applied once, to VO-01, now applied to a
claimed event instead of an instructed one. That consistency is itself part of what makes CL-01 credible to
an Engineer assessing it: the same discipline, applied evenhandedly to both variations and claims, is far
more persuasive than a number that appears only when money is being claimed.

**Mini-case — an entitlement lost to a missed time-bar.** KA 12.3.3's R3 (subcontractor mobilisation delay,
Month 1, actual cost $23,000, drawn from Meridian's own contingency with no fresh approval needed) also
caused a short schedule slip to the procurement sequence. Unlike CL-01, however, Meridian's site team did not
issue formal notice of the schedule effect until Month 1, Day 40 — well beyond the illustrative 28-day bar
from when the delay became apparent. The **direct cost** ($23,000) remains entirely unaffected: it was
already funded from contingency, an internal decision requiring no notice to the Employer at all (section 7.2.1).
But the **time and prolongation entitlement** — the EOT and any associated time-related cost R3's delay might
otherwise have justified — is lost, not because the underlying event was not genuinely delaying, but purely
because notice was late. This is the sharpest lesson a time-bar teaches: a real entitlement and a lost
entitlement can share the exact same underlying fact pattern, differing only in whether a deadline for
paperwork was met.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Waiting for the delaying event to be fully resolved before giving any notice at all
— a prompt notice of intention to claim, followed by fuller particulars later, is normal practice and
protects entitlement even before quantum is known. (2) Using the event's **gross** duration as the claimed
delay without checking available float at that specific point in the sequence, overstating the entitlement.
(3) Assuming an internally well-managed risk (funded from contingency, no dispute about the cost) means no
external notice was needed — the two questions (who pays internally vs what the contract entitles Meridian to
claim externally) are entirely separate, as the mini-case shows.

**AI in this topic.** Schedule-analytics tools can flag, from a digital programme, which activities and dates
correspond to a stated delaying event and calculate net critical impact automatically once a Level-3 model is
built and current (cross-reference KA 5.4.4, KA 13.5) — a genuine speed advantage once the underlying schedule
data exists. Correspondence-monitoring tools can also flag an approaching notice deadline from a logged
awareness date (cross-reference KA 13.4). Neither can determine, on its own authority, whether an event is
genuinely excusable under the specific contract, nor guarantee the underlying schedule model is itself
complete and current enough to trust — both remain the contract administrator's responsibility.

### 7.2.4 Claims II — prolongation, disruption and acceleration

**Definition & purpose.** Three related but distinct cost consequences follow a delaying or disrupting event,
and confusing them is a common, costly error. **Prolongation** is the time-related cost of an EOT-entitled
extension — overheads that continue to be incurred for longer than planned because the contract now runs
longer. **Disruption** is a loss of productivity or efficiency caused by an event, whether or not the overall
completion date moves at all — work simply costs more, or takes longer *per unit*, without necessarily
extending the finish. **Acceleration** is the cost of deliberately compressing remaining work to recover lost
time, either **instructed** (the Employer directs it, and should compensate for it) or **constructive** (the
contractor accelerates on its own initiative, commonly after a wrongly rejected or delayed EOT, to avoid
exposure to liquidated damages it should not, in fact, bear).

**The underlying principle — claims are valued at cost, not at rate.** section 7.2.2 valued VO-01 and VO-02 at rates
that include Meridian's normal margin, because a variation is new work the Employer has asked for and should
pay for on the same commercial terms as the rest of the contract. A claim is different: it compensates
Meridian for a **loss actually suffered**, not for new work performed, so — as most standard forms draw this
distinction explicitly, at concept level, without this book asserting a specific clause number — prolongation
and disruption are valued at the contractor's **actual, substantiated cost**, without an additional profit
margin loaded on top. This is why CL-01's $18,000 prolongation entitlement, quantified below, carries no
20.0% margin uplift, unlike VO-01's and VO-02's contract-price values in section 7.2.2.

**Formula(e).**

`Prolongation cost = Time-related cost (TRC) rate ($/working day) × Net critical delay (working days, section 7.2.3)`

`Disruption cost (measured-mile method) = Productivity loss % × Labour cost incurred in the impacted period`,
where `Productivity loss % = (Reference-period output rate − Impacted-period output rate) ÷ Reference-period
output rate`

**Example 7.2.4 — quantifying CL-01's prolongation claim.**

*Scenario.* CL-01's 10-working-day net critical delay (section 7.2.3) is substantiated against two time-related cost
components. (1) **General time-related overhead** — site supervision, standing plant, temporary facilities —
at the project's already-established rate of $1,200 per working day (KA 5.4.4). (2) **Specialist tie-in crew
and plant standby**, kept mobilised specifically awaiting the permit and evidenced by daily standby dockets
co-signed by the Engineer's site representative, at $600 per working day.

*Required.* (a) The general time-related overhead cost. (b) The specialist standby cost. (c) The total
prolongation claim.

*Working.*

(a) `10 working days × 1,200/day = 12,000`

(b) `10 working days × 600/day = 6,000`

(c) `Total = 12,000 + 6,000 = 18,000`

*Answer.* **Prolongation cost = $18,000 (SAR 67,500)** — matching CL-01's claimed figure exactly.

*Interpretation.* The general overhead component ($12,000) rests on a rate this book has already established
and applied once before, without dispute (VO-01, KA 5.4.4) — largely uncontroversial. The specialist standby
component ($6,000) is genuinely new to this claim and is exactly where substantiation matters most, since it
is an event-specific cost, not the project's standing rate; section 7.2.5 shows what evidence turns this component
from an assertion into an agreed figure.

**Mini-case — disruption and acceleration on the same programme.** Two further, separate illustrations,
distinct from CL-01, show the other two claim types. *Disruption:* re-sequencing forced by the tie-in delay
temporarily disrupts an unrelated stretch of pipe-laying and welding work. In a comparable, unimpacted
reference period, Meridian's crew welds 50 m/day; during the disrupted period, output falls to 35 m/day —
`Productivity loss % = (50 − 35) / 50 = 30.0%`. Applied to an illustrative $28,000 of labour cost incurred
during the disrupted period (a measured-mile calculation, distinct from and additional to WP4's definitive
costed figures used elsewhere in this book): `Disruption cost = 30.0% × 28,000 = 8,400`. *Acceleration:* had
CL-01's 10 working days (2 weeks) **not** been granted as an EOT, Meridian would have faced liquidated-damages
exposure of `2 weeks × 15,000/week = 30,000` (KA 10.3.2's established rate) for a delay it did not cause.
Accelerating other activities to absorb the 2 weeks instead, at an illustrative cost of $20,000, would have
been the cheaper response — a net benefit of `30,000 − 20,000 = 10,000` over simply absorbing the LDs — a
comparison a professional runs *before* recommending acceleration, never after the cost is already committed.

### 7.2.5 Records and contemporaneous evidence

**Definition & purpose.** **Contemporaneous records** — site diaries, correspondence, instructions, progress
photographs, weather logs, labour and plant returns, standby dockets — are the evidentiary backbone without
which even a genuinely well-founded claim can fail. The Society of Construction Law's **Delay and Disruption
Protocol**, a widely referenced piece of international industry guidance (not a contract form in itself),
sets out — in outline, and described here in this book's own words, never quoted — good record-keeping as a
foundational discipline for delay and disruption claims generally, precisely because a delay analysis or a
quantum build (section 7.2.4, section 7.2.6) is only as reliable as the underlying data it is built from.

**The underlying principle.** Entitlement without substantiation rarely succeeds, however genuine the
underlying event. The professional maxim from section 7.2.1 — entitlement equals a genuine right, a compliant notice,
**and** substantiating evidence — is completed here: the first two elements can be perfectly satisfied and a
claim can still fail, or succeed only in part, for want of the third.

**Example 7.2.5 — substantiating and closing out CL-01.**

*Scenario.* Following section 7.2.3's valid notice and section 7.2.4's quantified $18,000 claim, the Engineer requests
Meridian's supporting records before determining CL-01. Meridian produces: (1) correspondence recording the
permit's original due date and its actual release date; (2) the site diary entry logging the tie-in crew as
idle from Month 4, Day 8; (3) daily standby dockets for the specialist tie-in crew and plant, each co-signed
by the Engineer's own site representative, for all 10 claimed working days; (4) the notice correspondence of
Month 4, Day 22; (5) labour and plant time returns underpinning both the $12,000 general-overhead and $6,000
specialist-standby components.

*Required.* Assess whether the record set substantiates CL-01 in full.

*Working/Answer.* Every element of section 7.2.3's and section 7.2.4's calculation is independently evidenced: the notice
date is documented (not merely asserted), the idle period is logged contemporaneously rather than
reconstructed afterward, and — critically — the specialist-standby component, the part of the claim that
carries no pre-existing, uncontested rate to fall back on, is co-signed by the Employer's own representative
on each of the 10 days, removing any dispute about whether the standby genuinely occurred. **CL-01's status
updates from "under review, pending substantiation" (KA 5.4.2) to Agreed: 10 working days' EOT, $18,000
prolongation cost, in full.**

*Interpretation.* The general-overhead component needed little independent evidence because it rests on an
already-agreed project rate; the specialist-standby component needed a great deal, because it is a new,
event-specific assertion. A professional preparing a claim should expect, and prepare for, exactly this
asymmetry — evidentiary effort belongs where a figure is genuinely contestable, not spread evenly across every
line regardless of how settled it already is.

**Mini-case — a claim that fails for want of records.** Separately, Meridian's site team believes part of the
delay attributed to R2 (weld defect rework, already funded internally from contingency per KA 12.3) was in
fact caused by the Engineer's own delay in approving a revised weld procedure — a potentially compensable,
Employer-caused element within an otherwise contractor-risk event. No instruction log, no correspondence
trail and no contemporaneous note of the approval delay exist; the assertion rests entirely on a site
supervisor's after-the-fact recollection, written up only once the claim was being prepared, weeks later. The
Engineer rejects this element of the claim for want of substantiation — not because the underlying assertion
is necessarily untrue, but because nothing on record can independently confirm it. R2's **direct cost** remains
entirely unaffected, since it was never dependent on this claim (section 7.2.1's internal/external distinction again);
only the additional, Employer-attributed element is lost, and lost purely for a records failure, not a
substantive one.

### 7.2.6 Delay analysis methods (awareness level)

**Definition & purpose.** Where a delay's cause and effect are genuinely contested — unlike CL-01's
relatively simple, single-event case — a more formal **delay analysis** may be needed. The Society of
Construction Law's Delay and Disruption Protocol (section 7.2.5) describes several recognised method families, at
awareness level here and never at clause- or section-number level: **impacted as-planned** (inserting a
delay event into the original baseline programme and reading off the effect — quick, but ignores how the
programme actually changed since); **time impact analysis (TIA)** (AACE's Recommended Practice 52R-06,
already named in KA 5.4.4 — inserting a change or delay event into the *current, updated* schedule at the
time it occurred, a prospective method well suited to assessing an event as it happens); **windows/
contemporaneous period analysis** (dividing the whole programme into consecutive time windows and analysing
critical-path movement window by window, using the schedule updates actually issued at the time); and
**as-planned versus as-built (collapsed as-built, or "but-for")** (comparing the baseline directly against
what was actually built, or removing delay events one by one from the as-built record to see what would have
happened without them — a retrospective method, typically used only once the project is complete or nearly
so).

**The underlying principle.** Different methods, applied honestly to the same facts, can produce different
delay allocations — not because one method is "wrong," but because they use different data and ask
subtly different questions. The right method depends on **what data is available and when the analysis is
being done** (prospectively, as CL-01's TIA-style net-schedule-impact assessment was in section 7.2.3, or
retrospectively, after the fact), never on which method happens to produce the most favourable answer for
whichever party is choosing it. A further complication, genuinely unsettled and jurisdiction-dependent, is
**concurrency**: where a compensable, Employer-caused delay and a non-compensable, contractor-caused delay
run over the same period, some approaches deny compensation for the concurrent period entirely (on the
reasoning that the contractor would have been delayed regardless), while others apportion responsibility
between the two causes. This book states both approaches exist and are used in practice; it does not assert
either as the universal rule, because the position genuinely differs by contract, jurisdiction and specific
facts.

**Formula.** No single arithmetic formula covers every method; section 7.2.3 already gave the specific relationship
(`Net critical delay = Gross duration − Available float`) this book uses for a single-event, prospective
assessment — a simplified, teaching-level instance of the TIA family, not a full method in its own right.

**Illustrative comparison — CL-01 assessed prospectively vs what a retrospective method would need.**
section 7.2.3's assessment of CL-01 was, in substance, a simple prospective TIA: the event was inserted into the
current Level-3 schedule at the time it occurred, and its net effect read off directly. A **windows analysis**
covering the same period would instead need every schedule update issued across Months 3–6, not just the
single data point used here, to confirm the critical path's position independently at each window boundary —
materially more data, and correspondingly more assurance, but not achievable until those updates actually
exist. A **collapsed as-built** analysis could only be run once Pipeline Segment 4B is complete, using the
full as-built record — useful for a final account dispute (section 7.2.9) but not for a decision the Engineer needs
to make in real time, as CL-01 required.

**Mini-case — a hypothetical concurrency.** Suppose, contrary to section 7.2.5's actual mini-case, the
weld-procedure assertion had been fully substantiated, and its 4 working days of Employer-caused approval
delay had genuinely overlapped CL-01's own permit-delay window. Under a **dominant-cause** approach, an
analyst asks which of the two causes actually governed the critical path during the overlap and allocates the
whole overlap period to that cause. Under an **apportionment** approach, the overlap period's cost is instead
split between the two causes, commonly in proportion to their assessed contribution. Neither approach is
adopted as this book's house rule; a professional facing genuine concurrency must establish which approach
the specific contract, its governing law, and the parties' agreed dispute-resolution forum actually apply,
rather than assume either as a default.

### 7.2.7 Dispute avoidance and resolution: negotiation, adjudication and arbitration

**Definition & purpose.** A **dispute resolution ladder** runs, in order of increasing cost, formality and
finality: **negotiation** between the parties directly; **mediation or a dispute board** — FIDIC's family of
forms describes a **Dispute Avoidance/Adjudication Board (DAAB)**, and NEC provides for an **adjudicator**,
both awareness-level concepts here, never cited by clause number — a lighter-weight, often faster, frequently
contractually mandatory step before formal proceedings; and **arbitration or litigation**, the most formal,
slowest and most expensive route, commonly the final step for an international construction contract that
specifies arbitration (under the rules of an institution such as the ICC, named here only as a
widely-recognised example, not a recommendation) as its governing dispute forum.

**The underlying principle — avoidance is the cheapest and most reliable stage.** Most disputes never need to
reach the top of this ladder if the discipline section 7.2.1–section 7.2.5 describe — clear roles, proper notice,
contemporaneous records, honest valuation — is actually followed. Escalation is costly in money, time and
relationship, roughly in that increasing order up the ladder; a professional's real skill is not winning at
arbitration, it is making arbitration unnecessary.

**Illustrative relative comparison (order of magnitude, not a fixed rule).**

| Route | Typical relative cost | Typical relative time | Formality | Binding? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Negotiation | Lowest | Fastest (days–weeks) | Informal | Only if both parties agree |
| Mediation / dispute board | Low–medium | Weeks–months | Semi-formal | Often not binding (mediation) or binding pending further reference (dispute board, form-dependent) |
| Adjudication | Medium | Weeks–a few months (often statutorily or contractually fast-tracked) | Formal | Typically binding until/unless revisited in arbitration or litigation |
| Arbitration / litigation | Highest | Months–years | Most formal | Binding, generally final |

**Example 7.2.7 — CL-01 resolved by negotiation.**

*Scenario.* Following section 7.2.5's full substantiation, the Engineer and Meridian's commercial team meet to
discuss CL-01's determination.

*Required/Working/Answer.* Because the record set independently confirms every element of the claim (section 7.2.5),
the Engineer determines CL-01 in full at first review — 10 working days' EOT, $18,000 prolongation — and the
matter closes at the **negotiation** stage, the cheapest and fastest rung of the ladder, without ever needing
mediation, adjudication or arbitration.

*Interpretation.* This is not a coincidence; it is the payoff of section 7.2.1–section 7.2.5's discipline, applied
consistently. A claim that arrives fully evidenced, valued on a defensible basis, and consistent with
methods already used elsewhere on the same contract gives an Engineer little reason to dispute it and no
reason to escalate.

**Mini-case — the weld-procedure claim proceeds to adjudication.** The unsubstantiated weld-procedure element
from section 7.2.5's mini-case is not simply dropped — Meridian's commercial team, believing the underlying
entitlement is genuine even without full records, refers it to adjudication once negotiation fails to resolve
it. Without contemporaneous evidence, the adjudicator can only partially credit the claim — accepting that
some delay occurred but unable to independently confirm the Employer-caused proportion Meridian asserts —
awarding a fraction of the amount claimed. The outcome is not that the claim was baseless; it is that a
genuinely stronger record, prepared at the time rather than reconstructed later, would very likely have
avoided the need for adjudication at all and secured a larger, faster, cheaper recovery — the clearest
possible illustration of why section 7.2.5's records discipline is itself a dispute-avoidance tool, not merely an
evidentiary one.

### 7.2.8 Retention, bonds, guarantees and insurances

**Definition & purpose.** Beyond the money owed for work done, a construction contract typically carries
several **security instruments** protecting each party against the other's default. **Retention** — a
percentage of each certified amount withheld by the Employer and released later — is already established for
Pipeline Segment 4B at **10.0% of each certified amount**, released 50% at Taking-Over (Practical Completion)
and 50% at the end of a stated Defects Notification Period (DNP), reaching **$75,000** in total at completion
(KA 11.1.5, 11.1.7, not repeated here — this topic covers retention's contractual purpose only, cross-
referencing that KA's full billing and receivable mechanics). A **performance bond** and an **advance payment
guarantee** are this topic's own new content: on-demand or conditional financial instruments, issued by a
bank or surety, that let the Employer draw funds directly if Meridian defaults, without first having to prove
loss through litigation. **Insurances** — commonly a Contractors' All Risks (CAR) or Erection All Risks (EAR)
policy covering physical loss or damage to the works, and third-party liability cover — transfer physical and
liability risk to an insurer entirely, a different protective mechanism again from a bond or retention, which
protect against the *other party's* financial default rather than physical loss.

**The underlying principle.** Retention, bonds and advance guarantees all protect the Employer, but at
different points in the contract's life and against different failure modes: retention is drawn from money
already earned, protecting against defects discovered after payment; a performance bond is an independent
promise from a third party (the surety), protecting against Meridian's outright default regardless of what
has or has not yet been certified; an advance payment guarantee protects the Employer's up-front cash advance
specifically, recovered as work proceeds. A professional must be able to state each instrument's value,
trigger and expiry without conflating them.

**Formula(e).**

`Performance bond value = Bond % × Contract price`

`Advance payment = Advance % × Contract price`; `Recovery point (cumulative certified value at which the
advance is fully recovered) = Advance ÷ Recovery rate %`

**Example 7.2.8 — Pipeline Segment 4B's securities.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's contract carries a performance bond at 10.0% of contract price, an advance payment of
12.0% of contract price recovered at 15.0% of each certified amount, and the retention terms already
established in KA 11.1.

*Required.* (a) The performance bond value. (b) The advance payment, and the cumulative certified value at
which it is fully recovered. (c) A comparison with retention's total value at completion.

*Working.*

(a) `Performance bond = 10.0% × 750,000 = 75,000`

(b) `Advance payment = 12.0% × 750,000 = 90,000`; `Recovery point = 90,000 / 15.0% = 600,000` (80.0% of
contract price)

(c) `Retention total at completion (KA 11.1.7) = 75,000`

*Answer.* **Performance bond = $75,000 (SAR 281,250); advance payment = $90,000 (SAR 337,500), fully
recovered once cumulative certified value reaches $600,000 (80.0% of contract price).**

*Interpretation.* The performance bond's $75,000 value coincidentally equals total retention at completion —
both happen to use a 10.0% convention on this contract — but they are entirely different instruments: the
bond is available from day one and covers outright default, while retention only accumulates as certificates
are issued and covers defects found after payment. A professional citing "$75,000 of security" without
specifying which instrument is being discussed would leave an auditor unable to tell whether $75,000, or in
fact $150,000 of combined security, is actually in place at any given point.

**Mini-case — retention release delayed by an unresolved defects list.** At Taking-Over, the Engineer
identifies a short snagging list of minor defects still to be made good before the Taking-Over certificate can
be issued. Meridian completes the listed items within two weeks, but administrative delay in re-inspection
pushes the certificate — and with it, the first 50.0% retention release ($37,500) — back by a further five
weeks beyond when the physical work was actually finished. The cash-flow effect (cross-reference KA 3.5) is
real: $37,500 that should have been released on completion of the punch-list sits, earning nothing, for five
additional weeks purely because of a certification delay, not a contractual entitlement dispute — a reminder
that administering retention well means chasing the *certificate*, not just completing the *work*.

### 7.2.9 Contract close-out and the final account

**Definition & purpose.** **Close-out** is the sequence that formally ends a contract's financial and
contractual exposure: **Taking-Over** (or Practical Completion) marks the point the works are fit for their
intended purpose, opening the Defects Notification Period (DNP); the **final account** reconciles every
variation, agreed claim and deduction into a single, agreed final contract value; the **final certificate**
and **final payment** discharge the remaining balance; and records are archived and lessons captured (cross-
reference KA 8.5, Closing, once written). A final account left unresolved is not a minor administrative
loose end — it is unresolved financial exposure for both parties, sometimes for years.

**The underlying principle.** The final account is the single number every earlier topic in this KA has been
building toward: it must reconcile, item by item, to the original contract price plus every variation and
agreed claim this book (and its sibling KAs) has already valued — never a fresh, unreconciled total asserted
at the end.

**Formula.**

`Final contract price = Original contract price + Σ(agreed variations, at contract-price/rate value) +
Σ(agreed claims, at cost value) − Liquidated damages (if the extended completion date, inclusive of all
agreed EOTs, is still exceeded) − Any other agreed deduction`

**Example 7.2.9 — Pipeline Segment 4B's final account.**

*Scenario.* By the point Pipeline Segment 4B reaches Taking-Over, four adjustments to the original $750,000
contract price have been agreed across this book: the utility-relocation dayworks variation (KA 7.1.5,
$10,850); VO-01's contract-price value (section 7.2.2, $47,500); VO-02 (section 7.2.2, $27,500); and CL-01, agreed in full
(section 7.2.5, $18,000). Across the same period, EOTs of 5 working days (VO-01, KA 5.4.4), 10 working days (CL-01,
section 7.2.3) and 5 working days (VO-02, agreed as part of the same instruction, following the same net-impact
method) have been granted, extending the Time for Completion by a total of 20 working days (4 weeks) beyond
the original 6-month date. Meridian achieves Taking-Over within this extended date.

*Required.* (a) The final contract price. (b) Whether liquidated damages apply.

*Working.*

(a)

| Component | Amount ($) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Original contract price | 750,000 | KA 7.1.2 |
| + Utility-relocation variation | 10,850 | KA 7.1.5 |
| + VO-01, contract-price value | 47,500 | KA 5.4.4, converted section 7.2.2 |
| + VO-02 | 27,500 | Section 7.2.2 |
| + CL-01, agreed | 18,000 | Section 7.2.3–section 7.2.5 |
| − Liquidated damages | 0 | (b), below |
| **Final contract price** | **853,850** | |

(b) Total agreed EOT = `5 + 10 + 5 = 20 working days (4 weeks)`. Meridian completed within the extended date
→ **no liquidated damages apply.**

*Answer.* **Final contract price = $853,850 (SAR 3,201,938, rounded)**; **liquidated damages = $0**, because
every day of Meridian's actual completion beyond the original 6-month date is covered by an agreed EOT.

*Interpretation.* Every line in this table cites a specific, already-worked figure elsewhere in this book —
nothing here is a fresh assertion. This is what a clean final account looks like in practice: not a
negotiation from scratch at the end of the contract, but a running total that simply collects decisions
already made, correctly, as the project went along. Retention (section 7.2.8) continues to apply against this final
figure exactly as KA 11.1.7 describes — the final account changes what is owed in total; it does not itself
release the security instruments still protecting that balance until Taking-Over and DNP end are separately
reached.

**Mini-case — a final account left open by an unresolved valuation.** On a comparable Meridian contract, a
single variation's valuation basis is never agreed — the Employer's Engineer insists an existing rate applies
(section 7.2.2's step 1); Meridian maintains the work was different enough in character to need a star rate (step
3) — and neither side revisits the disagreement until Taking-Over, by which point positions have hardened and
the amount in dispute, a modest $18,000 at the time of the work, has become entangled with three other, later
issues. The final account remains open for **eleven months** after Taking-Over, tying up retention release,
delaying the final certificate, and consuming far more commercial-management time than the original $18,000
disagreement ever justified. The lesson is the same one section 7.2.2 and section 7.2.7 already drew separately, now shown
at its most expensive: agreeing a valuation basis close to when the work happens, using the disciplined
process this KA describes, is materially cheaper than resolving the same disagreement later, entangled with
everything that followed it.

a remembered or re-estimated total rather than the specific, already-agreed figures this KA (and its sibling

discipline exists to prevent.

$18,000 disagreement for attention months before it became entangled with later issues. Such tools cannot
themselves agree a disputed valuation or determine a claim — that remains a negotiated or determined outcome
between the parties, exactly as section 7.2.2 and section 7.2.5–section 7.2.7 describe.

### Key terms

- **Contract administration (contract management)** — the discipline of monitoring performance against
  contractual obligations and actioning the contract's change, claim and dispute mechanisms during execution.
- **Variation (VO)** — an Employer-instructed change to the contracted scope, valued but not normally disputed
  as to whether it occurred.
- **Valuation hierarchy** — existing contract rate (similar work) → adjusted rate → fair/star rate built up
  from first principles, in descending order of preference.
- **Claim** — a contractor assertion of entitlement to time and/or money for an event it is not responsible
  for; contested until substantiated, and valued at cost, not at margin-inclusive rate.
- **Extension of time (EOT)** — a contractual extension of the Time for Completion for an excusable delay.
- **Excusable / compensable / non-compensable delay** — an event outside the contractor's own risk (excusable)
  that may or may not also entitle the contractor to money (compensable) as well as time.
- **Net critical delay** — a delaying event's gross duration, less any float available before it reaches the
  critical path.
- **Time-bar** — a contractual deadline for giving notice, after which an otherwise valid entitlement can be
  lost in whole or in part.
- **Prolongation** — the time-related cost of an EOT-entitled extension.
- **Disruption** — a loss of productivity or efficiency caused by an event, whether or not completion is
  delayed.
- **Acceleration (instructed / constructive)** — the cost of compressing remaining work to recover lost time,
  either directed by the Employer or undertaken by the contractor on its own initiative.
- **Measured mile method** — comparing productivity in an unimpacted reference period against an impacted
  period to quantify a disruption cost.
- **Global (total-cost) claim** — a claim based on the undifferentiated gap between total actual and budgeted
  cost, without isolating individual causes; widely regarded as weak evidentially.
- **Delay analysis methods** — impacted as-planned, time impact analysis (TIA), windows/contemporaneous
  period analysis, as-planned vs as-built (collapsed as-built) — each suited to different data availability
  and timing.
- **Concurrency** — a compensable and a non-compensable delay running over the same period; resolved by
  dominant-cause or apportionment approaches, both used in practice.
- **Dispute resolution ladder** — negotiation → mediation/dispute board (adjudication) → arbitration/
  litigation, increasing in cost, time and formality.
- **Retention** — a percentage of each certified amount withheld and released later, typically split between
  Taking-Over and the end of the Defects Notification Period.
- **Performance bond** — a third-party (surety) instrument letting the Employer draw funds directly if the
  contractor defaults.
- **Advance payment guarantee** — security for an up-front cash advance, recovered as the advance is offset
  against certified value.
- **Taking-Over (Practical Completion)** — the point the works are fit for their intended purpose, opening the
  Defects Notification Period.
- **Final account** — the reconciled total of the original contract price plus every agreed variation and
  claim, less any deduction, closing the contract's financial exposure.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 7.2.1 [topic 7.2.2, level: application — numerical]**
A variation's assessed internal cost impact is $60,000. The contractor's established target margin on the
contract is 20.0% of contract price. What is the variation's contract-price value?
A. $72,000
B. $48,000
C. **$75,000** ✔
D. $60,000
*Rationale:* Contract-price value = Cost ÷ (1 − Margin %) = 60,000 ÷ 0.80 = 75,000 (section 7.2.2). A wrongly
multiplies by 1.20 (a margin-on-cost, not margin-on-price, calculation); B wrongly multiplies by 0.80; D
omits any margin at all.

**MCQ 7.2.2 [topic 7.2.3, level: application — numerical]**
A delaying event lasts 15 working days. A Level-3 schedule review finds 4 working days of float available at
that point in the sequence before the delay reaches the critical path. What is the net critical delay?
A. 15 working days
B. 4 working days
C. **11 working days** ✔
D. 19 working days
*Rationale:* Net critical delay = Gross duration − Available float = 15 − 4 = 11 working days (section 7.2.3). A
ignores the float entirely; B states the float, not the net delay; D wrongly adds instead of subtracting.

**MCQ 7.2.3 [topic 7.2.3, level: analysis]**
A contractor becomes aware of a delaying event but does not give formal notice until well after the
contract's illustrative 28-day time-bar has passed, even though the underlying event was genuinely outside
its own risk. What is the most likely consequence?
A. The time-bar has no effect, because the underlying entitlement is genuine
B. **The entitlement to time and/or money can be lost or reduced, even though the underlying event was
   genuine** ✔
C. The contractor may still claim, provided the claim is valued correctly
D. The time-bar only applies to claims for money, never to extensions of time
*Rationale:* B is the governing point of the time-bar (section 7.2.3, illustrated by the R3 mini-case): a genuine
entitlement can still be lost through late notice. A and C both wrongly assume the underlying merit of the
claim overrides the notice requirement; D incorrectly narrows the time-bar's scope.

**MCQ 7.2.4 [topic 7.2.4, level: application — numerical]**
A contractor's prolongation claim is built from a general time-related overhead rate of $1,500 per working
day and a specialist standby rate of $400 per working day, both applying across a net critical delay of 8
working days. What is the total prolongation claim?
A. $12,000
B. $3,200
C. **$15,200** ✔
D. $1,900
*Rationale:* `(1,500 × 8) + (400 × 8) = 12,000 + 3,200 = 15,200` (section 7.2.4). A states only the general-overhead
component; B states only the specialist-standby component; D mistakenly adds the two daily rates and
multiplies by 1 day instead of 8.

**MCQ 7.2.5 [topic 7.2.4, level: recall]**
Why is a claim for prolongation typically valued at the contractor's actual cost, without an additional
profit margin, unlike a valuation for an instructed variation?
A. Because claims are always smaller in value than variations
B. **Because a claim compensates for a loss the contractor asserts it has suffered, not for new work the
   Employer has asked for and should pay for on normal commercial terms** ✔
C. Because the contract's priced rates never apply to claims
D. Because margin is only relevant on remeasurement contracts
*Rationale:* B is the governing distinction (section 7.2.4): variations are new, Employer-instructed work priced at
rates that include normal margin; claims compensate an incurred loss, valued at cost. A is not a general
rule; C and D misstate the reasoning.

**MCQ 7.2.6 [topic 7.2.8, level: application — numerical]**
A contract price is $600,000. The advance payment is 10.0% of contract price, recovered at 20.0% of each
certified amount. At what cumulative certified value is the advance fully recovered?
A. $120,000
B. **$300,000** ✔
C. $60,000
D. $600,000
*Rationale:* Advance = 10.0% × 600,000 = 60,000; Recovery point = 60,000 ÷ 20.0% = 300,000 (section 7.2.8). A states
the advance grown, not the recovery point; C states the advance itself; D wrongly assumes full contract value
is needed.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using section 7.2.2's method, explain why VO-01's contract-price value ($47,500) is higher than its internally
   assessed cost impact ($38,000, KA 5.4.4), and show the calculation that connects the two figures.
2. A delaying event lasts 9 working days, with 3 working days of float available before it reaches the
   critical path. The contractor became aware of the event on Day 5 and gave formal notice on Day 30, against
   an illustrative 28-day time-bar measured from awareness. State (a) the net critical delay, and (b) whether
   the notice is valid, and why.
3. Explain, using CL-01 as the example, why the general time-related overhead component of a prolongation
   claim typically needs less independent evidence than an event-specific component such as specialist
   standby, even though both are part of the same claim.
4. **Numerical.** A contract's original price is $500,000. Two agreed variations add $20,000 and $35,000
   respectively, and one agreed claim adds $12,000. No liquidated damages apply. Calculate the final contract
   price.

**Answers**

1. VO-01's $38,000 figure is Meridian's internal cost impact, correctly used to drive KA 5.4.6's reserve
   mechanics, but it does not itself include Meridian's normal margin. Because the contract's established
   target margin is 20.0% of contract price (equivalently, cost is 80.0% of price), the contract-price value
   is obtained by dividing the cost by 0.80: `38,000 ÷ 0.80 = 47,500`. The $9,500 difference is the margin the
   variation should recover, exactly as any other instructed work on this lump-sum contract would.
2. (a) `Net critical delay = 9 − 3 = 6 working days`. (b) `Notice date (Day 30) − Awareness date (Day 5) = 25
   working days ≤ 28-day illustrative bar` → the notice is **valid**, given with 25 days elapsed, inside the
   28-day window.
3. The general overhead component rests on a rate already established and applied without dispute elsewhere
   on the same contract (KA 5.4.4's $1,200/working-day rate), so it carries little residual uncertainty for an
   Engineer to test. The specialist-standby component is a new, event-specific assertion with no pre-existing,
   agreed rate behind it, so it needs independent, contemporaneous evidence — in CL-01's case, daily dockets
   co-signed by the Engineer's own site representative — before an Engineer can reasonably accept it as fact
   rather than assertion.
4. `Final contract price = 500,000 + 20,000 + 35,000 + 12,000 − 0 = 567,000`.
