# Domain 7 — Contracts, Commercial Management, BoQ, Invoicing & Revenue

## KA 7.4 — Invoicing & applications for payment

A construction or engineering contract is rarely paid in one lump sum on completion: it is paid **as the
work proceeds**, through a recurring cycle of measurement, claim, certification and payment that can run for
months or years before the contract is finished. Getting this cycle right is a core project-controls skill
in its own right, distinct from — but built directly on — two chapters either side of it: **KA 7.3** supplies
the Bill of Quantities structure, measured rates and pricing logic that give an interim claim its unit
values, and **KA 6.1** supplies the Earned Value discipline that measures physical progress internally; this
KA is where that measured, priced progress is turned into a certified demand for cash. **KA 11.1** already
walked the *process-control* view of this cycle — the O2C hand-offs, the credit check, the three-point
reconciliation — using a simplified version of one application; this KA does not repeat that control
narrative but instead builds the *full commercial mechanics* KA 11.1 explicitly deferred here: gross
valuation, materials on site, retention, advance-payment recovery, contra-charges and VAT, assembled into a
complete, reconciled payment certificate. **KA 7.5** then takes the same billing data and asks the IFRS 15
question — how much of it is *revenue*, and whether the contract asset/liability position is over- or
under-billed; this KA stops at "how much cash is due," and cross-refers rather than repeats that analysis.
The chapter continues the recurring illustrative entity **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC** and its **Pipeline
Segment 4B** contract (contract price $750,000, SAR 2,812,500; internal cost BAC $600,000; 10.0% retention;
30-day payment terms — KA 6.1, KA 11.1), picking up immediately after KA 11.1's Application 4 dispute was
resolved, at an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1** and an illustrative **VAT rate of 15.0%**, stated for
worked-example purposes only and never to be read as the current statutory rate of any specific jurisdiction.

### 7.4.1 The interim valuation / application-for-payment cycle: purpose, principle and process flow

**Definition & purpose.** An **interim valuation** (also called a **progress claim** or, once submitted, an
**application for payment**) is a periodic assessment of the value of work a contractor has executed under a
contract, up to a stated date, submitted as the basis for a payment on account of the final contract price
before the whole of the work is complete. The **interim valuation / application-for-payment cycle** is the
recurring sequence — measure, value, apply, certify, invoice, pay — that repeats at an agreed interval
(commonly monthly) from the contract's start until its final account (7.4.9).

**The underlying principle.** A construction contract can run for years; a contractor financing that work
entirely from its own cash until final completion would carry an unreasonable and largely uninsurable
funding burden, and an employer paying nothing until completion would have no periodic evidence that value is
actually being delivered for money already committed. The interim cycle exists to share that funding burden
sensibly: the contractor is paid, periodically, for value genuinely delivered (subject to retention as
security, 7.4.5), while the employer only pays for work that has been **measured and independently verified**
(7.4.2, 7.4.4) — never simply for time elapsed or cost incurred. This is the same reasoning behind
percentage-of-completion billing recognised throughout Domain 2/7 more broadly; this KA is concerned with the
mechanics of the *claim and certificate*, not the accounting recognition of the resulting revenue (KA 7.5).

**Formula.** `Cycle time (measurement to cash) = Payment date − Data date (measurement/valuation date)`,
made up of the sub-periods: valuation-to-application, application-to-certificate, certificate-to-payment
(the latter governed by the contract's payment terms and, in many jurisdictions, a statutory notice regime —
7.4.9).

**Example 7.4.1 — the cycle timeline for Meridian's Application 5.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's data date for the next valuation is Day 0. Under the contract, Meridian
must submit its application within 3 days of the data date; the certifier must issue a certificate within a
further 11 days; the paying party then has a further 7-day window in which it may issue a formal notice
proposing to pay less than certified (7.4.9); the contract's final date for payment is 21 days after the
certificate is issued.

*Required.* The data date, application date, certification date, and the latest date payment is due, if no
notice to pay less is issued.

*Working.*
`Application date = Day 0 + 3 = Day 3`
`Certification date = Day 3 + 11 = Day 14`
`Latest date for a notice proposing to pay less = Day 14 + 7 = Day 21`
`Final date for payment = Day 14 + 21 = Day 35`

*Answer.* **Data date Day 0; application Day 3; certificate Day 14; payment due by Day 35** — a 35-day cycle
from measurement to cash, assuming no notice to pay less is issued.

*Interpretation.* Every day in that 35-day cycle is contractually meaningful: the application window disciplines
the contractor to submit promptly on a fixed rhythm; the certification window disciplines the certifier; the
notice window is the paying party's only opportunity to challenge the certified figure before it becomes
payable in full (7.4.9); and the final date for payment is the hard deadline against which late payment,
interest and — ultimately — suspension rights are measured (KA 7.2).

**Mini-case — what happens when the certifier is late.** Suppose the certifier, overloaded with other
projects, issues Meridian's certificate on Day 20 instead of Day 14 — six days late, with no extension agreed.

notice window and final date for payment still run from the *actual* certification date, so payment is
correspondingly delayed by six days), or (b), under some standard forms, triggers a **default/deemed
certificate** mechanism where the amount *applied for* becomes payable in the certifier's silence, precisely
to stop a slow or unresponsive certifier from indefinitely delaying a contractor's cash flow. Which mechanism
applies is a contract-specific question (cross-reference KA 7.2) — but either way, a late certificate is a
real commercial event, not a harmless administrative slip, because it delays cash by exactly the same number
of days it was late.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating the interim cycle as a fixed, universal timetable — the number of days at each step (application
  window, certification period, notice window, final date for payment) is contract-specific and must be read
  from the actual contract, never assumed from a generic description like Example 7.4.1's illustrative figures.
- Losing track of which date actually started the clock on a downstream step (e.g., the *actual* certification
  date after a delay, not the originally scheduled one) — the mini-case above shows how this silently shifts
  every later deadline.
- Conflating the interim cycle with the final account cycle (7.4.9) — the interim cycle repeats every period
  until completion; the final account happens once, after completion, and is materially larger in scope.

**AI in this topic.** Workflow and contract-management tools can track every application's key dates
automatically — submission, certification deadline, notice deadline, final date for payment — and flag an
approaching or missed deadline before it becomes a dispute, which is genuinely valuable given how date-driven
this cycle is (cross-reference KA 13.4). Such a tool only knows the dates it has been configured with; it
cannot itself interpret ambiguous contract wording about which mechanism applies when a step is missed (the
mini-case above), and a wrongly configured deadline is worse than no reminder at all because it creates false
confidence. The commercial/contracts professional must verify the configured dates against the actual
executed contract — **AI proposes the reminder; the professional decides the contractual interpretation.**

**Cross-references.** KA 7.2 (contract lifecycle, obligations, default/deemed-certificate mechanisms in
depth); KA 7.3 (BoQ structure and pricing that feeds the valuation); KA 11.1.5 (the O2C control view of
certification and billing — not repeated here); 7.4.9 (the statutory notice regime in full).

### 7.4.2 Measuring work done for payment: the gross valuation of measured work

**Definition & purpose.** The **gross valuation of measured work** is the cumulative value, at contract
(BoQ) rates, of the permanent work the contractor has actually executed and had measured as of the data
date — the foundation line of every interim valuation, before materials on site, retention or any other
adjustment is applied.

**The underlying principle.** The value used here must come from the contract's own priced Bill of
Quantities (KA 7.3) — the agreed **selling** rates the employer contracted to pay — not from the contractor's
internal cost budget. KA 11.1.4 already flagged the closely related caution that EV (an *internal
performance* measure, built at budget/cost rates) is not automatically the same figure as billing entitlement
(a *contractual* measure, built at contract/selling rates); this topic is where that distinction becomes
concrete. Pipeline Segment 4B's BAC of $600,000 (KA 6.1) is Meridian's internal cost budget; the $750,000
contract price is what the client actually pays, at a roughly 20.0% margin (KA 11.1, Example 11.1.3) — so a
BoQ selling rate for, say, trenching will genuinely differ from the internal cost rate used to build EV, and
the two must never be silently substituted for one another.

**Formula.** `Gross valuation of measured work (cumulative) = Σ (measured quantity of each BoQ item ×
that item's contract rate)`, or, where progress is tracked as a single percentage of the whole (as in this
chapter's continuing illustration), `Cumulative measured-work value = Cumulative % physical progress ×
Contract price`. `Period (incremental) measured-work value = Cumulative measured-work value (this period) −
Cumulative measured-work value (prior period)`.

**Example 7.4.2 — Meridian's Application 5: the certified measured-work value.**

*Scenario.* Carrying forward from KA 11.1, Example 11.1.8: after Application 4's dispute was resolved,
cumulative certified value on Pipeline Segment 4B stood at **$500,250** gross (the original $510,000 cumulative
less the $9,750 gross reversed by credit note), with cumulative retention of $50,025 and cumulative net
certified value of $450,225 — the recap figures this chapter's Application 5 and 6 build on. By the data date
for Application 5, cumulative physical progress (independently measured, per 7.4.1's process) stands at
**75.0%** of the $750,000 contract price.

*Required.* (a) The certified cumulative measured-work value at Application 5. (b) This period's
(incremental) measured-work value.

*Working.*
(a) `Cumulative measured-work value = 75.0% × 750,000 = 562,500`
(b) `Period measured-work value = 562,500 − 500,250 = 62,250`

*Answer.* **Cumulative certified measured-work value $562,500; this period's measured-work value $62,250.**

*Interpretation.* $562,500 is the value of *permanent work only* — it says nothing yet about materials
delivered to site but not yet built in (7.4.3), and it is stated *before* any retention, previous-payment or
other deduction (7.4.5–7.4.8). Exactly how the certifier arrived at $562,500, rather than a different figure
Meridian itself might have claimed, is shown in Example 7.4.4.

**Mini-case — why "75.0% progress" needs a measurement basis, not an impression.** Suppose a second,
inexperienced measurer estimates Pipeline Segment 4B's progress at "roughly three-quarters done" without
re-measuring any BoQ item, and simply carries forward last period's measurement sheet with a stated round
number. Even if 75.0% happens to be correct this time, the practice is unsound: a BoQ-based valuation must be
traceable, item by item, back to an actual measured quantity (metres welded, cubic metres placed, units
installed) — a defensible record that can be checked, re-measured and, if necessary, defended in a dispute
(cross-reference 7.4.9). A single, ungrounded percentage is not a valuation; it is a guess that happens to be
expressed to one decimal place.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Over-certification** — including in the gross valuation work that has been started but not actually
  completed to the standard the BoQ item describes (Example 7.4.4 shows exactly this happening, and being
  corrected, in Meridian's own application).
- Reading internal EV (built at cost/budget rates for performance measurement, KA 6.1) straight across into a
  billing claim (built at contract/selling rates) without checking the BoQ's actual measurement rules —
  the two are related but not interchangeable (KA 11.1.4).
- Valuing a partially-complete BoQ item at its full rate rather than a fair proportion of it, or vice versa
  undervaluing a nearly-complete item — both distort the period's claimed value independently of any
  deliberate manipulation.

**AI in this topic.** Computer-vision analysis of site photographs, drone/LIDAR surveys, or as-built model
comparison can produce a rapid, independent estimate of measured quantities (e.g., estimated metres of pipe
welded from image analysis) that is a genuinely useful cross-check against a manual measurement sheet
(cross-reference KA 11.1.4, KA 13.5). Such estimates carry their own error margins, cannot verify the *quality*
of work behind a measured quantity (only that something appears present), and cannot substitute for the
independent certifier's sign-off (7.4.4) — an AI-generated quantity estimate is a cross-check, not a
certified measurement.

**Cross-references.** KA 7.3 (BoQ structure, measurement rules and pricing, in full depth — not repeated
here); KA 6.1 (EV measurement methods — the internal-performance analogue); KA 11.1.4 (the EV-vs-billing
caution, first raised there); 7.4.4 (certification of this figure).

### 7.4.3 Materials on site (and materials off site)

**Definition & purpose.** **Materials on site** are goods and materials delivered to, and secured on, the
project site, intended for permanent incorporation into the works, but not yet fixed/built in — and which
many standard contract forms allow the contractor to claim, at their **delivered/invoiced cost**, ahead of
being physically incorporated. **Materials off site** (sometimes called **vested materials**) are the
equivalent goods held in a supplier's or fabricator's premises, not yet delivered to site; because the
employer bears more risk over goods it cannot inspect or possess, contracts that permit off-site claims
typically require additional protection — a vesting certificate transferring title, evidence of insurance,
and sometimes a bond — before they may be certified at all.

**The underlying principle.** Materials-on-site provisions exist to smooth a contractor's cash flow on
long-lead or bulk-procured items (specialist valves, structural steel, cable) that must often be paid for, or
at least committed to, well before they are actually built into the works — without this provision, a
contractor could be forced to carry the full cost of major procurement for months with no corresponding
payment. The trade-off is risk: unfixed material can be lost, stolen, damaged or diverted to another project
before it is built in, which is exactly why the contract usually requires it to be *evidenced* (supplier
invoices), *protected* (secured, weatherproofed, insured) and *identifiable* (clearly allocated to this
contract) before it may be certified.

**Formula.** `Materials-on-site value = Σ (invoiced/delivered cost of unfixed materials and goods, delivered
to site, protected and evidenced, per the contract's materials clause)`. Materials are valued at
**delivered cost**, not at the (higher) BoQ rate that would apply once fixed, because that rate includes
labour and overhead not yet expended.

**Example 7.4.3 — Materials on site: Meridian's Application 5.**

*Scenario.* Ahead of the final tie-in works, Meridian has delivered and secured on the Pipeline Segment 4B
site a consignment of specialist valve assemblies, evidenced by supplier invoices totalling **$18,000**, and
stored under weatherproof cover with the delivery note cross-referenced to the relevant BoQ items, per the
contract's materials-on-site clause. None of the valves has yet been fixed into the pipeline.

*Required.* The amount to be included in Application 5's gross valuation for materials on site, and the
resulting gross valuation to date (combining with Example 7.4.2's measured-work figure).

*Working.*
`Materials-on-site value = 18,000` (delivered/invoiced cost, fully evidenced and protected)
`Gross valuation to date = Cumulative measured-work value + Materials on site = 562,500 + 18,000 = 580,500`

*Answer.* **Materials on site $18,000; gross valuation to date $580,500.**

*Interpretation.* The $18,000 is not yet "physical progress" in the sense KA 6.1's EV methods measure it —
no labour has been expended fixing the valves — but it is genuine, evidenced value the employer has agreed
to fund early, precisely because a bulk-procured, long-lead item like this would otherwise sit entirely on
Meridian's own balance sheet, unfunded, for weeks.

**Mini-case — the double-count risk when materials are later fixed.** In the period between Application 5
and Application 6, the valve assemblies are fixed into the pipeline. Once fixed, their value is now properly
part of the *measured-work* line (7.4.2) — the cumulative measured-work percentage at Application 6 reflects
work that includes those now-installed valves. If the $18,000 were **also** left in the materials-on-site
line at Application 6, its value would be counted twice: once as installed measured work, once as unfixed
material still supposedly awaiting installation. The correct treatment (applied in Example 7.4.8) is to drop
a material from the materials-on-site line the moment it is fixed into the permanent works, exactly offsetting
its appearance in the now-higher measured-work percentage.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Double-counting** — leaving a materials-on-site line in the valuation after the material has been fixed
  and is already reflected in the measured-work percentage (the mini-case above).
- Certifying materials that are not adequately evidenced (no invoice, no delivery note) or not properly
  secured/protected on site — a common source of later dispute if the material is subsequently lost, damaged
  or found not to exist in the quantity claimed.
- Valuing on-site materials at the BoQ's *fixed* rate rather than delivered cost, effectively pre-paying for
  labour and overhead not yet incurred.
- Certifying off-site (vested) materials without the vesting certificate, insurance evidence or bond the
  contract requires — exposing the employer to the very risk those protections exist to manage.

**AI in this topic.** OCR/data-extraction and RPA tools can read supplier invoices and delivery notes and
match them automatically to a materials-on-site register, flagging items claimed without a matching invoice
or those still outstanding after an unusually long period on site (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — a
real time saver on a project with many procurement lines. Such tools verify *paperwork*, not physical
reality: they cannot confirm the material is actually still on site, undamaged and correctly identified —
that remains a physical inspection the certifier (7.4.4) must still perform or rely on a verified site report
for.

**Cross-references.** KA 7.2 (bonds and guarantees, including any materials/vesting bond); KA 7.3 (BoQ rates
that will apply once materials are fixed); 7.4.2 (the measured-work line materials convert into); 7.4.8
(full worked integration).

### 7.4.4 The payment certificate and the certification process

**Definition & purpose.** **Certification** is the independent verification, by a person or role empowered
under the contract, of the value the contractor has claimed in an application, resulting in a **payment
certificate** — the document stating the amount the certifier considers properly due for that period. Under
FIDIC-style standard forms of contract, widely used in international infrastructure and EPC work, this role
is commonly given to a named certifier — often titled the **Engineer** or, on some forms, the **Employer's
Representative** — who reviews the contractor's application against the measured record and the contract's
payment terms and issues an **Interim Payment Certificate (IPC)**. This is described here at awareness level
only, in general terms; specific clause wording and numbering vary materially between FIDIC editions (and
between FIDIC and other standard forms) and must always be read from the actual contract in force, never
assumed from a generic description (cross-reference KA 7.2 for the fuller contract-administration treatment).

**The underlying principle.** No amount should become payable purely on the strength of the contractor's own
assessment of its own work — the same three-point reconciliation logic introduced in KA 11.1.5 (contract,
independently verified delivery, invoice) applies here at the point the *claim itself* is turned into a
*certificate*. The certifier's role is to check the application against the actual measured record and the
contract's rates and rules, and to certify what is properly due — which may be less than, equal to, or (rarely)
more than what the contractor applied for.

**Formula/control rule.** `Certified amount = min(Contractor's application, Certifier's independently
verified assessment)` in respect of any individual line where the two differ, unless the parties otherwise
agree; the certificate as a whole need not equal the application in total.

**Example 7.4.4 — how the certifier reached Application 5's $562,500.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's own Application 5 claimed a cumulative measured-work value of **$567,000** (a
self-assessment). On review, the certifier's site verification found that a section of pipe coating included
in Meridian's claim, valued at **$4,500**, had not in fact been completed to the standard the relevant BoQ
item describes — the coating had been applied but not yet cured and inspected.

*Required.* The certified cumulative measured-work value.

*Working.* `Certified value = 567,000 − 4,500 = 562,500`

*Answer.* **Certified cumulative measured-work value $562,500** — matching Example 7.4.2's figure exactly,
which is used throughout the rest of this chapter's Application 5 build-up.

*Interpretation.* The $4,500 reduction is not a rejection of Meridian's whole application, nor a punitive
act — it is the certification process working exactly as intended: one specific, verifiable item was found
not yet to meet its measured criterion, and only that item's value was adjusted. Meridian can re-include the
$4,500 once the coating is properly cured and inspected, typically in the very next application.

**Mini-case — certifier independence.** Suppose the certifier on a different contract is a direct employee of
the contractor's own commercial department, rather than an independent role appointed under the contract.
Even if that individual is diligent and honest, the *structure* is compromised: the same three-point-
reconciliation logic that makes certification meaningful (11.1.5, 11.1.9) depends on the certifier being
independent of the party whose claim is being checked. This is why standard forms typically name a specific,
contractually defined certifying role (often, though not exclusively, engaged or appointed by the employer,
subject to its own duty to certify fairly) rather than leaving certification to whichever internal function
happens to be convenient.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Over-certification** — a certifier who rubber-stamps an application without independent verification
  (time pressure, an easy relationship with the contractor, or simple overload) removes the one control this
  whole cycle depends on; Example 7.4.4 shows the alternative — a specific, evidenced, defensible adjustment.
- Confusing "certified" with "paid" — a certificate states what is *due*; the actual payment follows the
  cycle's remaining steps (7.4.1) and can itself be reduced by a later, validly issued notice (7.4.9).
- A certifier who is not, in substance, independent of the party being assessed (the mini-case above).

**AI in this topic.** GenAI and anomaly-detection tools can flag an application whose claimed values or
percentages jump unusually compared with prior periods, or that are inconsistent with a photographic/drone
progress record — a useful triage step that helps a certifier prioritise where to look closely (cross-reference
KA 11.1.5, KA 13.5). It cannot itself determine whether a specific BoQ item genuinely meets its measured
criterion — that judgement requires site verification against the contract's own definitions, exactly as in
Example 7.4.4 — and an unreviewed AI-drafted certificate is not a certified one.

**Cross-references.** KA 7.2 (the certifier's role and duties under the contract, in fuller depth); KA 11.1.5,
KA 11.1.9 (the O2C three-point reconciliation and segregation-of-duties framing of certification); 7.4.9
(what happens if certification, or a subsequent notice, is late or absent).

### 7.4.5 Retention: deduction mechanics and release

**Definition & purpose.** **Retention** is a percentage of each certified amount withheld by the employer as
security against defective or incomplete work, released back to the contractor in stages once that security
is no longer needed — most commonly half at **Practical Completion (PC)** and the remaining half at the end
of a stated **Defects Notification Period (DNP)**, once any notified defects have been made good.

**The underlying principle.** Retention exists to give the employer a straightforward, self-executing
remedy — withheld cash — if the contractor fails to remedy defects, without the employer having to pursue a
separate claim for the whole value of the work. Because it is deducted from *every* certified amount as the
contract proceeds, and only ever released in full at two specific, later trigger events, retention is
precisely the O2C balance KA 11.1.7 already flagged as the one most often lost track of: it sits outside the
normal invoice-to-cash rhythm and can be forgotten by neglect rather than decided by choice.

**Formula.** `Retention withheld (period) = Retention rate × Gross valuation (period, or cumulative, per the
contract's basis)`; `Cumulative retention held = Retention rate × Cumulative gross valuation to date`;
`Tranche released = (Cumulative retention held ÷ 2) − Any valid deduction against that tranche (e.g.
unremedied defects, cross-reference 7.4.7)`.

**Example 7.4.5a — retention deduction, recap.** Applications 1–4 on Pipeline Segment 4B already withheld a
cumulative $50,025 of retention against $500,250 of gross certified value (10.0% throughout — KA 11.1,
Example 11.1.5, Example 11.1.8), which is not re-derived here; Application 5's incremental retention
($8,025, taking cumulative retention to $58,050) is worked in full in 7.4.8. This topic's remaining content
covers what happens to that withheld retention once the contract nears and reaches completion.

**Example 7.4.5b — retention release with a defects deduction (illustrative, at completion).**

*Scenario.* At an illustrative point of full completion, total retention withheld across Pipeline Segment 4B's
original $750,000 contract price would reach `10.0% × 750,000 = $75,000` (SAR 281,250), to be released in two
equal tranches of $37,500 each — the first at Practical Completion, the second at the end of the Defects
Notification Period. At DNP end, a minor defect (a section of protective coating that has since deteriorated)
remains unremedied by Meridian despite notice; the employer engages another contractor to make it good at a
cost of **$2,500**, which the contract entitles it to deduct from the second retention tranche.

*Required.* (a) The amount released at Practical Completion. (b) The amount released at the end of the DNP.
(c) A check that total retention is fully accounted for.

*Working.*
(a) `First tranche = 75,000 / 2 = 37,500` (no deduction — released in full at PC)
(b) `Second tranche = 37,500 − 2,500 = 35,000`
(c) `Total released = 37,500 + 35,000 = 72,500`; `Retained permanently (defect rectification) = 2,500`;
    `72,500 + 2,500 = 75,000` ✓ — matches total retention withheld exactly.

*Answer.* **First tranche $37,500; second tranche $35,000 (net of the $2,500 defect deduction); total
retention fully accounted for.**

*Interpretation.* The $2,500 deduction follows the same contra-charge/set-off principle developed fully in
7.4.7, applied here against retention rather than against an interim certificate — a legitimate, evidenced
deduction, not a dispute, provided proper notice of the defect and the intended deduction was given.

**Common pitfalls (retention errors).**
- Applying the retention rate to the *cumulative* value when only the *period* value should be retained (or
  vice versa), which either double-withholds or under-withholds retention relative to what has already been
  taken on prior periods (cross-reference KA 11.1.5).
- Losing track of the release trigger dates (PC, DNP end) so retention balances age indefinitely, quietly
  written off by neglect rather than released by a considered decision (KA 11.1.7) — the single most common
  retention error in practice.
- Deducting a defects cost from a retention tranche without proper notice or an evidenced cost, converting a
  legitimate security mechanism into an unjustified withholding — the same due-process point developed in
  7.4.7 for contra-charges generally.
- Treating a *retention bond* (a bank/insurance guarantee substituted for cash retention, common on larger
  contracts, allowing the contractor to receive the cash retention would otherwise withhold in exchange for a
  bond the employer can call on) as equivalent to cash retention without checking the bond's own conditions
  and expiry — a bond that has lapsed or is capped below the retention value it was meant to replace leaves
  the employer with less security than it believes it has.

**AI in this topic.** Portfolio-level workflow tools can track retention balances and release-trigger dates
across many contracts simultaneously, flagging an approaching PC or DNP-end date, or a retention balance that
has aged well past its expected release point with no action taken (cross-reference KA 11.1.7, KA 13.4). This
directly addresses the "forgotten retention" pitfall at scale. It cannot itself judge whether a defect is
validly outstanding or whether a proposed deduction is properly evidenced — that remains a professional,
contract-specific judgement, informed by the site record, not by the tracking tool's date field.

**Cross-references.** KA 7.2 (retention terms, bonds and guarantees within the wider contract-administration
treatment); KA 11.1.7 (retention as an O2C receivable, and the risk of it being forgotten); 7.4.7

recalculated on the final, variated contract value in the final account).

### 7.4.6 Advance payment and its recovery

**Definition & purpose.** An **advance payment** (sometimes called a **mobilisation payment** where it funds
contract start-up specifically) is a sum paid to the contractor ahead of, or independently of, normal
measured progress, typically to fund a specific need — mobilisation, a major procurement commitment, or
similar — and is subsequently **recovered** by deduction from the amounts otherwise due on later
applications, until the advance is fully repaid.

**The underlying principle.** An advance payment is not free money and not extra contract value — it is a
short-term, interest-free (or, in some contracts, interest-bearing) loan from the employer to the contractor,
secured (commonly) by an advance-payment bond, and it must be **fully and only** recovered — no more, no
less — before the contract's final account is settled. Two common recovery mechanisms exist: **fixed-instalment
recovery** (an agreed amount deducted each period until repaid) and **proportional (pro-rata) recovery** (a
fixed percentage of each period's gross valuation deducted until repaid) — which method applies is a matter
of the specific contract term, not a universal rule.

**Formula.** Fixed-instalment method: `Advance recovery (period) = Agreed fixed instalment, until Σ
recoveries = Original advance`. Proportional method: `Advance recovery (period) = Recovery rate × Gross
value of work certified this period, until Σ recoveries = Original advance`. Either way: `Advance
outstanding = Original advance − Cumulative recovered to date`.

**Example 7.4.6 — Meridian's advance payment and its first recovery instalment.**

*Scenario.* Ahead of Application 5, the client and Meridian agree a supplementary advance of **$30,000**
(SAR 112,500; 4.0% of the $750,000 contract price) to fund procurement of the specialist valve assemblies for
the pipeline's final tie-in works (the same valves valued as materials on site in Example 7.4.3), paid outside
the normal certification cycle and recoverable in two equal, fixed instalments deducted from the net amount
otherwise due on Applications 5 and 6.

*Required.* (a) The recovery instalment on Application 5. (b) The advance still outstanding after that
instalment.

*Working.*
(a) `Instalment = 30,000 / 2 = 15,000`
(b) `Outstanding = 30,000 − 15,000 = 15,000`

*Answer.* **Application 5 recovery $15,000; advance still outstanding after Application 5 = $15,000** — to be
recovered in full by a further $15,000 instalment on Application 6 (completed in Example 7.4.8).

*Interpretation.* This deduction reduces the *cash Meridian actually receives* this period; it does **not**
reduce the certified *value* of work done, retention withheld, or any other line — advance recovery is a
repayment of a loan already received, entirely separate from the value the work itself has earned (a
distinction developed further in 7.4.8).

**Mini-case — the double-recovery risk.** Suppose a junior commercial assistant, unaware the advance was
already fully recovered by Application 6 (Example 7.4.8), continues deducting a further $15,000 "advance
recovery" instalment from Application 7. This is **double recovery** — the employer would recoup $45,000
against an advance of only $30,000, effectively short-paying Meridian $15,000 it is genuinely owed. The only
reliable defence against this is a maintained recovery schedule, checked at every application, that shows the
cumulative amount recovered against the original advance — not a mental note that "we're still recovering the
advance" carried forward indefinitely.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Double recovery of advance** — continuing to deduct advance-recovery instalments after the advance is
  already fully repaid (the mini-case above), or applying both a fixed-instalment schedule *and* a
  proportional deduction simultaneously by error.
- The opposite failure — under-recovery — where instalments are missed or reduced without adjusting the
  remaining schedule, so the advance is never fully recovered by completion and has to be chased separately
  in the final account (7.4.9), a materially worse position for the employer than catching it at the next
  application.
- Confusing advance-payment recovery with retention — both reduce the cash paid on an application, but
  retention is security released later against the *same* work; advance recovery is repayment of cash already
  *advanced* for different work — conflating the two in a report misstates both balances.
- Failing to confirm the advance-payment bond (where required) remains valid and sized to the *outstanding*
  advance as it reduces — a bond left at its original face value after most of the advance is recovered
  over-secures the employer at unnecessary cost to the contractor, or, if allowed to lapse early, under-secures
  the employer while a real balance is still outstanding.

**AI in this topic.** A workflow tool can maintain the running advance-recovery schedule automatically —
cumulative recovered, balance outstanding, and a hard stop once the original advance is fully repaid —
directly addressing the double-recovery pitfall by making the "already fully recovered" state visible at every
application rather than relying on memory (cross-reference KA 13.4). The tool is only as reliable as its
configured recovery method and rate; if either is entered incorrectly, it will confidently produce a wrong
schedule. The professional must verify the schedule against the actual advance-payment agreement before
relying on any automated recovery figure.

**Cross-references.** KA 7.2 (advance-payment bonds and guarantees); 7.4.3 (the specific procurement the
advance in this example funds); 7.4.8 (the advance's second, final recovery instalment, fully reconciled).

### 7.4.7 Contra-charges, set-off and cross-claims against a certified sum

**Definition & purpose.** A **contra-charge** (or **back-charge**) is a cost the paying party is entitled to
deduct from an amount otherwise due to the contractor, for a cross-claim against that contractor — commonly
the cost of remedying a default, making good damage the contractor caused, or having work executed by a third
party at the contractor's risk and cost. **Set-off** is the broader contractual/legal right to reduce a
payment by the value of a cross-claim rather than paying in full and pursuing that claim separately.

**The underlying principle.** A contra-charge is not, in itself, a control failure or evidence of a poor
relationship — legitimate cross-claims arise routinely on any live contract. What matters is **due process**:
most contracts require the paying party to give the contractor notice of the intended deduction, its basis
and its amount, within a stated period, before it can validly reduce a certified sum. A deduction made without
that notice is vulnerable to challenge regardless of whether the underlying claim itself is genuine —
procedurally invalid, even where substantively justified.

**Formula.** `Net amount due (period) = Net additional value certified this period − Advance recovery (7.4.6)
− Contra-charge/set-off (this period, if validly notified)`.

**Example 7.4.7 — a contra-charge on Meridian's Application 5.**

*Scenario.* During the period covered by Application 5, Meridian's subcontractor left an excavation on the
Pipeline Segment 4B site unprotected overnight, a breach of the contract's site-safety obligations. The
employer engaged a third party to install emergency temporary fencing, at a cost of **$3,225**, and gave
Meridian written notice of the cost and its intention to deduct it from the next certified amount, as the
contract's set-off clause requires.

*Required.* The isolated effect of this contra-charge on the net amount otherwise due at Application 5.

*Working.* `Contra-charge deduction = 3,225` (deducted after retention, previous payments and advance
recovery are applied — full sequence in Example 7.4.8)

*Answer.* **A $3,225 deduction from the net amount otherwise due at Application 5**, integrated into the full
build-up in 7.4.8.

*Interpretation.* Because the employer gave written notice of the cost and its basis before deducting it, the
contra-charge is procedurally valid; had it simply appeared as a silent reduction on the certificate with no
prior notice, Meridian would have a legitimate procedural objection regardless of whether $3,225 was a fair
cost for the fencing.

**Mini-case — an improperly notified deduction.** Suppose, on a different application, the employer deducts
$5,000 for a separate quality defect without any prior written notice, discovering the issue only while
preparing the certificate itself. Even if the $5,000 defect is entirely genuine and the cost entirely
reasonable, deducting it without the notice the contract requires is a process breach in its own right — the
contractor is entitled to have the deduction reversed (and re-notified, then reapplied at the next
opportunity) purely on procedural grounds, independently of the underlying claim's merit. This is precisely
why 7.4.9's statutory notice regime places such emphasis on formal, timely notices rather than on the
substantive fairness of a deduction alone.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Deducting a contra-charge without the notice the contract requires (the mini-case above) — a process
  failure distinct from, and just as damaging as, deducting an unjustified amount.
- Treating every disputed measurement as a contra-charge (or vice versa) — a measurement dispute (KA 11.1.8)
  concerns *how much work was done*; a contra-charge concerns a *separate cross-claim* against the
  contractor, and the two require different evidence and different processes.
- Leaving a contra-charge undocumented in the certificate build-up, so a later reviewer cannot see why the net
  amount due differs from the gross valuation less retention alone.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI tools can scan site correspondence, incident reports and instructions to identify
and log potential contra-charge events and their supporting evidence automatically, and can track whether the
required notice was actually issued and within the contractual deadline (cross-reference KA 11.1.8, KA 13.5).
Automated extraction from unstructured correspondence can miss context or misjudge whether an event genuinely
supports a valid cross-claim; the commercial team must verify the extracted record, confirm proper notice was
given, and remains accountable for the deduction actually applied.

**Cross-references.** KA 7.2 (claims, cross-claims and default remedies under the contract, in depth); KA
11.1.8 (disputes, short-pays and credit notes — the O2C treatment of a *measurement* disagreement, distinct
from a contra-charge); 7.4.5 (the same due-process logic applied to a retention deduction); 7.4.9 (the
statutory notice regime this due-process requirement sits within).

### 7.4.8 VAT/tax on certified payments, and the full certificate build-up: a worked two-period mini-case

**Definition & purpose.** This topic brings every element of 7.4.2–7.4.7 together into the complete,
standard build-up of an interim payment certificate — gross work done, plus materials on site, less
retention, less previously certified value, plus/less period adjustments (advance recovery, contra-charges),
equals the net amount due, to which **VAT** (value-added tax, or the equivalent consumption tax in the
applicable jurisdiction) is then added at the applicable rate to arrive at the total amount payable.

**The underlying principle.** VAT is charged by the contractor as **output tax** on the value of the taxable
supply of construction services, on top of the net amount otherwise due; the point at which VAT becomes due
(the **tax point**) and the *base* it is charged on can differ by jurisdiction and by the specific VAT scheme
in use. This chapter's worked model charges VAT only on the **net amount actually invoiced each period** —
i.e., after retention, advance recovery and any contra-charge have already been deducted — so that retention,
when it is later released (7.4.5, 7.4.9), itself attracts VAT at that later point, when it is actually paid.
Some VAT regimes instead require output tax on the **full gross certified value** (including the retained
portion) at the point of the *original* certificate, in which case no further VAT is due when retention is
later released, because it was already accounted for — creating a real cash-flow cost, since the contractor
must account for VAT on money it has not yet collected. Both approaches, correctly and consistently applied,
should reconcile to the same total VAT over the life of the contract (demonstrated in Example 7.4.9b); which
basis actually applies is a question of local VAT law and the applicable invoicing scheme, never assumed.

**Formula.** `Gross valuation to date = Cumulative measured-work value (7.4.2) + Materials on site (7.4.3)`.
`Net valuation to date = Gross valuation to date × (1 − Retention rate)`. `Net additional value certified
this period = Net valuation to date − Net valuation previously certified`. `Net amount due this period
(before VAT) = Net additional value certified this period − Advance recovery (7.4.6) − Contra-charge (7.4.7)`.
`VAT payable = VAT rate × Net amount due this period (before VAT)`. `Total certificate amount payable = Net
amount due this period (before VAT) + VAT payable`.

**Example 7.4.8 — the full payment certificate build-up: Meridian's Applications 5 and 6.**

*Scenario.* Bringing together Examples 7.4.2, 7.4.3, 7.4.6 and 7.4.7 for **Application 5** (cumulative
measured progress 75.0%), and adding **Application 6** (cumulative measured progress 87.0%), on Pipeline
Segment 4B (contract price $750,000; retention 10.0%; illustrative VAT 15.0%). Cumulative net valuation
previously certified through Application 4 was $450,225 (KA 11.1, Example 11.1.8).

*Required.* The complete certificate build-up for Applications 5 and 6, and a reconciliation confirming the
figures are internally consistent across both periods.

*Working — Application 5.*

### 7.4.9 The statutory payment and notice regime, and the final account

**Definition & purpose.** Many jurisdictions overlay construction payment cycles with **statutory payment
and notice legislation** — in the United Kingdom, the framework established by the Housing Grants,
Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 (as later amended) is a well-known example — designed to protect
regular cash flow through a construction supply chain by mandating, at a minimum, a right to payment by
instalments, a clear mechanism for determining the sum due, defined notice procedures, and a right to
adjudicate disputes quickly. Broadly comparable "security of payment" style legislation exists in a number of
other jurisdictions (described here generically, not by any specific act or clause). At **contract
completion**, the interim cycle gives way to the **final account** — the single, comprehensive statement of
the total final value of the whole contract, incorporating all agreed variations, resolving all outstanding
claims, and reconciling every prior payment and retention movement to a final balance due.

**The underlying principle.** The core mechanism common to most statutory payment regimes of this kind is
that **silence favours the payee**: if the paying party fails to issue a valid payment notice (stating the
sum it considers due) or, later, a notice proposing to pay less than the amount otherwise notified, within
the deadlines the legislation and the contract set, the sum applied for (or last notified) generally becomes
the sum properly due and payable — regardless of whether it is, in fact, the "correct" value. This is a
deliberate policy choice: it places the burden of engaging with the payment process, promptly and formally,
onto the paying party, rather than allowing payment to be delayed indefinitely by inaction. This description
is given at awareness level only; the specific notice periods, forms of wording and consequences vary
materially by jurisdiction and by contract, and must always be confirmed against the applicable law and the
executed contract — never assumed from this general description (cross-reference KA 7.2).

**Formula/control rule.** `If (Paying party issues no valid payment notice by its deadline) → Sum applied for
becomes the notified sum`. `If (Notified sum stands and no valid notice to pay less is issued by its
deadline) → Notified sum becomes payable in full by the final date for payment`. `Final account: Final
contract value = Original contract price + Σ (approved variations, net)`; `Amount due on final certificate =
(Final contract value × (1 − Retention rate)) − Cumulative net payments previously received`.

**Example 7.4.9a — a missed pay-less-notice deadline (illustrative).**

*Scenario.* On a certificate for $50,000 net (before VAT), the paying party disagrees with $8,000 of the
certified value but fails to issue a formal notice proposing to pay less by the deadline the contract and the
applicable statutory regime require.

*Required.* The amount that becomes payable, and why.

*Working.* No valid notice was issued within the deadline, so the certified sum stands as notified in full.
`Amount payable = 50,000` (plus VAT, per 7.4.8)

*Answer.* **The full $50,000 becomes payable — the disagreement over $8,000 does not, by itself, reduce what
is due**, because it was never converted into a valid, timely notice.

*Interpretation.* The paying party's disagreement may still be genuine and may still be pursued — through
negotiation, the next application's adjustment, or adjudication — but it cannot retroactively reduce *this*
certificate once the notice deadline has passed unused. This is precisely why 7.4.1's timeline and 7.4.7's
due-process discipline matter as much as the underlying commercial merits of any dispute.

**Example 7.4.9b — Pipeline Segment 4B's final account.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B reaches Practical Completion. During the contract, a variation for additional
valve chambers, valued net at **$22,000**, was approved through change control (cross-reference KA 5.4, KA
7.2). Cumulative net payments already received prior to the final certificate total **$625,320** (a
summary-level figure for this illustration, not traced through every intervening application). Retention
remains 10.0%; VAT remains an illustrative 15.0%. At Practical Completion, no further defects are outstanding
(unlike the illustration in Example 7.4.5b), so both retention tranches release in full.

*Required.* (a) The final contract value. (b) The amount due on the final certificate (before retention
release). (c) The total amount due at Practical Completion, including the first retention tranche. (d) The
second retention tranche at the end of the DNP. (e) A reconciliation of total net value invoiced, and total
VAT charged, across the whole contract.

*Working.*
(a) `Final contract value = 750,000 + 22,000 = 772,000`
(b) `Total retention (recalculated on the final value) = 10.0% × 772,000 = 77,200` → two tranches of
    `77,200 / 2 = 38,600` each — **superseding** the $75,000 estimate used illustratively in Example 7.4.5b,
    because retention must always be recalculated against the *current, final* contract value once variations
    are incorporated, not frozen at the original contract price.
    `Net cumulative certified value = 772,000 − 77,200 = 694,800`
    `Net amount due on final certificate (remaining measured work) = 694,800 − 625,320 = 69,480`
    `VAT on final certificate = 15.0% × 69,480 = 10,422`
    `Final certificate total (incl. VAT) = 69,480 + 10,422 = 79,902`
(c) `Retention tranche released at PC (net) = 38,600`; `VAT on that release = 15.0% × 38,600 = 5,790`;
    `Tranche released, incl. VAT = 38,600 + 5,790 = 44,390`
    `Total paid at Practical Completion = 79,902 + 44,390 = 124,292`
(d) `Retention tranche released at DNP end (net) = 38,600`; `VAT = 5,790`; `Incl. VAT = 44,390`
(e) `Total net value invoiced over the whole contract = 625,320 + 69,480 + 38,600 + 38,600 = 772,000` —
    matches the final contract value exactly ✓
    `Total VAT over the whole contract = 15.0% × 772,000 = 115,800`; checked against the four components —
    VAT already charged on the $625,320 of prior net payments (`15.0% × 625,320 = 93,798`) + VAT on the final
    certificate (10,422) + VAT on each retention tranche (5,790 + 5,790) = `93,798 + 10,422 + 5,790 + 5,790 =
    115,800` ✓ — matches exactly.

*Answer.* **Final contract value $772,000 (SAR 2,895,000). Total retention (recalculated) $77,200 (SAR
289,500), two tranches of $38,600 each. Total paid at Practical Completion $124,292 (SAR 466,095), including
VAT. Second retention tranche at DNP end $44,390 including VAT. Total net value invoiced over the whole
contract reconciles exactly to the final contract value ($772,000); total VAT reconciles exactly to 15.0% of
that value ($115,800).**

*Interpretation.* The final account is where every thread of this chapter closes out simultaneously:
variations are folded into the contract value, retention is recalculated on that final value (not the
original estimate), every prior net payment is accounted for, and — as the reconciliation in (e) shows — the
whole contract's net billing and VAT tie out exactly once every retention tranche has been released. A final
account that does not reconcile this cleanly is a signal that a payment, a variation, or a VAT treatment has
been missed or mis-recorded somewhere in the cycle, and must be traced back through the interim applications
before the contract can genuinely be closed (cross-reference KA 8.5).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assuming statutory notice deadlines and consequences are identical across jurisdictions or contract forms —
  Example 7.4.9a's mechanism is a common pattern, not a universal rule; the applicable law and contract must
  always be checked.
- Recalculating the final account against the *original* contract price rather than the final, variated
  value — understating both the final payment due and the retention that should have been withheld and is
  now due for release (Example 7.4.9b).
- Treating the final account as a simple "add up everything" exercise rather than a full reconciliation —
  the check in Example 7.4.9b(e) is the discipline that actually confirms nothing has been missed.
- Closing the final account before the Defects Notification Period has genuinely ended, releasing the second
  retention tranche prematurely and losing the security it was meant to provide.

**AI in this topic.** NLP-based contract-monitoring tools can scan correspondence and the payment record for
statutory notice compliance — flagging an approaching payment-notice or pay-less-notice deadline before it is
missed — which is a genuine, high-value use given how strictly time-bound this regime is (cross-reference KA
13.4, KA 13.5). Such tools do not give legal advice, cannot determine which statutory regime actually applies
to a given contract and jurisdiction, and cannot themselves judge whether a notice, once drafted, is legally
valid in form and content; a missed or defective notice has real financial consequences (Example 7.4.9a), and
the commercial/legal team must verify both the deadline and the notice's validity — **AI proposes the
reminder and a first-draft flag; the professional decides the legal and commercial judgement.**

**Cross-references.** KA 7.2 (contract administration, claims and the certifier's/Engineer's duties, in

### Key terms

- **Interim valuation / application for payment** — a periodic claim for the value of work executed to date,
  submitted as a payment on account of the final contract price before completion.
- **Gross valuation of measured work** — the cumulative value, at contract (BoQ) rates, of permanent work
  actually executed and measured.
- **Materials on site / materials off site (vested materials)** — unfixed goods delivered to (or held off)
  site, claimable at delivered cost under the contract's materials clause, subject to evidence and protection
  (and, for off-site materials, a vesting certificate, insurance and often a bond).
- **Certification / certifier / payment certificate** — the independent verification of a claimed value, and
  the document stating the amount properly due; under FIDIC-style forms, often an Interim Payment Certificate
  (IPC) issued by the Engineer or Employer's Representative.
- **Retention** — a percentage withheld from each certified amount as security, released in stages (commonly
  half at Practical Completion, half at the end of the Defects Notification Period), net of any valid
  deduction for unremedied defects.
- **Practical Completion (PC) / Defects Notification Period (DNP)** — the point the works are substantially
  complete and fit for use, and the subsequent period during which defects may be notified and must be
  remedied before final retention release.
- **Advance payment / mobilisation payment, and its recovery** — a sum paid ahead of, or independently of,
  measured progress, recovered by fixed-instalment or proportional deduction until fully repaid.
- **Contra-charge / back-charge / set-off** — a cost the paying party is entitled to deduct from an amount
  otherwise due, for a cross-claim against the payee, valid only where the contract's notice requirements are
  followed.
- **VAT (value-added tax) / output tax / tax point** — the consumption tax charged on the value of the
  taxable supply, whose base (net-of-retention or full gross including retention) depends on the applicable
  VAT law and invoicing scheme.
- **Statutory payment/notice regime** — legislation (e.g. the UK's Housing Grants, Construction and
  Regeneration Act 1996 framework) or equivalent "security of payment" law mandating payment by instalments,
  a defined sum-due mechanism, notice procedures and rapid dispute resolution (adjudication).
- **Notified sum / payment notice / notice to pay less** — the sum considered due absent a valid notice
  reducing it, the notice stating the paying party's assessed sum, and the notice proposing to pay less than
  that sum, each subject to strict statutory/contractual deadlines.
- **Final date for payment** — the contractual deadline by which the notified (or reduced) sum must actually
  be paid.
- **Final account** — the single, comprehensive statement of a contract's total final value, incorporating
  all variations, resolving outstanding claims, and reconciling all prior payments and retention movements.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 7.4.1 [topic 7.4.1, level: recall]**
What best describes the purpose of an interim valuation / application for payment?
A. A final, binding statement of the total value of a completed contract
B. **A periodic claim, based on measured work executed (and, where the contract allows, materials on site), for payment on account of the final contract price before the contract is complete** ✔
C. A formal notice that varies the scope of the contract
D. A document that transfers title to unfixed materials from the contractor to the employer
*Rationale:* B is the definition of an interim valuation/application (7.4.1). A describes the final account
(7.4.9); C describes a variation order (cross-reference KA 5.4, KA 7.2); D describes a vesting certificate for
off-site materials (7.4.3), a narrower and different document.

**MCQ 7.4.2 [topic 7.4.3, level: application — numerical]**
A contractor has delivered and secured on site $24,000 of unfixed structural steel, fully evidenced by
supplier invoices and protected per the contract's materials-on-site clause. None of the steel has yet been
fixed into the permanent works. What amount should be included in the gross valuation for materials on site
this period?
A. $0 — unfixed materials cannot be claimed
B. **$24,000** ✔
C. $12,000 (a 50% allowance)
D. $2,400 (a 10% allowance)
*Rationale:* A properly evidenced and protected materials-on-site clause allows the full delivered/invoiced
cost of unfixed material to be claimed (7.4.3): `24,000`. A wrongly assumes materials on site are never
claimable; C and D apply arbitrary partial allowances with no basis in the mechanism.

**MCQ 7.4.3 [topic 7.4.5, level: application — numerical]**
Total retention on a contract is $60,000, released in two equal tranches at Practical Completion and the end
of the Defects Notification Period. At DNP end, a defect costing $5,000 to rectify remains unremedied by the
contractor and is validly deducted from the second tranche. What amount is released at DNP end?
A. $30,000
B. **$25,000** ✔
C. $55,000
D. $35,000
*Rationale:* `Second tranche = (60,000 / 2) − 5,000 = 30,000 − 5,000 = 25,000` (Example 7.4.5b's method). A
ignores the deduction; C and D reflect arithmetic slips.

**MCQ 7.4.4 [topic 7.4.6, level: application — numerical]**
An advance payment of $50,000 is recoverable in four equal, fixed instalments from the next four
applications. How much of the advance remains outstanding immediately after the second instalment has been
recovered?
A. $12,500
B. **$25,000** ✔
C. $37,500
D. $50,000
*Rationale:* `Instalment = 50,000 / 4 = 12,500`; after two instalments, `Recovered = 2 × 12,500 = 25,000`;
`Outstanding = 50,000 − 25,000 = 25,000`. A is a single instalment, not the outstanding balance; C is the
balance after only one instalment; D ignores that any recovery has occurred.

**MCQ 7.4.5 [topic 7.4.8, level: application — numerical]**
A period's gross valuation of work done is $200,000 (no materials on site). Retention is 5.0%. The net
valuation previously certified was $150,000. There is no advance-recovery or contra-charge adjustment this
period. VAT is due at an illustrative 15.0%. What is the total certificate amount payable?
A. $40,000
B. $44,000
C. **$46,000** ✔
D. $57,500
*Rationale:* `Retention = 5.0% × 200,000 = 10,000`; `Net valuation to date = 200,000 − 10,000 = 190,000`;
`Net amount due before VAT = 190,000 − 150,000 = 40,000`; `VAT = 15.0% × 40,000 = 6,000`; `Total =
40,000 + 6,000 = 46,000`. A omits VAT entirely; B applies an incorrect ~10% VAT rate; D omits the retention
deduction before comparing with the previously certified value.

**MCQ 7.4.6 [topic 7.4.9, level: analysis]**
Under a typical statutory payment/notice regime for construction contracts, if the paying party issues
neither a payment notice nor a notice to pay less by the deadlines the law and contract require, what is the
usual consequence?
A. The application automatically lapses and must be resubmitted from scratch
B. **The sum applied for (or last notified) generally becomes the sum properly due, payable in full by the final date for payment** ✔
C. The contractor forfeits its right to payment for that period entirely
D. VAT is automatically waived on the sum applied for
*Rationale:* B reflects the "silence favours the payee" mechanism common to statutory payment regimes of this
kind (7.4.9, Example 7.4.9a) — the burden is on the paying party to engage with the process on time. A, C and
D describe consequences with no basis in this mechanism.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using Example 7.4.8's Application 5 build-up, trace the figure from the $580,500 gross valuation to date
   down to the $62,100 total certificate amount payable, naming what each deduction (retention, previous
   certified value, advance recovery, contra-charge) represents and confirming the arithmetic reconciles.
2. Explain, in your own words, why the $18,000 of materials on site included in Application 5 does not also
   appear as a separate line in Application 6, and name the pitfall this avoids.
3. Using Example 7.4.6 and Example 7.4.8, confirm that Meridian's $30,000 advance payment is exactly fully
   recovered by the end of Application 6, and explain what would go wrong if a further recovery instalment
   were mistakenly deducted from Application 7.
4. Using Example 7.4.9b's final account, confirm that the two retention tranches ($38,600 each) sum to the
   total retention recalculated on the final contract value ($77,200), and explain why that figure supersedes
   the $75,000 estimate used illustratively in Example 7.4.5b.

**Answers**

1. `Gross valuation to date (580,500) − Retention 10.0% (58,050) = Net valuation to date (522,450)`; `522,450
   − Net valuation previously certified (450,225) = Net additional value certified this period (72,225)`;
   `72,225 − Advance recovery (15,000) − Contra-charge (3,225) = Net amount due before VAT (54,000)`; `54,000
   + VAT 15.0% (8,100) = Total certificate amount payable (62,100)`. Retention is security withheld against
   this period's work; the previously-certified deduction avoids re-paying value already certified in earlier
   applications; advance recovery repays cash already advanced for a different purpose (valve procurement);
   the contra-charge repays a specific cross-claim (temporary fencing) — each a distinct commercial event,
   summing correctly to $62,100.
2. Once the valve assemblies are physically fixed into the pipeline, their value is already included within
   Application 6's higher cumulative measured-work percentage (87.0%, valued at BoQ rates once installed).
   Leaving the same $18,000 in the materials-on-site line as well would count that value twice — once as
   installed measured work, once as unfixed material supposedly still awaiting installation — the
   double-counting pitfall flagged in 7.4.3.
3. `Application 5 recovery (15,000) + Application 6 recovery (15,000) = 30,000`, exactly matching the
   original advance — fully recovered. A further deduction on Application 7 would be double recovery: the
   employer would recoup $45,000 against an advance of only $30,000, short-paying Meridian $15,000 it is
   genuinely owed for work actually done, with no advance obligation left to justify the deduction.
4. `Total retention (recalculated) = 10.0% × Final contract value (772,000) = 77,200`; `77,200 / 2 = 38,600`
   per tranche; `38,600 + 38,600 = 77,200`, matching exactly. This figure supersedes the earlier $75,000
   estimate because that estimate was based on the *original* $750,000 contract price, before the $22,000
   variation for additional valve chambers was approved and folded into the final contract value; retention
   must always track the current, final contract value, not remain frozen at an earlier figure once
   variations change that value.
