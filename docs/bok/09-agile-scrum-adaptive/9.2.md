# Domain 9 — Agile, Scrum & Adaptive Delivery for Project Controls

## KA 9.2 — The Scrum framework — in depth

KA 9.1 sets out agile's shared foundation — the Agile Manifesto's values and principles, the empirical
mindset, and when adaptive planning fits a piece of work at all. Scrum is the framework almost every adaptive
project a controls professional will meet actually runs on, so this KA takes the step from "agile in general"
to "Scrum in particular," working through its rules precisely enough to plan, measure and audit against. The
Scrum Guide frames Scrum as a deliberately minimal framework — a small, fixed set of accountabilities, events
and artefacts, built to help a team tackle complex, uncertain work and deliver value adaptively, leaving the
specific engineering and estimation practices to the team itself. That minimalism matters here for a practical
reason: almost everything Scrum requires a team to do — plan visibly, meet daily, demonstrate working output,
reflect and improve — leaves behind a data trail, and KA 9.3 and KA 9.5 build the project-controls metrics
(velocity, burnup, AgileEVM) directly out of it. This KA covers Scrum's theory, the three pillars of
empiricism and the Scrum values (9.2.1); the three accountabilities — Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers
(9.2.2); the Sprint and Sprint Planning (9.2.3); the Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective
(9.2.4); the three artefacts and their commitments (9.2.5); and closes with a full sprint walk-through, a
sprint that goes wrong, and Scrum's response to it (9.2.6). Continuity runs on Meridian Rail Contracting LLC's
**Field Progress & Punch-list App** (KA 8.6.4, KA 8.6.5): a three-person, self-managing development team,
funded at a fixed **$15,000 (SAR 56,250, at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) per two-week Sprint**, against a
200-point Product Backlog. KA 8.6.4 recorded Sprints 1–3 (16, 19, 21 points; cumulative 56; average velocity
18.7 points/sprint) — this KA picks the story up at Sprint 4.

### 9.2.1 Scrum's foundation: empiricism, the three pillars and the Scrum values

**Definition & purpose.** Scrum rests on **empirical process control**: the working assumption that, for
complex, only-partly-predictable work, reliable knowledge comes from what is actually observed as work
proceeds, not from an upfront plan assumed correct until proven otherwise (KA 9.1 develops the empirical
mindset against predictive planning in full). The Scrum Guide — most recently revised in 2020, referenced here
only for its principles, never quoted — expresses empiricism through **three pillars**, and gives the Scrum
Team five behavioural commitments, the **Scrum values**, that make the pillars work in practice, not in name
only.

**The underlying principle: three pillars, one cycle.**
- **Transparency** — the state of the work, the plan and the definitions used to judge it must be visible and
  understood the same way by everyone with a stake in the outcome. A Sprint Backlog nobody but the Developers
  can read, or a "done" that means something different to the Product Owner than to the team, is not
  transparent even if a status report is technically being produced.
- **Inspection** — the artefacts and progress toward the Sprint Goal must be checked often enough, and
  attentively enough, to catch an undesirable variance while it is still cheap to correct — but not so often
  that inspection itself gets in the way of doing the work.
- **Adaptation** — when inspection shows the process or the product drifting outside an acceptable range, the
  plan (or, more rarely, the product itself) is adjusted as soon as practicable, not at the next scheduled
  review. Adaptation only works if the people doing the work are actually empowered to change their own plan —
  which is why Scrum makes the Developers self-managing (9.2.2) rather than routing every adjustment through an
  external approval step.

The three pillars form a cycle, not a checklist: transparency is what makes inspection meaningful (there is
nothing honest to inspect otherwise); inspection is what triggers adaptation; adaptation, executed well,
restores or improves transparency for the next cycle. A two-week Sprint gives this cycle a hard, countable
cadence: one Sprint Planning, ten Daily Scrums (one for each working day of a two-week Sprint), one Sprint
Review and one Sprint Retrospective — five distinct, scheduled inspection points in every ten working days,
quite apart from whatever informal checking happens between them.

**The Scrum values.** The Scrum Guide names five values that the Scrum Team is expected to live, not merely
display: **Commitment** (to the Sprint Goal and to each other), **Focus** (on the current Sprint's work rather
than on everything the backlog could contain), **Openness** (about work, progress and problems, including
admitting a Sprint is off-track), **Respect** (for colleagues as capable, independent professionals), and
**Courage** (to raise a hard truth, challenge a bad decision, or say a Sprint Goal is no longer achievable). The
values are not a separate compliance item bolted onto the framework — they are what makes transparency
trustworthy in the first place: a team that lacks Openness or Courage will produce a Sprint Backlog and a
Daily Scrum that look transparent on paper while quietly hiding the one fact that matters.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Mechanical Scrum** — running the events on the calendar (a daily meeting called "the Daily Scrum," a
  fortnightly meeting called "the Review") without the pillars actually functioning underneath: nothing is
  genuinely transparent, inspection is a status recital rather than a real check, and nothing changes as a
  result. The ceremonies survive; empiricism does not (developed further, with a live example, in 9.2.6).
- Treating the Scrum values as soft, unmeasurable and therefore optional — in practice, a Retrospective that
  never surfaces a hard problem, or a Daily Scrum where impediments are never actually raised, is a direct,
  observable symptom of Openness or Courage being absent, not an unrelated "culture issue."
- Confusing "inspect frequently" with "inspect constantly" — over-inspection (for example, requiring hourly
  status updates) breaches the same pillar it is meant to serve, by taking time away from the work being
  inspected.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based dashboarding tools can pull ticket/board state automatically into a live status
view, which looks like it strengthens transparency — but an automatically generated dashboard is only as
transparent as the underlying data entry is honest and current, and a shiny dashboard can mask, rather than
expose, a team quietly avoiding Openness (cross-reference KA 13.2, KA 13.6). AI cannot supply Commitment,
Focus, Openness, Respect or Courage; these remain team-culture matters for the Scrum Master to nurture, not a
tool to install.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.1 (empirical process control and adaptive planning, in full); KA 9.2.6 (mechanical
Scrum shown live, in the mini-case); KA 12.1 (risk and uncertainty, the wider context empiricism responds to).

### 9.2.2 The Scrum Team: three accountabilities — Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers

**Definition & purpose.** A **Scrum Team** is a single, cross-functional, self-managing unit made up of three
distinct **accountabilities** — Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers — with no sub-roles, tiers or reporting
hierarchy prescribed inside it. "Self-managing" means the team decides internally who does what, when and how,
without an external manager assigning individual tasks; "cross-functional" means the team collectively holds
every skill needed to turn a Product Backlog item into a usable Increment, without depending on people outside
the team for that. The Scrum Guide suggests the whole Scrum Team is usually best kept to **ten people or
fewer** — large enough to get meaningful work done inside a Sprint, small enough to coordinate without the
formal structure larger groups need.

**The three accountabilities.**
- **Product Owner (one person, not a committee)** — accountable for maximising the value the Scrum Team's work
  produces. In practice this means: setting and communicating the Product Goal; creating and ordering Product
  Backlog items so the highest-value work sits at the top; and making sure the Product Backlog stays visible,
  transparent and understood by everyone who needs it. The Product Owner may delegate the drafting work to the
  Developers, but the accountability for the resulting decisions cannot be delegated away.
- **Scrum Master** — accountable for the Scrum Team's effectiveness and for the framework itself being
  understood and genuinely applied, not merely staged. This runs in three directions: coaching the Developers
  in self-management and cross-functionality and clearing impediments in their way; helping the Product Owner
  work the Product Backlog effectively and communicate the Product Goal clearly; and helping the wider
  organisation understand empirical, Scrum-based working well enough to stop working against it (for example,
  by routing every small decision through an approval chain the team was supposed to own itself).
- **Developers** — the people who do the work of turning Product Backlog items into a usable Increment each
  Sprint, accountable collectively for: creating the plan for the Sprint (the Sprint Backlog); building
  quality into the work by holding it to the Definition of Done; adapting their plan daily toward the Sprint
  Goal; and holding one another accountable as professionals, rather than waiting for external direction.
  "Developers" is a Scrum accountability label, not a job title — on a software team it typically covers
  everyone building and testing the product (design, coding, testing), and on a non-software Scrum Team it
  covers whoever does the equivalent hands-on work.

On the Field Progress & Punch-list App, the three-person team (KA 8.6.4) instantiates all three
accountabilities within that small headcount: Meridian's PMO nominates one person as Product Owner (owning the
200-point backlog and its priority order), a second acts as Scrum Master alongside a share of hands-on
development work, and all three, including the Product Owner and Scrum Master when they pick up build or test
work, act as Developers for whatever portion of the Sprint Backlog they are actively delivering.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating "Developers" as excluding the Product Owner or Scrum Master from ever doing delivery work — on a
  small team especially, both routinely also act as Developers for part of the Sprint; the accountability
  label describes a function, not an exclusive person.
- Assigning tasks to individual Developers from outside the team (a project manager or line manager deciding
  who does what) — this directly breaches self-management, one of the conditions adaptation (9.2.1) depends
  on, and is a common symptom of an organisation that has adopted Scrum's ceremonies without its authority
  model.
- Confusing the Scrum Master with a project manager or an administrative meeting-scheduler — the accountability
  is for effectiveness and genuine adherence to the framework, including the uncomfortable parts (challenging
  an organisation's habits), not for task allocation or status reporting on the team's behalf.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based scheduling and note-taking assistants can handle real administrative load for a
Scrum Master (booking events, drafting a first pass at meeting notes) and can help a Product Owner draft
candidate Product Backlog items from stakeholder requests (cross-reference KA 9.3, KA 13.4). Neither can hold
an accountability: the Product Owner's value judgement, the Scrum Master's coaching and organisational-change
work, and the Developers' professional commitment to quality are not delegable to a tool, however useful the
tool is for the paperwork around them.

### 9.2.3 The Sprint and Sprint Planning: the container event and the commitment it produces

**Definition & purpose.** The **Sprint** is the container for all other Scrum events: a fixed-length period,
capped at **one calendar month**, inside which a usable Increment is created, and immediately followed by the
next Sprint with no gap between them. **Sprint Planning** is the event that starts a Sprint — the whole Scrum
Team's collaborative work to decide, and commit to, what that Sprint will attempt.

**The underlying principle: fixed time, variable scope, decided collaboratively.** Because the Sprint's length
is fixed in advance, Sprint Planning's job is not to schedule a fixed piece of work, but to decide how much of
a genuinely variable Product Backlog the Developers can realistically turn into a Done Increment inside that
fixed window (KA 8.6.4's "inverted iron triangle" — time and cost fixed, scope the variable being planned
for). Sprint Planning answers three questions in turn: **why** is this Sprint worth doing (the Product Owner
proposes how the product could gain value this Sprint, and the whole team agrees a single **Sprint Goal**);
**what** can realistically be Done (the Developers select Product Backlog items, using the Product Owner's
priority order and their own forecast of what they can deliver, as the basis for **commitment**, not the
Product Owner or anyone outside the team dictating the number); and **how** the selected work will get done
(the Developers break the selected items into a short-term, actionable plan, typically in pieces of a day or
less, that becomes the **Sprint Backlog**).

**Sprint length and the Sprint Planning timebox.** The Sprint Planning timebox is capped, for a one-month
Sprint, at **eight hours**; for a shorter Sprint, the Guide indicates the event should usually be shorter too,
without fixing an exact figure. A widely used facilitation convention — a practitioner guideline, not a Scrum
Guide rule — is to scale the one-month maximum roughly in proportion to Sprint length: for a two-week Sprint,
around **four hours or less**. Meridian's Field App team, running two-week Sprints, times its own Sprint
Planning to roughly **half that guideline figure**, at about **two hours**, reflecting a small, stable,
three-person team with a mature, well-refined backlog rather than the four-hour ceiling a larger or newer team
might need.

**Example 9.2.3 — Forecasting Sprint 4's commitment from capacity and historical velocity.**

*Scenario.* The Field App team is planning Sprint 4, a two-week (10-working-day) Sprint. Standard team
capacity is **3 Developers × 10 working days = 30 person-days**. One Developer is booked for two days of
external training during the Sprint, reducing available capacity to **28 person-days**. The three-Sprint
rolling average velocity from KA 8.6.4 is **18.7 points/Sprint** (Sprints 1–3: 16, 19, 21 points; cumulative
56; `56 ÷ 3 = 18.7`).

*Required.* Forecast a defensible Sprint 4 commitment ceiling, and select Product Backlog items up to that
ceiling.

*Working.* Capacity ratio = available person-days ÷ standard person-days = `28 ÷ 30 = 0.933`. Adjusted
forecast velocity = historical average × capacity ratio = `18.7 × 0.933 = 17.45 points`, rounded down to **17
points** — Developers forecast conservatively, since rounding up commits to a level of delivery the team's
actual reduced capacity does not support. The Developers then select Product Backlog items, in the Product
Owner's priority order, up to that ceiling: "Automated punch-list completion alerts to supervisors" (8 points);
"Supervisor dashboard read receipts" (3 points); "Push-notification delivery for the offline-then-reconnect
scenario" (5 points). Selected total = `8 + 3 + 5 = 16 points` — inside the 17-point ceiling, leaving a
1-point buffer.

*Answer.* Sprint 4's forecast capacity ceiling is **17 points**; the Sprint Backlog selected against it totals
**16 points**, under a single Sprint Goal: *"Give site supervisors real-time visibility of punch-list
completions, including when a site reconnects after working offline."*

*Interpretation.* The 17-point ceiling is a forecast, not a promise of exact delivery — it converts a known
capacity reduction (one Developer, two days) into a defensible, evidence-based adjustment to the historical
average, rather than either ignoring the reduced capacity or guessing at a round number. 9.2.6 follows this
same Sprint through to Sprint Review and shows that even a carefully forecast commitment can still be
disrupted mid-Sprint.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Letting the Product Owner or a manager dictate the Sprint Backlog's content or size rather than the
  Developers forecasting it themselves — this reproduces the accountability breach flagged in 9.2.2 and
  typically produces chronic over-commitment.
- Treating a Sprint Planning timebox scaling like the one used above as a Scrum Guide rule rather than a
  practitioner convention — the Guide fixes only the one-month maximum and says shorter Sprints usually need
  less time, without prescribing an exact proportional formula.
- Padding the commitment "just in case" rather than forecasting from genuine capacity data — an artificially
  low ceiling looks safe but wastes capacity and distorts the velocity trend KA 9.3 and KA 9.5 rely on.

**AI in this topic.** An AI-based capacity-planning assistant can perform exactly the arithmetic shown above —
combine a rolling average velocity with known absences to suggest a forecast ceiling — far faster than a
manual recalculation, and can flag when a proposed Sprint Backlog looks inconsistent with recent trend data
(cross-reference KA 9.3, KA 13.5). The commitment decision itself remains the Developers' own forecast of what
they can genuinely deliver to the Definition of Done; a tool's suggested ceiling is an input to that judgement,
not a substitute for it.

### 9.2.4 Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective: inspecting and adapting through the Sprint

**Definition & purpose.** These three events are where the pillars of inspection and adaptation (9.2.1) are
actually exercised at three different rhythms inside a single Sprint: daily (the **Daily Scrum**), once at the
Sprint's end for the product (the **Sprint Review**), and once at the Sprint's end for the team's own way of
working (the **Sprint Retrospective**).

- **Daily Scrum** — a **15-minute**, time-boxed event held at the same time and place every working day of
  the Sprint, for the Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the Sprint Backlog for
  the day ahead. It is not a status report to the Product Owner or Scrum Master; if either of them is actively
  delivering Sprint Backlog work, they participate as Developers, and anyone else attending does so without
  disrupting the Developers' own event. Its practical value is early surfacing of anything blocking progress —
  an **impediment** — while there is still runway left in the Sprint to respond to it.
- **Sprint Review** — held at the Sprint's end to inspect the outcome and decide what happens next. The Scrum
  Team demonstrates the actual Increment to key stakeholders and discusses progress toward the Product Goal;
  it is a working, collaborative session, not a one-way status presentation, and its output — stakeholder
  feedback, and any resulting change to the Product Backlog's content or order — feeds directly into the next
  Sprint Planning. Capped, for a one-month Sprint, at **four hours**.
- **Sprint Retrospective** — closes the Sprint by having the Scrum Team inspect how the last Sprint went for
  itself: its people, interactions, process, tools and how well the Definition of Done served it — and agree
  the most valuable changes to make before the next Sprint starts. Capped, for a one-month Sprint, at **three
  hours**.

**The underlying principle.** These three events give the pillars three distinct objects of inspection at
three distinct intervals: the Daily Scrum inspects the *plan*, daily; the Sprint Review inspects the *product*,
once per Sprint; the Sprint Retrospective inspects the *team and process that produced the product*, once per
Sprint. Removing any one of the three leaves a genuine blind spot — daily-only inspection never asks whether
the product itself is actually right, and product-only inspection never asks whether the team's own working
practices need to change.

**Practitioner timebox guideline for the Field App's two-week Sprint.** As introduced in 9.2.3, halving the
one-month maximums is a common, though not Scrum Guide-mandated, convention: Sprint Review at roughly **two
hours or less**, Sprint Retrospective at roughly **90 minutes or less**. The Daily Scrum's 15-minute timebox
does not scale with Sprint length — it is fixed regardless of Sprint duration, because its purpose (a quick
daily check, not a deep analysis) does not change with the length of the Sprint around it.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Turning the Daily Scrum into a status report delivered upward to the Scrum Master or a manager, rather than
  a peer-to-peer planning conversation among the Developers — this quietly reintroduces external task
  assignment (9.2.2's pitfall) through the back door.
- Treating the Sprint Review as a demo-only presentation with no real stakeholder discussion, or skipping it
  when "there's nothing finished to show" — an incomplete or disappointing Increment is exactly the situation
  the Review exists to surface honestly, not a reason to cancel it.
- Running a Sprint Retrospective that only lists positives, or running it so infrequently that it stops
  influencing the next Sprint's plan — a Retrospective that produces no genuine change is a symptom of the
  same mechanical-Scrum pitfall flagged in 9.2.1.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based transcription and summarisation tools can capture Daily Scrum updates, draft
Sprint Review demo notes, and produce a first-pass log of Sprint Retrospective discussion points and agreed
actions, reducing note-taking overhead (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.4). They can also flag a recurring word
or theme across several Daily Scrums (for example, the same blocker mentioned three days running) faster than
a busy Scrum Master might notice it. They cannot hold the live conversation that makes inspection and
adaptation real — deciding what a piece of stakeholder feedback actually means for the Product Backlog, or
what a Retrospective's root cause actually is, remains the Scrum Team's own judgement.

### 9.2.5 The three artefacts and their commitments: Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment

**Definition & purpose.** Scrum's three **artefacts** each represent work or value in progress, and each
carries a **commitment** — a statement of intent that gives the artefact a clear target to measure progress
against, so that transparency (9.2.1) has something concrete to be transparent about.

| Artefact | What it is | Its commitment |
|---|---|---|
| **Product Backlog** | The single, ordered, emergent list of everything currently understood to be needed to improve the product | **Product Goal** — the longer-term target the Scrum Team is currently working toward |
| **Sprint Backlog** | The Sprint Goal, the Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint, and the Developers' plan for delivering them | **Sprint Goal** — the single objective the whole Sprint's selected work serves |
| **Increment** | Every Product Backlog item that has met the Definition of Done during the Sprint (or across Sprints), added to everything delivered before it | **Definition of Done** — the agreed quality bar an item must clear before it counts as part of the Increment |

**The underlying principle.** The **Product Goal** is a durable target — normally spanning many Sprints —
that must be substantially achieved (or deliberately abandoned) before the Scrum Team takes up an unrelated
new objective; it gives the Product Backlog's ordering a "why" beyond "highest priority item first." The
**Sprint Goal** is the single coherent purpose that turns a set of individually selected backlog items into one
Sprint rather than several unrelated mini-projects running in parallel; because it is a purpose rather than a
literal list, the exact items delivering it can be renegotiated during the Sprint without the Sprint Goal
itself needing to change (9.2.6 shows exactly this happening). The **Definition of Done** is the artefact
commitment a controls professional should pay closest attention to, because it functions as Scrum's equivalent
of a binary progress-measurement rule (compare the 0/100 earned-value method, KA 6.1.4): an item that has not
met every Definition of Done criterion is **not** part of the Increment, earns **no** velocity credit, and
returns to the Product Backlog for a future Sprint — there is no partial credit for "nearly done."

**Example 9.2.5 — The Field App's Definition of Done, applied.**

*Scenario.* The Field App team has agreed the following Definition of Done, applied to every Product Backlog
item before it can count toward the Increment: (1) code complete and merged to the main branch; (2) unit tests
written and passing, covering at least 80% of new code; (3) peer-reviewed by at least one other Developer;
(4) functional/acceptance criteria verified against the paired backlog item; (5) tested on at least two
representative device/network profiles, including one low-bandwidth/offline profile; (6) no known critical or
high-severity defect open against the item; (7) user-facing documentation or help text updated where
applicable. At Sprint 4's Review, the Developers check "Automated punch-list completion alerts to supervisors"
(8 points, selected in Example 9.2.3) against this checklist.

*Required.* Determine whether the item meets the Definition of Done and can be counted in the Increment.

*Working.* All seven criteria are confirmed: code merged; unit tests passing at 84% coverage; peer-reviewed by
a second Developer; acceptance criteria (supervisors receive an alert within 60 seconds of a punch-list item
being marked complete) verified against the paired backlog item; tested on both a current and a two-year-old
device model, including a throttled 2G network profile; no open critical/high-severity defects; the in-app
help text for supervisors updated. Criteria met = `7 of 7`.

*Answer.* The item **meets the Definition of Done in full (7/7)** and is counted as part of the Sprint 4
Increment, contributing its full **8 points**.

*Interpretation.* Because every criterion was met, this item's 8 points are earned cleanly — no partial or
provisional credit is recorded. 9.2.6 applies the same seven-criterion checklist to a second Sprint 4 item
that does **not** clear it, and follows the consequence through.

Chart type: a three-lane horizontal flow diagram, brand blue `#1D4ED8`. Lane 1: "Product Backlog" flowing
toward a distant marker labelled "Product Goal." Lane 2, nested inside a Sprint-length box beneath Lane 1:
"Sprint Backlog" flowing toward a marker labelled "Sprint Goal." Lane 3, beneath that: individual Product

**Animation 9.2.5 — "The Definition of Done as a gate" [digital-only].** Frame 1: a Product Backlog item
labelled "8 points" approaches a gate. Frame 2: the seven Definition of Done criteria light up one at a time
as each is checked. Frame 3: all seven lit, the gate opens, and the item slides into the "Increment" bar,

**Common pitfalls.**
- **No agreed Definition of Done at all**, or one so vague ("looks finished") that different team members
  apply it differently — this destroys the transparency the whole framework depends on, because "done" stops
  meaning the same thing to everyone, and velocity figures built on it become unreliable inputs to KA 9.3 and
  KA 9.5's forecasts.
- Confusing the Definition of Done (a quality bar, applied to every item) with acceptance criteria (a
  description of what one specific item must do) — both must be satisfied, but they answer different
  questions: "is this good enough to ship, generally?" versus "is this the right thing, specifically?"
- Letting the Product Goal drift unstated or informally understood only by the Product Owner — without a
  stated Product Goal, backlog ordering decisions have no shared "why" for the Scrum Team or stakeholders to
  evaluate against.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted checks integrated into a build pipeline can automatically verify some
Definition of Done criteria that are objectively testable — test coverage percentage, whether a peer review
was recorded, whether known defects are open against an item (cross-reference KA 13.4). They cannot certify
criteria that require human judgement, such as whether an interface is genuinely usable by a site supervisor
in the field; a green tick from an automated check is evidence toward meeting the Definition of Done, not a
substitute for the team's own sign-off on it.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.2.3 (Sprint Planning, which selects the items this topic's gate is later applied
to); KA 6.1.4 (the 0/100 earned-value measurement method, the closest classical analogue to a binary
Definition-of-Done gate); KA 9.3 (velocity — built entirely from points that have passed this gate); KA 9.5
(AgileEVM, which inherits the same "no partial credit" logic).

### 9.2.6 A Sprint from backlog to increment: a full walk-through, a sprint gone wrong, and the data it leaves for project controls

**Definition & purpose.** This topic threads 9.2.1–9.2.5 together into a single, continuous Sprint — Sprint 4
of the Field Progress & Punch-list App — from Sprint Planning through to Sprint Review and Retrospective,
including a genuine mid-Sprint disruption, so the framework's response to a sprint going wrong can be shown
operating, not merely described.

**Example 9.2.6 — Sprint 4, start to finish.**

*Scenario.* Following Example 9.2.3, Sprint 4's Sprint Backlog (16 of a 17-point forecast ceiling) comprises
three items under the Sprint Goal *"Give site supervisors real-time visibility of punch-list completions,
including when a site reconnects after working offline"*: **Item A** — automated punch-list completion alerts
(8 points, confirmed Done in Example 9.2.5); **Item B** — supervisor dashboard read receipts (3 points);
**Item C** — push-notification delivery for the offline-then-reconnect scenario (5 points). At the Day 6 Daily
Scrum (of 10 working days), the Developer working Item C reports an impediment: the third-party push-
notification vendor's test sandbox has become intermittently unreachable, blocking the low-bandwidth/offline
network-profile test that Item C's Definition of Done criterion 5 (9.2.5) requires.

*Required.* Trace Scrum's response to the impediment through to Sprint Review, and determine each item's final
status and Sprint 4's actual delivered velocity.

*Working.* The impediment is surfaced at the Daily Scrum on Day 6, with four working days of the Sprint
remaining — early enough to act on. The Scrum Master takes ownership of chasing the vendor and finding a
workaround (escalating a support ticket, then locating an alternative test harness), rather than the
Developers absorbing that overhead themselves. The Developers **adapt the Sprint Backlog**, not the Sprint
Goal: they re-sequence remaining work so Items A and B — not dependent on the vendor sandbox — are finished
first, while a workaround for Item C's test is pursued in parallel. By Day 9, a partial workaround is found,
but it does not give enough confidence to certify criterion 5 for Item C by Sprint end. At Sprint Review (Day
10): Item A — 8 points, 7/7 Definition of Done criteria met (Example 9.2.5) — is demonstrated as Done. Item B
— 3 points — is independently confirmed 7/7 and demonstrated as Done. Item C — 5 points — is checked against
the same seven-criterion Definition of Done and scores **6 of 7**: criterion 5 (tested on the low-bandwidth/
offline network profile) remains unmet. Per 9.2.5's rule, Item C does **not** count toward the Increment; it
returns to the Product Backlog for re-selection in a future Sprint. Sprint 4's actual delivered velocity =
`8 + 3 = 11 points` — against a Sprint Backlog of 16 points selected, a shortfall of `16 − 11 = 5 points`, or
`5 ÷ 16 ≈ 31%` of the sprint's planned scope.

*Answer.* The impediment did **not** require cancelling the Sprint or renegotiating the Sprint Goal — the
Scrum Master's escalation and the Developers' re-sequencing kept two of three items on track, and the Sprint
Goal was **substantially achieved** (supervisors gained real-time alerts and read-receipt visibility; the
reconnect-scenario notification did not). Sprint 4 delivered **11 points** against a 16-point Sprint Backlog
and a 17-point forecast ceiling.

*Interpretation.* This is Scrum's graduated, built-in response to disruption, applied in the order the
framework actually intends: (1) surface the problem as early as possible, via the Daily Scrum, not at Sprint
Review; (2) attempt to remove the impediment, a Scrum Master accountability; (3) if the impediment cannot be
fully cleared in time, re-plan the *Sprint Backlog* — re-sequencing or dropping specific items — while
protecting the *Sprint Goal* wherever the Goal can still be substantially met; (4) apply the Definition of
Done honestly rather than stretching it, even under schedule pressure, so a genuinely incomplete item is
recorded as incomplete rather than smuggled into the Increment. Cancelling the Sprint outright — an option the
Product Owner alone can exercise, and only when the Sprint Goal itself has become obsolete, not merely harder
to reach — was never appropriate here, because the Goal remained substantially achievable throughout.

**Mini-case — what a genuinely obsolete Sprint Goal looks like, by contrast.** Suppose, instead, that on Day 3
Meridian's client had informed the Product Owner that offline capability was being dropped entirely from the
programme's scope for regulatory reasons unrelated to the app. Every one of Sprint 4's three items existed
*only* to serve the now-void Sprint Goal. This is the narrow case the Scrum Guide reserves for **Sprint
cancellation** — a Product Owner decision, rarely exercised, because in almost every real disruption (as in
the Day 6 impediment above) at least part of the Sprint Goal remains worth pursuing and a full cancellation
would waste the Developers' work-in-progress unnecessarily. The distinction matters for a controls
professional: an impediment inside a Sprint is managed through Sprint Backlog adaptation and normally leaves
the Sprint Goal, and any downstream cost/schedule commitment built on it, intact; a genuinely obsolete Sprint
Goal is a materially different event, closer in scale to a predictive project's formal re-baseline (KA 6.1.2),
and should be reported as such rather than folded quietly into ordinary sprint variance.

**The controls data this Sprint's cadence has already produced.** Nothing in Sprint 4 was measured specially
for a controls audience — the data below is simply what running the framework honestly generates as a
by-product, and is exactly what KA 9.3 and KA 9.5 build their metrics from:

| Scrum mechanism | Controls-relevant data it produced this Sprint |
|---|---|
| Sprint Planning (9.2.3) | A capacity-adjusted forecast ceiling (17 points) and a committed Sprint Backlog (16 points) — an auditable basis for what was expected |
| Daily Scrum, Day 6 (9.2.4) | Early, dated evidence of an impediment and when it was raised — a leading indicator, not a lagging surprise at Sprint Review |
| Definition of Done gate (9.2.5) | A binary, criterion-by-criterion record of why Item C did not count — auditable justification for the shortfall, not an unexplained miss |
| Sprint Review (9.2.4) | The actual delivered velocity, 11 points, as verified, demonstrated fact rather than a self-reported percentage |
| Sprint Retrospective (9.2.4) | An agreed improvement action — for example, building a mocked test harness for third-party dependencies earlier in future Sprints — a documented, dated response to the shortfall |

This single data point (11, not the forecast 16–17) will pull Sprint 4 below the 18.7 points/Sprint trend line
once it joins the rolling average KA 9.3 tracks, and will feed directly into KA 9.5's next AgileEVM forecast
update. Consistent with KA 8.6.4's inverted iron triangle, no formal change control or cost re-baseline is
triggered by this shortfall on its own — the fixed Sprint cost of $15,000 (SAR 56,250) does not change, and
the response is a re-forecast of remaining scope/timeline, not a re-budgeting exercise; the vendor-sandbox
dependency itself, however, is a fact worth carrying forward onto the project's risk register (KA 12.2), since
it may recur in a later Sprint that also depends on the same third party.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Stretching the Definition of Done under schedule pressure** — counting Item C as Done because "it's
  basically finished" would have overstated Sprint 4's velocity, quietly corrupting every rolling-average
  forecast built on it afterwards (KA 9.3, KA 9.5); the discipline of an honest gate matters most exactly
  when there is pressure to relax it.
- Treating every shortfall as grounds for a Sprint cancellation, or conversely never considering cancellation
  when a Sprint Goal genuinely is obsolete — both misjudge the narrow, Product-Owner-only condition the Scrum
  Guide reserves cancellation for, illustrated by contrast in the mini-case above.
- Reporting Sprint 4 as "one bad sprint" without carrying the underlying cause (a third-party dependency) into
  the risk register — the Retrospective's improvement action addresses the team's own process, but the
  external dependency itself is a risk-management item, not only a team-process one.

**AI in this topic.** An AI-based flow- or velocity-anomaly detection tool, watching the Sprint Backlog board
in near real time, could plausibly have flagged Item C's stalled progress before Day 6 (cross-reference KA
13.5), giving the Scrum Master an earlier nudge than the scheduled Daily Scrum alone. It cannot decide whether
a stalled item still serves the Sprint Goal, whether the Scrum Master's chosen workaround is acceptable, or
whether a criterion has genuinely been met — those remain live, in-the-room judgements for the Scrum Team.
**AI proposes; the professional disposes** — including, and especially, the decision of whether an Increment
genuinely meets its Definition of Done.

### Key terms

- **Empirical process control** — the working assumption that reliable knowledge, for complex work, comes
  from observation as work proceeds rather than from an upfront plan alone.
- **Three pillars** — transparency, inspection and adaptation; the cycle empiricism runs on in Scrum.
- **Scrum values** — Commitment, Focus, Openness, Respect, Courage; the behaviours that make the pillars
  trustworthy rather than performative.
- **Scrum Team** — a single, cross-functional, self-managing unit of ten people or fewer, comprising the
  Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers.
- **Product Owner** — accountable for maximising value; sets the Product Goal and orders the Product Backlog.
- **Scrum Master** — accountable for the Scrum Team's effectiveness and genuine adherence to the framework.
- **Developers** — accountable for the Sprint Backlog, the Increment's quality, and daily adaptation toward
  the Sprint Goal.
- **Sprint** — the fixed-length, one-month-or-less container for all other Scrum events.
- **Sprint Planning** — the event that starts a Sprint, producing the Sprint Goal and the Sprint Backlog.
- **Daily Scrum** — the Developers' 15-minute daily inspection-and-adaptation event.
- **Sprint Review** — the end-of-Sprint inspection of the Increment with stakeholders.
- **Sprint Retrospective** — the end-of-Sprint inspection of the team's own process and practices.
- **Product Backlog / Product Goal** — the ordered, emergent list of product work, and the durable target it
  serves.
- **Sprint Backlog / Sprint Goal** — the selected work-plus-plan for one Sprint, and the single objective
  unifying it.
- **Increment / Definition of Done** — the accumulated, verified product output, and the quality bar an item
  must clear to be counted in it.
- **Impediment** — anything blocking a Developer's progress, surfaced for removal, typically via the Daily
  Scrum.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 9.2.1 [topic 9.2.1, level: recall]**
Which three elements make up Scrum's empirical process control?
A. Planning, execution, closure
B. **Transparency, inspection, adaptation** ✔
C. Commitment, focus, courage
D. Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment
*Rationale:* the three pillars are transparency, inspection and adaptation (9.2.1). C lists three of the five
Scrum values, not the pillars; D lists the three artefacts, a different concept entirely.

**MCQ 9.2.2 [topic 9.2.2, level: application]**
On a three-person Scrum Team, the person accountable for maximising the value of the product also spends part
of every Sprint writing and testing code. Which statement is correct?
A. This breaches Scrum, because a Product Owner may never do delivery work
B. **This is consistent with Scrum — accountabilities describe functions, and one person may hold more than
   one, especially on a small team** ✔
C. This means the team has no true Product Owner
D. This is only acceptable if the person is also the Scrum Master
*Rationale:* 9.2.2 explains that "Developers" describes a function, not an exclusive person, and both the
Product Owner and Scrum Master commonly also act as Developers on a small team. A and C wrongly treat the
accountabilities as mutually exclusive job titles; D adds an unsupported extra condition.

**MCQ 9.2.3 [topic 9.2.3, level: application — numerical]**
A three-Developer team's standard capacity for a two-week Sprint is 30 person-days. One Developer is
unavailable for 3 days during the Sprint. The three-Sprint rolling average velocity is 20 points/Sprint. What
is the capacity-adjusted forecast ceiling, rounded down to the nearest whole point?
A. 20 points
B. 17 points
C. **18 points** ✔
D. 15 points
*Rationale:* available capacity `= 30 − 3 = 27` person-days; capacity ratio `= 27 ÷ 30 = 0.9`; adjusted
forecast `= 20 × 0.9 = 18` points exactly. A ignores the reduced capacity; B and D apply the wrong ratio.

**MCQ 9.2.4 [topic 9.2.4, level: recall]**
What is the fixed timebox for the Daily Scrum, regardless of Sprint length?
A. 30 minutes
B. **15 minutes** ✔
C. 1 hour
D. It scales proportionally with Sprint length, like Sprint Planning
*Rationale:* the Daily Scrum's 15-minute timebox is fixed and does not scale with Sprint length, unlike
Sprint Planning, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective, whose one-month maximums a shorter Sprint typically
scales down from (9.2.4).

**MCQ 9.2.5 [topic 9.2.5, level: application]**
A Product Backlog item meets six of the Field App's seven Definition of Done criteria by Sprint end; the
seventh (a low-bandwidth network test) is not completed. What happens to the item?
A. It counts as 6/7 = 86% of its points toward the Increment
B. It counts in full, since most criteria were met
C. **It does not count toward the Increment at all, and returns to the Product Backlog** ✔
D. The Scrum Master decides whether to count it
*Rationale:* the Definition of Done is a binary gate, not a partial-credit scale (9.2.5) — an item that has
not met every criterion is not part of the Increment and earns no velocity credit, exactly as Item C is
treated in 9.2.6. A and B wrongly imply partial credit; D misattributes the decision, which is not a
discretionary call by any single accountability but the mechanical consequence of the gate itself.

**MCQ 9.2.6 [topic 9.2.6, level: analysis]**
Midway through a Sprint, a Developer reports at the Daily Scrum that a specific backlog item is blocked by an
external vendor issue, but the rest of the Sprint Backlog is unaffected and the Sprint Goal remains largely
achievable. What is the framework-consistent response?
A. The Product Owner immediately cancels the Sprint
B. The team ignores the impediment until Sprint Review, since raising it mid-Sprint is not Scrum's role
C. **The Scrum Master works to remove the impediment, and the Developers adapt the Sprint Backlog while
   protecting the Sprint Goal wherever it remains achievable** ✔
D. The item is marked Done anyway, since most of the Sprint Goal will still be met
*Rationale:* 9.2.6 shows exactly this graduated response — early surfacing, impediment removal, Sprint
Backlog adaptation, and an honestly applied Definition of Done. A is reserved for a genuinely obsolete Sprint
Goal, not a partial, contained impediment; B contradicts the Daily Scrum's purpose; D breaches the Definition
of Done's binary gate.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. In your own words, explain how the three pillars of empiricism (transparency, inspection, adaptation) form
   a cycle, and name one Scrum event or artefact that principally serves each pillar.
2. A three-Developer team has a standard two-week Sprint capacity of 30 person-days. Two Developers are each
   unavailable for one day during the Sprint (2 person-days lost in total). The three-Sprint rolling average
   velocity is 22 points/Sprint. Calculate the capacity-adjusted forecast ceiling, rounded down to the nearest
   whole point.
3. Distinguish the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal and the Definition of Done — what does each commitment
   attach to, and over what timeframe does each apply?
4. A Sprint's forecast Sprint Backlog was 20 points; only 14 points met the Definition of Done by Sprint
   Review. Explain, referring to KA 8.6.4's inverted iron triangle, why this shortfall does not by itself
   trigger a formal cost re-baseline in the way a predictive project's equivalent shortfall might.

**Answers**

1. Transparency makes an honest, shared picture of the work available (served principally by the Sprint
   Backlog and the Definition of Done); inspection uses that shared picture to check progress and detect
   problems early (served principally by the Daily Scrum and the Sprint Review); adaptation acts on what
   inspection finds by adjusting the plan or the product (served principally by Sprint Backlog re-planning and
   the Sprint Retrospective) — and a well-adapted plan restores transparency for the next cycle, closing the
   loop (9.2.1).
2. Available capacity `= 30 − 2 = 28` person-days; capacity ratio `= 28 ÷ 30 = 0.933`; adjusted forecast
   `= 22 × 0.933 = 20.53`, rounded down to **20 points**.
3. The Product Goal is the durable, multi-Sprint target the Product Backlog's ordering serves, and normally
   spans many Sprints until substantially achieved or deliberately abandoned. The Sprint Goal is the single
   objective unifying one Sprint's selected work, set fresh (or reaffirmed) at each Sprint Planning, and
   protected even when the specific items delivering it are renegotiated mid-Sprint. The Definition of Done is
   a standing quality bar, not tied to any one Sprint or Goal, that every Product Backlog item must clear
   before it is counted as part of the Increment (9.2.5).
4. Under the inverted iron triangle (KA 8.6.4), an adaptive Sprint fixes time and cost in advance (the
   Sprint's length and the team's run-rate) and treats scope as the variable being forecast, not the fixed
   baseline scope treats it as under classical EVM. A shortfall against the Sprint Backlog is therefore read as
   new information about achievable scope/velocity — feeding the next rolling-average forecast (KA 9.3, KA
   9.5) — rather than as a variance against a fixed, baselined scope requiring formal change control and
   re-baselining, because no scope baseline was fixed in the first place for the Sprint to vary against.
