# Domain 9 — Agile, Scrum & Adaptive Delivery for Project Controls

## KA 9.4 — Kanban, Lean & scaling

KA 9.1 introduces the agile mindset and empirical process control; KA 9.2 develops Scrum, the best-known
timeboxed framework built on that mindset; KA 9.3 develops the estimation and flow metrics — story points,
velocity, burndown and burnup — that a Scrum team uses to plan and report a sprint at a time. This KA turns
to a second major family of adaptive practice that does not depend on fixed-length iterations at all:
**Kanban**, a pull-based method for visualising work and managing its flow, and the **Lean** thinking that
underlies it. Where Scrum answers "how much can this team commit to in the next fixed-length sprint?",
Kanban answers "how do we keep a continuous stream of arriving work moving smoothly, without over-committing
the team at any moment?" — a question that fits work with unpredictable arrival, such as production support,
maintenance and small enhancement requests, at least as naturally as it fits new-build development. Flow
metrics divide by family in this Body of Knowledge: KA 9.3 covers Scrum's own metrics (velocity, burndown,
burnup); this KA covers Kanban's flow metrics (the cumulative flow diagram, lead time, cycle time and
Little's Law) — both describe the same underlying delivery, viewed through each method's own lens. Continuity
runs on the same illustration KA 8.6 introduced: Meridian Rail Contracting LLC's **Field Progress &
Punch-list App**, built by a three-person team across 11 two-week Scrum sprints for a total of $165,000
(SAR 618,750 at an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**) (KA 8.6.4). From go-live, Meridian's PMO retains two of
the three developers on an ongoing enhancement-and-support flow — bug fixes and small feature requests
reported by site engineers arrive continuously and unpredictably, not as a prioritised, sprint-ready backlog,
which is exactly the kind of work Kanban, rather than Scrum, fits best (extending the suitability judgement
of KA 8.6.2 from "predictive vs. adaptive" to "which adaptive method"). This KA closes with scaling
frameworks — SAFe, LeSS and Scrum of Scrums — at awareness level: not because Meridian's small teams need
them yet, but because a project controls professional must recognise what coordination problem each addresses
once a programme genuinely outgrows a single team.

### 9.4.1 Kanban: principles, practices and the board

**Definition & purpose.** Kanban — Japanese for "signal" or "visual card" — is a pull-based method for
visualising work, limiting how much is in progress at any moment, and managing the resulting flow, without
prescribing fixed-length iterations, sprint-based accountabilities or a mandated set of ceremonies. Its
origin lies in the mid-20th-century Toyota Production System's use of physical cards to signal that a
downstream station was ready to pull more work from an upstream one; its adaptation to knowledge and software
work is most closely associated with David J. Anderson's **Kanban Method**, which frames the practices below
as an evolutionary way of improving an existing workflow, rather than a prescribed process that replaces it
wholesale. Where Scrum (KA 9.2) organises delivery around fixed-length sprints and defined accountabilities,
Kanban organises delivery around the continuous flow of individual work items through a visualised set of
workflow stages.

**The underlying principle.** The Kanban Method rests on four foundational ideas, summarised here rather than
reproduced verbatim: start with the workflow as it exists today, rather than redesigning it from scratch;
agree to pursue change through small, incremental, evolutionary steps rather than a single disruptive
overhaul; respect existing roles, responsibilities and job titles at the outset, changing them only where the
data supports it; and encourage acts of leadership, and improvement, at every level, not only from
management. Six practices operationalise those ideas: visualise the workflow; limit work in progress; manage
flow; make process policies explicit; implement feedback loops; and improve collaboratively, evolving
experimentally using measured outcomes. This topic develops the board itself (visualise, explicit policies,
feedback loops); 9.4.2 develops limiting WIP and managing flow.

**The Kanban board.** A Kanban board is the visual representation of those practices in use: a set of
columns, left to right, representing the stages a work item passes through from request to done (for
example, Backlog, Analysis, In Progress, Review, Done); cards, one per work item, that move across the board
as work advances; and, for at least the active-work columns, an explicit, visible work-in-progress (WIP)
limit — the maximum number of cards a column may hold at once (9.4.2). Each column's explicit policy — what
must be true for a card to move into the next column — is one of the six practices in itself: making the
"definition of done" for each stage visible and agreed, not assumed.

**Example 9.4.1 — reading the board's current state against its limits.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's Field App support flow uses the board in Fig 9.4.1: Analysis (WIP limit 2), In
Progress (WIP limit 3), Review (WIP limit 1), with Backlog and Done unlimited (an intake queue and a
completed-work record respectively). On a given morning the board shows 12 cards in Backlog, 2 in Analysis, 3
in In Progress, 1 in Review, and 24 in Done for the current measurement window.

*Required.* State whether the board is within its WIP limits, and the total number of cards actively "in
process" (committed, not yet done).

*Working.* Analysis: 2 of 2 (at limit). In Progress: 3 of 3 (at limit). Review: 1 of 1 (at limit). Every
active column sits exactly at, not over, its limit. Total WIP (Analysis + In Progress + Review, excluding
Backlog and Done) = `2 + 3 + 1 = 6`.

*Answer.* The board is within limits; total WIP = **6 cards** — the figure 9.4.2's Little's Law example
reuses directly.

*Interpretation.* A board sitting exactly at its limits, column by column, is a healthy, fully-loaded state,
not a problem: no column can pull in new work until it releases a card downstream, which is precisely the
"pull" discipline 9.4.2 and 9.4.4 develop.

**Fig 9.4.1 — the Field App support-flow Kanban board.**
Chart type: a five-column Kanban board diagram. Columns left to right: Backlog (no WIP limit — an intake
queue), Analysis (WIP limit 2), In Progress (WIP limit 3), Review (WIP limit 1), Done (a running count, no
limit). Each active column's header shows "current / limit" (e.g. "2/2"); cards are small rounded rectangles
in brand blue `#1D4ED8`. A column header fills solid blue when at its limit, signalling "full — nothing more
may be pulled in until a card leaves."

*Underlying data (the snapshot in Example 9.4.1):*

| Column | WIP limit | Cards currently in column |
|---|---|---|
| Backlog | — (intake) | 12 |
| Analysis | 2 | 2 |
| In Progress | 3 | 3 |
| Review | 1 | 1 |
| Done (this window) | — | 24 |

**Animation 9.4.1 — "Pulling a card across the board" [digital-only].** Frame 1: the board as in Fig 9.4.1,
each active column shown full and shaded solid. Frame 2: a card completes in Review and moves to Done; the
Review column header unshades, showing "0/1." Frame 3: a card is pulled from In Progress into the now-open
Review slot; In Progress unshades to "2/3" and Review re-shades to "1/1." Frame 4: a new card is pulled from
Analysis into the open In Progress slot, and a Backlog card is pulled into Analysis, restoring every column to
its limit. Narration cue: "Nothing moves forward until something moves out — that is the pull system in one
sequence."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating the board as decoration rather than the single source of truth for actual work state — a board
  whose cards are not moved in real time gives a false picture of where work actually stands.
- Too many or too few columns — too many obscures the real workflow in noise; too few (e.g. a single "In
  Progress" column covering analysis, build and review together) hides exactly where a card is stuck.
- Skipping the explicit column policy — without an agreed "what must be true to move this card," different
  team members move cards on different, inconsistent judgements.

**AI in this topic.** AI-enabled board tools (Jira, Azure DevOps and similar platforms) can auto-suggest a
starting column structure from a description of a team's process, and can flag cards that have sat inactive
unusually long. They cannot determine the *right* workflow stages for a specific team's real process without
that team's own domain knowledge; the professional configures, and periodically revalidates, the board design
(cross-reference KA 13.4).

**Cross-references.** KA 9.2 (Scrum's timeboxed alternative to bounding commitment); KA 8.6.2 (the
suitability judgement, extended here from predictive-vs-adaptive to Scrum-vs-Kanban); KA 9.3 (backlog
structuring, shared with Kanban's Backlog column).

### 9.4.2 Limiting work in progress, Little's Law and managing flow

**Definition & purpose.** A work-in-progress (WIP) limit is an explicit, agreed ceiling on the number of work
items a workflow stage — a board column, or the board as a whole — may hold at any one time; **managing
flow** is the ongoing practice of using that ceiling, and the data it generates, to keep work moving smoothly
rather than merely tracking status. Where Scrum bounds work by time (the sprint) and negotiates scope to fit
within it (KA 9.2, KA 9.3), Kanban bounds work by WIP and lets the resulting flow determine how long any one
item takes — two different mechanisms for the same underlying goal of matching commitment to actual capacity.

**The underlying principle: why limiting WIP shortens lead time.** Starting more work at once feels
productive but is not the same as finishing more work: every additional item in progress adds
context-switching overhead and dilutes attention, and because a team's completion capacity (its throughput)
does not increase merely because more items are started, average waiting time per item rises. This
relationship is formalised by **Little's Law**, a general result from queueing theory (John D. C. Little,
1961) that applies to any stable flow system, a Kanban board included.

**Formula — Little's Law.**

`L = W / λ`

where **L** = average lead time (the average time an item spends in the system, in days); **W** = average
work in progress (the average number of items in the system at once, a count); **λ** (throughput) = the
average rate at which items are completed (items per day). Equivalently, `W = L × λ`: for a given throughput,
the WIP a team carries directly sets its average lead time, and vice versa — the lever a WIP limit actually
pulls.

**Example 9.4.2 — deriving average lead time for the Field App's Kanban support flow.**

*Scenario.* From go-live (KA 8.6.4), two of the Field App's three developers are retained on the Kanban
support-and-enhancement flow of Fig 9.4.1, at a combined burdened cost of approximately $1,000 per working
day (SAR 3,750 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1). Over a 20-working-day measurement window the team
completed 40 cards; the board's average total WIP, sampled daily across Analysis, In Progress and Review, was
6 cards (matching Example 9.4.1's snapshot).

*Required.* (a) Throughput; (b) average lead time by Little's Law.

*Working.* (a) `λ = 40 cards ÷ 20 days = 2 cards/day`. (b) `L = W / λ = 6 cards ÷ 2 cards/day = 3 days`.

*Answer.* Average lead time ≈ **3 days** per card, at a throughput of 2 cards/day and average WIP of 6 cards.

*Interpretation.* A site engineer raising a ticket today can expect it done in about three days on average,
given the team's current WIP discipline — a concrete, defensible service expectation the Kanban flow can
quote, in the same way Scrum quotes a sprint-length commitment for its own backlog (KA 9.3).

**Mini-case — what happens when a WIP limit is breached.**

*Scenario.* A new site joins the Meridian programme and nine urgent tickets land in one day. Under delivery
pressure, the team abandons the In Progress column's limit of 3 and pulls all nine into progress at once
("start everything, sort it out as we go"). Over the following week, measured throughput falls to 1.5
cards/day, because developers are now context-switching between nine half-finished items instead of finishing
three at a time.

*Required.* Compare the Little's Law lead time under the WIP-limit discipline of Example 9.4.2 with the lead
time once WIP balloons to 9 and throughput falls to 1.5 cards/day.

*Working.* Disciplined state: `L = 6 / 2 = 3 days` (as before). Breached state: `L = 9 / 1.5 = 6 days`.

*Answer.* Lead time **doubles, from 3 to 6 days**, even though — indeed, precisely because — more items were
started at once.

*Interpretation.* Starting more work did not finish more work faster; it finished the same or less work, more
slowly, for every ticket, including the nine urgent ones the team was trying to help. The correct response to
an arrival spike is to swarm on the highest-priority items within the existing limit, clearing them before
pulling more, or to consciously renegotiate and communicate a temporarily higher limit with its consequences
understood — not to let the limit lapse informally under pressure.

**Common pitfalls.**
- No WIP limits at all — a board that only shows status without constraining how much is committed at once is
  a task list, not a Kanban system, and forfeits the entire flow benefit (the mini-case above).
- Treating a WIP limit as a target to *fill* rather than a ceiling not to *exceed* — a column sitting below
  its limit is not "underperforming"; it may simply mean upstream or downstream capacity is the current
  constraint.
- Setting limits once and never revisiting them — a limit is a hypothesis about capacity, tested and adjusted
  as flow data (9.4.3) accumulates, not a fixed rule.

**AI in this topic.** Many Kanban-capable tools include AI-assisted flow analytics that compute rolling WIP
and throughput automatically and can suggest a WIP-limit adjustment from historical data. Such a suggestion
reflects only what the tool measured — one built on mis-tagged cards, paused work miscounted as active, or an
unrepresentative period (a holiday week, a one-off spike) will mislead — so the professional must check the
underlying data quality (cross-reference KA 13.2) before accepting an AI-suggested limit change.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.2 (Scrum's time-boxed alternative to bounding commitment); KA 9.3 (velocity as
Scrum's own capacity measure); KA 8.6.2 (the suitability judgement, extended from predictive-vs-adaptive to
Scrum-vs-Kanban).

### 9.4.3 Lead time vs. cycle time, and the cumulative flow diagram

**Definition & purpose.** Lead time and cycle time are both durations, and are frequently — and wrongly —
used interchangeably; the distinction matters because they answer different questions for different
audiences. **Lead time** is measured from the moment a work item is requested or logged (enters the system)
to the moment it is delivered (leaves the system as Done) — it is what the requester actually experiences.
**Cycle time** is measured only across the active-work portion of that journey — typically from when a card
is pulled into the first active-work column to when it reaches Done — excluding time spent queuing, unstarted,
in Backlog. The **cumulative flow diagram (CFD)** is the primary Kanban chart for reading both, and for
reading WIP and throughput trends, at a glance.

**The underlying principle.** Because lead time = queue time + cycle time, cycle time is always less than or
equal to lead time for the same item; the gap between them is exactly the time a request spent waiting before
anyone started on it — itself a candidate for the "waiting" waste Lean thinking names explicitly (9.4.4). A
team can improve cycle time (working faster once started) without touching lead time at all if queue time is
the larger problem, or vice versa — which is why both must be tracked, not just one.

**Example 9.4.3 — separating lead time and cycle time for a single card.**

*Scenario.* A card logged in Backlog on the morning of Day 1 is pulled into Analysis on the morning of Day 3
(two days queuing), then takes a further two days of active work across Analysis, In Progress and Review
before reaching Done at the end of Day 5.

*Required.* State the card's lead time, cycle time and queue time.

*Working.* Lead time = Day 1 (logged) to Day 5 (done) = **4 days**. Cycle time = Day 3 (pulled into active
work) to Day 5 (done) = **2 days**. Queue time = `4 − 2 = 2 days`.

*Answer.* Lead time 4 days; cycle time 2 days; queue time 2 days.

*Interpretation.* The site engineer who logged the ticket experienced a 4-day wait; the team's own delivery
discipline, once it picked the ticket up, took 2 days — and it is that 2-day figure, not the 4-day one, that a
kaizen action targeting the team's own working practice (9.4.4) will move.

**Fig 9.4.3 — cumulative flow diagram: reading WIP and lead time from the same chart.**
Chart type: stacked-area / cumulative-count line chart. Horizontal axis: working day (Day 0–10, the first
half of Example 9.4.2's 20-day measurement window). Vertical axis: cumulative number of cards. Two lines
plotted, both rising left to right: cumulative cards arrived (entered Backlog) and cumulative cards done; the
shaded band between them, in brand blue `#1D4ED8`, represents WIP at each day. A vertical double-headed arrow
at Day 10 spans the band, labelled "WIP = 6"; a horizontal double-headed arrow connects the point where each
curve crosses the same cumulative count, labelled "Lead time ≈ 3 days."

*Underlying data* (first 10 working days of Example 9.4.2's 20-day window; steady arrivals and completions at
2 cards/day hold WIP constant, consistent with Little's Law):

| Day | Cumulative arrived | Cumulative done | WIP (arrived − done) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 10 | 4 | 6 |
| 1 | 12 | 6 | 6 |
| 2 | 14 | 8 | 6 |
| 3 | 16 | 10 | 6 |
| 4 | 18 | 12 | 6 |
| 5 | 20 | 14 | 6 |
| 6 | 22 | 16 | 6 |
| 7 | 24 | 18 | 6 |
| 8 | 26 | 20 | 6 |
| 9 | 28 | 22 | 6 |
| 10 | 30 | 24 | 6 |

Throughput check: `(24 − 4) done ÷ 10 days = 2 cards/day`, matching Example 9.4.2. Reading the chart
horizontally: the arrived curve reaches a cumulative count of 10 at Day 0; the done curve reaches the same
cumulative count of 10 at Day 3 — a 3-day horizontal gap, the same lead time Little's Law derived
algebraically.

**Animation 9.4.3 — "Reading WIP and lead time off one chart" [digital-only].** Frame 1: both curves draw in
together, Day 0 to Day 10, band unshaded. Frame 2: the band between them shades in brand blue, captioned "the
vertical gap is WIP." Frame 3: a vertical double-headed arrow snaps onto the Day-10 band, labelled "WIP = 6."
Frame 4: a horizontal double-headed arrow draws between the two curves at the same cumulative-count level,
labelled "Lead time ≈ 3 days," with a caption "same chart, same data, two different readings." Narration cue:
"Height apart tells you how much is in progress; width apart tells you how long it waits."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting cycle time to a requester as if it were lead time — it understates their actual experienced wait
  by the queue-time portion (Example 9.4.3).
- Reading a CFD's band width only at the most recent date rather than its trend — a momentarily narrow band
  can sit at the end of a widening trend a single snapshot hides.
- Treating Little's Law as valid during a visibly non-steady state (a launch spike, a public-holiday freeze,
  the WIP-breach mini-case of 9.4.2) — it is a long-run average relationship for a stable flow, not an
  instantaneous predictor for an unstable one.

**AI in this topic.** Several Kanban tools include AI-assisted forecasting (for example, simulation-based
delivery forecasts run over historical cycle-time distributions) that can flag a widening CFD band, or a
lengthening lead-time trend, earlier than a manual weekly review would. Such forecasts assume future flow
resembles the historical flow they were trained on; a genuinely new class of work, or a structural team
change, breaks that assumption, and the professional must sense-check any AI-generated flow forecast against
known context before reporting it (cross-reference KA 13.5).

**Cross-references.** KA 9.3 (Scrum's own flow-adjacent metrics — burndown, burnup); KA 4.4 (data
visualisation and storytelling — the CFD as a control-chart-style device); KA 9.4.2 (Little's Law, from which
this topic's lead-time reading follows).

### 9.4.4 Lean thinking: waste, value stream, pull, flow and kaizen

**Definition & purpose.** Lean thinking is the broader management philosophy Kanban's practices draw on: a
discipline, originating in the Toyota Production System and adapted to software and knowledge work chiefly
through Mary and Tom Poppendieck's *Lean Software Development*, for identifying and removing waste from a
process so that value flows to the customer as directly as possible. This topic is covered at **awareness
level**: a project controls professional needs to recognise Lean's core vocabulary and reasoning, not to run
a Lean transformation programme.

**The underlying principle: five connected ideas.** Lean thinking is usually summarised as: identify **value**
from the customer's point of view, not the producer's; map the **value stream** — every step a request passes
through from ask to delivery — and distinguish value-adding steps from waste; make value **flow** without
interruption, stalling or unnecessary batching; let **pull** (a downstream signal of readiness, such as a
Kanban WIP limit freeing up) trigger new work, rather than pushing work forward regardless of downstream
capacity; and pursue **kaizen** — continuous, incremental improvement, driven by the people doing the work,
rather than a one-off redesign.

**Waste, adapted to knowledge work.** *Lean Software Development* adapts the Toyota Production System's waste
categories to software and knowledge work, in essence: **partially done work** (inventory sitting unfinished,
tying up effort without yet delivering value); **extra features** (building more than the customer asked for
or needs); **relearning** (losing, and having to rediscover, knowledge — for example through poor
documentation or staff turnover); **handoffs** (information lost or delayed each time work passes between
people or teams); **task switching** (the context-switching cost the WIP-breach mini-case of 9.4.2
demonstrated numerically); **delays/waiting** (the queue-time component Example 9.4.3 separated from cycle
time); and **defects** (rework caused by errors not caught at the point they were made). A value-stream map
makes these visible by laying out every step a request passes through and marking, honestly, which steps add
value and which do not.

**Example 9.4.4 — a small kaizen action on the Field App support flow, quantified through the flow metrics
already established.**

*Scenario.* A value-stream review of the support flow (9.4.2, 9.4.3) finds that Example 9.4.3's 2-day cycle
time splits into 0.8 days of active work (Analysis and In Progress combined) and 1.2 days of waiting in the
Review column, because only one senior developer is authorised to review and is frequently unavailable — a
"waiting" waste. The team cross-trains a second reviewer as a kaizen action.

*Required.* Recompute cycle time and lead time after the kaizen action reduces the average Review wait from
1.2 to 0.3 days, holding the 2-day queue time and 0.8-day active-work time unchanged.

*Working.* New cycle time = `0.8 (active work) + 0.3 (reduced Review wait) = 1.1 days` (down from 2.0). New
lead time = `2.0 (queue, unchanged) + 1.1 (new cycle time) = 3.1 days` — wait: Example 9.4.3's queue time was
2 days, giving original lead time `2 + 2 = 4 days`, matching that example exactly. New lead time =
`2.0 + 1.1 = 3.1 days`.

*Answer.* Lead time falls from 4.0 to **3.1 days** — a reduction of roughly 23% — without adding headcount,
purely by removing a single-reviewer bottleneck identified through the value-stream map.

*Interpretation.* At the flow's combined day-rate of $1,000 (SAR 3,750, 9.4.2), a smaller average work in
progress carrying cost per ticket is a direct, if modest, financial echo of the flow-time improvement; the
larger point for a project controls professional is that Lean's discipline of naming a waste precisely (here,
waiting caused by a single point of review capacity) turned a vague "things feel slow" complaint into a
specific, measurable, cheaply-fixed cause — exactly the improve-collaboratively practice 9.4.1 named as part
of the Kanban Method.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Equating "Lean" with headcount reduction — Lean thinking targets waste in the *process*, and explicitly does
  not treat people as the waste; using "Lean" as cover for a staffing cut misapplies the term and undermines
  the trust a genuine kaizen culture depends on.
- Treating a value-stream map as a one-off exercise rather than a recurring habit — kaizen means continuous
  improvement; a map drawn once and filed away captures a single moment, not an improving system.
- Optimising one step in isolation without checking its effect on the whole value stream — speeding up Review
  while starving it of properly-analysed cards from an under-resourced Analysis step simply moves the
  bottleneck (and the waiting waste) rather than removing it.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted analysis of a team's own ticket history can surface candidate wastes
automatically — for example, flagging a recurring rework pattern against a specific change type as a
defect-waste signal, or flagging columns with the longest average dwell time as a waiting-waste signal — as a
well-informed input to a value-stream-mapping session. It cannot run the kaizen conversation itself, weigh the
human and organisational trade-offs of a proposed change, or decide which improvement to prioritise; that
remains a team and professional judgement (cross-reference KA 13.5).

**Cross-references.** KA 9.4.2 (WIP and task-switching waste, quantified); KA 9.4.3 (cycle time vs. lead
time, the basis for this topic's worked example); KA 5.1 (cost drivers and waste in classical cost
management, the finance-domain analogue).

### 9.4.5 Scaling agile delivery: SAFe, LeSS and Scrum of Scrums (awareness level)

**Definition & purpose.** A single Scrum team (KA 9.2) or a single Kanban flow (9.4.1–9.4.3) is designed to
coordinate itself; neither, by itself, says how to coordinate several such teams working on a shared,
interdependent product or programme. Scaling frameworks exist to answer that coordination problem once it
genuinely arises. This topic covers three widely referenced approaches at **awareness level** — the problem
each addresses and its core mechanism, not a full treatment — because Meridian's own teams (three developers
building the Field App, two running its support flow) are far too small to need any of them; the purpose here
is recognition for future judgement, not implementation guidance.

**The underlying principle: right-size to the actual coordination problem.** Each of the three approaches
below trades a different amount of added process for a different amount of coordination capability; none is
"better" in the abstract, and each can be misapplied by adopting more structure than the actual number of
interdependent teams, and the actual degree of shared backlog, justify.

| Framework | Coordination problem it addresses | Core mechanism | Typical scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scrum of Scrums** | Cross-team dependency and impediment visibility, without adding a new framework layer | A regular meeting of a representative from each team's Scrum, surfacing cross-team blockers and dependencies | A handful of teams (roughly 2–9) |
| **LeSS (Large-Scale Scrum)** | Scaling Scrum's *own* rules, largely unchanged, to many teams building one product | One Product Backlog, one Product Owner and one Sprint shared across all teams; teams coordinate directly with each other rather than through added roles or layers | Several teams, up to roughly 8 (LeSS Huge extends the same idea further) |
| **SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)** | Aligning many teams — and multiple organisational levels (team, programme, portfolio) — around a shared cadence and value stream, including work beyond software | Agile Release Trains (ARTs): multiple teams synchronised on a fixed-cadence Program Increment (PI), with defined roles and events at each organisational level | Tens to hundreds of practitioners across a large programme or enterprise |

**Mini-case — which approach, if any, would fit a larger Meridian digital programme?** Suppose Meridian's
PMO, encouraged by the Field App's delivery record (KA 8.6.4, 8.6.5), commissions four further small digital
site tools — a materials-tracking app, a quality-inspection app and two others — each staffed by two to three
developers, all drawing on a shared component library and mobile shell. If the five backlogs remain largely
independent, with only occasional shared-library dependencies to flag, a lightweight Scrum of Scrums,
coordinating representatives across the five teams, is proportionate. If, instead, all five tools are
recognised as facets of one "digital site tools" product with a single roadmap and a single accountable
Product Owner, LeSS's one-backlog, one-Sprint model fits the actual structure of the work. If the digital
programme must additionally synchronise its releases with Meridian's wider 24-month predictive stage-gate
programme (KA 8.6.3's hybrid pattern) — for example, aligning a Program Increment boundary with a stage-gate
review date — SAFe's heavier, cadence-based structure earns its overhead precisely because a genuine
multi-team, multi-level coordination need exists. None of the three is the "right answer" in isolation; each
answers a different shape of coordination problem, and the shape of Meridian's actual programme, not a general
preference, decides.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Cargo-cult scaling** — adopting SAFe's full role and event set (or LeSS's, or any framework's) because it
  is well known, before the coordination problem it exists to solve — genuinely interdependent multiple
  teams — actually exists; a single team wearing a scaled framework's ceremonies carries the overhead without
  the benefit, the scaling analogue of 8.6.3's "agile-in-name-only" pitfall.
- The opposite failure — leaving a programme that has already scaled past a single team with no coordination
  mechanism at all, so cross-team dependencies surface only once they cause a delay.
- Assuming a scaling framework replaces the underlying team-level practice — SAFe's ARTs still run on
  team-level Scrum or Kanban (9.4.1–9.4.3); LeSS is explicitly built from single-team Scrum rules, not a
  replacement for them.

**AI in this topic.** Portfolio and programme-management AI features can help surface cross-team dependencies
at scale automatically — for example, detecting linked work items across several teams' boards — as a useful
input to a Scrum-of-Scrums session or a SAFe ART's Program Increment planning. Such tools cannot decide
whether, or which, scaling framework an organisation should adopt: that is a structural and governance
decision for accountable leadership, resting on the actual shape of the programme's coordination need, not a
tool recommendation. **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 9.6 (hybrid delivery & agile governance, including contracting and reporting cadence

### Key terms

- **Kanban** — a pull-based method for visualising work, limiting WIP and managing flow, without fixed-length
  iterations.
- **Kanban board** — the visual representation of a workflow's stages, work items (cards) and WIP limits.
- **WIP limit** — an explicit ceiling on the number of work items a workflow stage may hold at once.
- **Little's Law** — `L = W / λ`: average lead time equals average WIP divided by average throughput, for a
  stable flow.
- **Throughput (λ)** — the average rate at which items are completed (items per unit time).
- **Lead time** — the time from a work item's request/arrival to its completion.
- **Cycle time** — the time from when active work starts on an item to its completion (a subset of lead time).
- **Cumulative flow diagram (CFD)** — a chart of cumulative arrived vs. cumulative completed items over time,
  from which WIP (vertical gap) and lead time (horizontal gap) can be read.
- **Pull system** — work is drawn into a stage only when downstream capacity frees up, rather than pushed
  forward regardless of capacity.
- **Value stream** — the full sequence of steps a request passes through from ask to delivery.
- **Lean thinking** — a philosophy for removing waste from a process so value flows to the customer as
  directly as possible.
- **Kaizen** — continuous, incremental improvement driven by the people doing the work.
- **Muda (waste)** — Lean's term for any activity that consumes effort without adding customer value.
- **Scrum of Scrums** — a lightweight, regular cross-team coordination meeting.
- **LeSS (Large-Scale Scrum)** — a minimal scaling of Scrum's own rules to multiple teams sharing one backlog,
  one Product Owner and one Sprint.
- **SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)** — a multi-level framework aligning many teams around a fixed-cadence
  Program Increment via Agile Release Trains.
- **Agile Release Train (ART)** — SAFe's grouping of multiple teams synchronised on a shared Program
  Increment cadence.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 9.4.1 [topic 9.4.1, level: recall]**
Which best describes the Kanban Method's approach to organisational change, as distinct from Scrum's approach?
A. Kanban prescribes a fixed set of roles and a mandated set of ceremonies, applied from day one
B. **Kanban starts from the existing workflow and pursues change through small, incremental, evolutionary
   steps** ✔
C. Kanban requires all work-in-progress limits to be removed before a board can be used
D. Kanban and Scrum are two names for the same underlying method
*Rationale:* 9.4.1 states the Kanban Method's foundational approach as starting where you are and evolving
incrementally, contrasted with Scrum's prescribed roles/events (KA 9.2). C inverts actual practice (limiting,
not removing, WIP); D denies a real distinction the exam blueprint tests.

**MCQ 9.4.2 [topic 9.4.2, level: application — numerical]**
A Kanban flow completes 60 items over a 15-day measurement window, with an average of 8 items in progress at
any time. Using Little's Law, what is the average lead time?
A. 1.25 days
B. 4 days
C. **2 days** ✔
D. 7.5 days
*Rationale:* Throughput `λ = 60/15 = 4 items/day`. Lead time `L = W/λ = 8/4 = 2 days`. A inverts the division;
B mistakes throughput for lead time; D divides items by the wrong figure.

**MCQ 9.4.3 [topic 9.4.2, level: analysis]**
A team's In Progress column has an agreed WIP limit of 3. Facing a sudden spike of urgent requests, the team
pulls all of them into progress at once, well past the limit, and throughput then falls due to increased
context-switching. What does Little's Law predict?
A. Lead time will fall, because more items are being worked on simultaneously
B. Lead time is unaffected by WIP, only by throughput
C. **Lead time will rise, because WIP has increased while throughput has fallen — both changes push lead
   time up** ✔
D. Little's Law no longer applies once a WIP limit is breached
*Rationale:* per `L = W/λ`, a higher W and a lower λ both increase L (9.4.2's mini-case: 3 days → 6 days). B
ignores W's role in the formula; D is incorrect — Little's Law describes any stable flow, breached limit or
not, though a breach can itself destabilise the flow.

**MCQ 9.4.4 [topic 9.4.3, level: application]**
A card is logged on Day 1, sits unstarted until Day 3, and is completed on Day 5. What are its cycle time and
lead time?
A. Cycle time 4 days; lead time 2 days
B. **Cycle time 2 days; lead time 4 days** ✔
C. Cycle time 4 days; lead time 4 days
D. Cycle time 2 days; lead time 2 days
*Rationale:* lead time = Day 1 (logged) to Day 5 (done) = 4 days; cycle time = Day 3 (pulled into active
work) to Day 5 (done) = 2 days, per the definitions in 9.4.3. A reverses the two figures; C and D each
conflate the two measures.

**MCQ 9.4.5 [topic 9.4.4, level: recall]**
Under Lean thinking's adaptation to knowledge work, a ticket sitting unattended in a queue because only one
person is authorised to review it is best classified as which kind of waste?
A. Extra features
B. Relearning
C. **Waiting** ✔
D. Defects
*Rationale:* time an item spends queued, unworked, because of a capacity constraint (here, a single-reviewer
bottleneck) is the "waiting" waste (9.4.4's Example 9.4.4). Extra features concerns unneeded scope;
relearning concerns lost knowledge; defects concerns rework from errors — none describe queued, unworked time.

**MCQ 9.4.6 [topic 9.4.5, level: analysis]**
An organisation has five teams building genuinely separate products with only occasional shared-library
dependencies. Which scaling approach is most proportionate to that coordination need?
A. SAFe, with full Agile Release Train structure and Program Increment cadence
B. LeSS, merging all five teams onto one shared Product Backlog and Sprint
C. **Scrum of Scrums, coordinating a representative from each team on shared dependencies** ✔
D. No coordination mechanism is needed, since the products are separate
*Rationale:* occasional cross-team dependencies with otherwise independent backlogs is exactly the
lightweight coordination problem Scrum of Scrums addresses (9.4.5); SAFe and LeSS both assume a tighter
shared-product or shared-cadence structure than described, and D ignores the stated dependencies, which is
the mirror pitfall to cargo-cult scaling — coordination neglect.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. In your own words, explain the difference between lead time and cycle time, and state which of the two a
   requester waiting for a ticket actually experiences.
2. A Kanban flow measures an average WIP of 10 items and a throughput of 5 items/day. (a) Calculate the
   average lead time using Little's Law. (b) If the team wants to halve that lead time without changing
   throughput, what WIP limit should it target instead?
3. Explain, in your own words, why adopting SAFe's full framework for a single five-person team would likely
   be cargo-cult scaling.
4. Identify two of Lean's "wastes" that a Kanban board carrying no WIP limits and long queue times would tend
   to make visible, and explain why each applies.

**Answers**

1. Lead time runs from when a request is logged to when it is delivered; cycle time runs only from when
   active work starts to delivery, excluding queue time. The requester experiences **lead time** — the full
   elapsed wait, including time not yet started.
2. (a) `L = W/λ = 10/5 = 2 days`. (b) To halve lead time to 1 day at the same throughput of 5 items/day:
   `W = L × λ = 1 × 5 = 5 items` — the team should target a WIP limit of about 5, roughly half its current
   level.
3. A single team does not have a multi-team coordination problem for SAFe's Agile Release Train and Program
   Increment cadence to solve; imposing that structure adds roles, events and overhead without a genuine
   cross-team dependency problem to justify them — the scaling analogue of "agile-in-name-only" (8.6.3, 9.4.5).
4. Any two of: task switching/partially done work (uncapped WIP invites starting many items at once, each
   left partially finished); waiting (long queue times are, by definition, time items spend not being worked
   on); defects (context-switching increases error rates, generating rework). Credit any two correctly
   explained against the definitions in 9.4.4.
