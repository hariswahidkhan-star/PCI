# Domain 9 — Agile, Scrum & Adaptive Delivery for Project Controls

## KA 9.3 — Backlogs, estimation & agile metrics

KA 9.1 establishes the agile mindset and empirical process control; KA 9.2 walks the Scrum framework's
accountabilities, events and artefacts end to end. Both describe the Product Backlog as *an* artefact and
the Sprint as *a* timebox. This KA supplies the mechanics a project controls professional actually works
with day to day: how a backlog is written and refined, how its items are sized without a stopwatch, and how
size-over-time becomes the handful of metrics — **velocity**, **burndown**, **burnup** — that answer the two
questions every sponsor asks of an adaptive scope: *how much have we got, and when will the rest be done?*
KA 8.6.4 already showed why these metrics exist: adaptive delivery fixes time and cost and leaves scope
variable, inverting the classical "iron triangle" that Domain 6's EVM assumes. This KA is where that
inversion is made operational. It reuses the **Field Progress & Punch-list App** — Meridian Rail Contracting
LLC's three-person, in-house software team, funded at a fixed **$15,000 (SAR 56,250) per two-week sprint**
against a **200-point backlog**, first introduced in KA 8.6.4 — and extends its Sprint 1–3 figures (16, 19
and 21 points, reused here **byte-for-byte**) through two further sprints to build the fuller worked examples
below. Everything computed here is the direct input to KA 9.5's AgileEVM, which re-expresses the same points
and run-rate in EV/CPI/SPI/EAC terms, and to KA 9.4's Kanban flow metrics, which pick up cycle time and
throughput where this KA's brief mention of the cumulative flow diagram leaves off.

### 9.3.1 The product backlog: structure, refinement and prioritisation

**Definition & purpose.** The **Product Backlog** is, in the Scrum Guide's own description, the single,
ordered list of everything known to be needed in the product — the sole source of requirements for any
changes to be made. **Backlog refinement** (sometimes called grooming) is the ongoing activity of adding
detail, estimates and order to backlog items so that a subset is ready to be pulled into an upcoming Sprint
Planning event (KA 9.2). Refinement is continuous, not a single gated review: items near the top of the
backlog are refined into small, well-understood units; items further down remain deliberately coarse until
they are closer to being worked on.

**The underlying principle.** A Product Backlog is never complete — it is an emergent artefact that evolves
as the product and its environment evolve, which is precisely why classical, once-and-for-all scope
baselining (KA 8.2, KA 6.1.1) does not apply to it. A widely used mnemonic for a healthy backlog's
characteristics is **DEEP**: **D**etailed appropriately (top items fine-grained, lower items coarse),
**E**stimated (at least the near-term items), **E**mergent (expected to change as understanding grows), and
**P**rioritised (ordered by value, risk and dependency, not merely listed). This is common agile practice
terminology, not a Scrum Guide requirement itself, but it is a useful checklist for a controls professional
auditing whether a backlog is actually being managed or merely accumulating.

**Prioritisation (awareness level).** Two widely used techniques order a backlog beyond simple value
judgement:

- **MoSCoW** — categorises items as **M**ust have, **S**hould have, **C**ould have, **W**on't have (this
  time); a coarse but fast way to align a release's scope with a fixed deadline or budget.
- **WSJF (Weighted Shortest Job First)** — associated with the SAFe framework (awareness level; the form
  shown is the widely used general shape, not quoted from any single source) — prioritises by **Cost of
  Delay ÷ Job Size**, where Cost of Delay is typically a sum of relative scores for user/business value,
  time criticality and risk-reduction/opportunity-enablement value, and Job Size is a relative-estimation
  figure (9.3.4). A small illustration: Story A scores user-value 8, time-criticality 5, risk-reduction 2
  (Cost of Delay `= 8+5+2 = 15`) against a job size of 5, giving `WSJF = 15/5 = 3.0`; Story B scores Cost of
  Delay 8 against job size 8, giving `WSJF = 8/8 = 1.0`. Despite being roughly the same effort band, Story A
  is prioritised first because it delivers more value per unit of size sooner.

**Mini-case — MoSCoW on the Field App's next release.** The Field App's Product Owner refines five candidate
items ahead of Sprint 6 (9.3.5's continuation point):

| Backlog item | MoSCoW category | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Offline photo capture with punch-list tagging | Must | Core value proposition; site engineers cannot log progress without it |
| Punch-list item assignment to a trade contractor | Must | Required for the workflow the app replaces |
| Automatic sync retry with conflict resolution | Should | High value at poor-connectivity sites, but a manual workaround exists |
| Push notifications for overdue punch-list items | Could | Useful, not release-blocking |
| Custom report branding/theming | Won't (this release) | No progress-tracking value; deferred |

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a refinement session as a formal, gated sign-off (mirroring predictive change control, KA 5.4)
  rather than a continuous, lightweight activity — this reintroduces predictive rigidity into an artefact
  whose entire value is staying emergent.
- Refining the *whole* backlog to the same fine-grained level of detail — wasted effort, since lower-priority
  items will likely change before they are worked on; DEEP's "detailed *appropriately*" is deliberate.
- Confusing MoSCoW's "Won't have this time" with "never" — it defers, it does not delete.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based backlog assistants can draft candidate backlog items and epics from a feature
description or stakeholder notes, and suggest a first-pass split of a coarse epic into smaller candidate
stories (cross-reference KA 9.6, KA 13.4, KA 13.5). This is a genuine time-saver in refinement sessions, but
the Product Owner remains accountable for value, order and what is actually included in the backlog — an
AI-suggested split or priority order is a draft for the refinement conversation, not its output.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.2 (Product Backlog as a Scrum artefact, in full); KA 9.3.2 (turning a refined item
into a written story); KA 9.3.4 (estimating the "Job Size" WSJF uses); KA 8.6.4 (why scope stays variable in

### 9.3.2 Writing user stories: format, INVEST and acceptance criteria

**Definition & purpose.** A **user story** is a short, structured description of a piece of functionality
from the perspective of the person who wants it, conventionally written *"As a `<role>`, I want `<capability>`,
so that `<benefit>."* Its purpose is to keep a backlog item's value visible and to defer the *how* to the
Developers, who design the implementation during the Sprint. **Acceptance criteria** are the specific,
checkable conditions a story must satisfy to be accepted as complete — narrower and item-specific,
distinguishable from the Definition of Done (KA 9.2), which is the team's shared, Increment-wide quality
bar applied to every item alike.

**The underlying principle: INVEST.** A widely used checklist (industry practice, not Scrum Guide text)
holds that a well-formed story should be:

| Letter | Criterion | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| I | Independent | Stories that can only be delivered, or estimated, in a fixed sequence with others |
| N | Negotiable | A rigid specification that leaves the Developers no room to choose *how* |
| V | Valuable | A technical task with no visible benefit to a user or the product |
| E | Estimable | A story too vague, or too dependent on unknowns, to size at all |
| S | Small | A story too large to complete within one Sprint |
| T | Testable | A story with no way to check, objectively, whether it is done |

**Example 9.3.2 — Critiquing and rewriting a Field App story against INVEST.**

*Scenario.* A first-draft story reads: *"As a user, I want the app to be good, so that I can do my job."*

*Required.* Assess it against INVEST; rewrite it, with acceptance criteria, so that it passes.

*Working.* The draft fails Valuable (no specific benefit named), Estimable (nothing to size), Small
(unbounded scope — "good" could mean anything) and Testable (no way to check "good" objectively);
Independent and Negotiable are not assessable until the story says something concrete. A rewritten version:
*"As a site engineer, I want to attach a geotagged photo to a punch-list item from my phone while offline, so
that the item can be verified once I regain connectivity."* Acceptance criteria, in a Given/When/Then form
common in agile practice (not a Scrum Guide requirement, simply a widely used convention for stating
testable conditions): *Given* the app has no network connection, *When* a site engineer attaches a photo to
a punch-list item, *Then* the photo and its geotag are queued locally and sync automatically, without data
loss, once connectivity is restored.

*Answer.* The rewritten story is Independent (does not depend on other unstated stories), Negotiable (does
not prescribe the sync mechanism), Valuable (verification without a return site visit), Estimable (a
concrete, single capability — sized at 8 points in 9.3.4's worked example), Small (fits comfortably within a
two-week Sprint) and Testable (the Given/When/Then criterion is checkable).

*Interpretation.* INVEST is a diagnostic, not a certificate — a story can satisfy all six letters and still
turn out, mid-Sprint, to hide an unexpected technical dependency; the checklist reduces that risk, it does
not eliminate it.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Writing acceptance criteria so loosely ("works properly offline") that "Done" becomes a subjective
  argument at Sprint Review rather than an objective check.
- Conflating acceptance criteria (item-specific) with the Definition of Done (Increment-wide) — a story can
  meet its own acceptance criteria and still fail the Definition of Done (e.g., no automated test written),
  and both must hold for the item to count toward velocity (9.3.5).
- Writing a story so large it cannot fail Estimable outright but produces a wildly uncertain estimate — the
  Small criterion exists precisely to keep estimates (9.3.3–9.3.4) meaningful.

**AI in this topic.** A GenAI assistant can draft a first-pass user story and candidate acceptance criteria
from a short feature description or a stakeholder's notes, and can check a drafted story against the INVEST
checklist mechanically (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.5). It cannot judge whether the story is genuinely
*Valuable* to the actual user population — that judgement requires context (site conditions, user research,
commercial priority) an AI assistant does not have visibility into; the Product Owner reviews and owns every
story before it enters the Sprint Backlog.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.2 (Definition of Done, Sprint Backlog); KA 9.3.1 (refining a story into
Sprint-ready form); KA 9.3.4 (estimating the story sized in this topic's example).

### 9.3.3 Sizing units: story points vs ideal days vs #NoEstimates

**Definition & purpose.** Before a team can plan a Sprint or forecast a release, every backlog item needs a
size. Three distinct conventions exist for stating that size, and confusing them is one of the most common
errors a controls professional encounters when reading agile reporting.

- **Story points** — an abstract, unitless, *relative* measure combining size, complexity and uncertainty,
  calibrated only against other stories the same team has sized, never against a clock.
- **Ideal days** — a time-based unit: the number of days a story would take *if worked without interruption,
  meetings or context-switching*. Unlike points, an ideal day retains an implicit link to calendar time.
- **#NoEstimates** — a minority school of thought within the agile community that questions estimating
  altogether, favouring very small, similarly sized stories tracked by **throughput** (items completed per
  unit time, cross-reference KA 9.4) rather than by a size figure at all. It is included here at awareness
  level only — a controls professional should recognise the term and its rationale, not necessarily
  recommend it.

**The underlying principle.** No formula converts between these three cleanly, and that is deliberate.
Story points exist specifically to *break* the link between an estimate and a clock: because points are
relative ("this story is about as big as that one we called a 5"), the same team can re-use its own
historical velocity (9.3.5) as an empirical, self-calibrating conversion factor, without the team ever
having stated, or being held to, an hours-based commitment on an individual item. Ideal days look similar to
points on the surface (both are given as small numbers per story) but, because they are still explicitly a
time unit, they invite exactly the comparison points are designed to avoid — "3 ideal days late" reads as a
schedule slip in a way "3 points" does not, even though both statements carry the same underlying
uncertainty. No numerical formula attaches to distinguishing the three; the operational check is simply
asking what unit a number is stated in before comparing it to anything.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Treating story points as disguised hours** — converting "1 point = X hours" as a planning input (rather
  than observing, after the fact, how many hours a point *turned out* to represent) reintroduces the
  time-estimation pressure points exist to remove, and produces the same optimism bias hourly estimates are
  prone to. Any hours-per-point figure is a retrospective observation, never a forward planning conversion.
- Mixing ideal days and story points within the same backlog, so that velocity (9.3.5) is computed over a
  set of numbers that do not share a consistent unit.
- Adopting #NoEstimates piecemeal — tracking throughput *in addition to* points without changing how
  Sprint Planning or release forecasting actually uses the numbers achieves neither benefit cleanly.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based estimation-assistance tools can suggest a story-point value by pattern-
matching a new story's description against a team's own historically sized and completed stories
(cross-reference KA 13.5). This can usefully anchor a discussion, but planning poker's real value (9.3.4) is
the shared understanding the *conversation* produces, not only the number it lands on — an AI-suggested
point value skips that conversation and should be treated as one data point brought into it, not a
replacement for it.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.3.4 (how a relative point value is actually reached); KA 9.3.5 (velocity as the
empirical points-to-outcomes conversion); KA 9.4 (throughput and cycle time, the #NoEstimates alternative,
in full).

### 9.3.4 Relative estimation techniques: planning poker, t-shirt sizing and affinity estimating

**Definition & purpose.** These are the practical techniques teams use to arrive at a story-point value.
None is mandated by the Scrum Guide, which is silent on estimation method; all three are widely used agile/
Extreme-Programming-derived practices layered on top of Scrum.

- **Planning poker** — each estimator privately selects a card from a set of allowed values (commonly a
  modified Fibonacci-like sequence, e.g. 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20, 40, 100, chosen because the growing gaps
  force a genuine size decision rather than false precision), then all reveal simultaneously. Where
  estimates diverge, the highest and lowest estimators explain their reasoning and the group re-votes,
  converging on a consensus value.
- **T-shirt sizing** — items are bucketed into a small number of coarse size categories (XS/S/M/L/XL), useful
  for rapid, early-stage sizing of a large, still-coarse backlog before individual points are assigned.
- **Affinity estimating** — a large batch of items is sorted, without discussion, into relative-size
  clusters simply by placing each new item next to items it feels similarly sized to; only afterwards are
  point values attached to each cluster. It trades planning poker's precision for speed across a large batch.

**The underlying principle.** All three rely on the same cognitive basis: people are measurably better, and
faster, at *comparative* judgements ("is this bigger or smaller than that?") than at *absolute* judgements
("how many hours is this?"). Planning poker's simultaneous reveal specifically guards against **anchoring
bias** — if the most senior or most vocal estimator spoke first, subsequent estimators would unconsciously
converge toward that number rather than forming an independent judgement; revealing together removes that
distortion. The operative test in every technique is comparative, not computed: *is this story bigger,
smaller, or the same size as the reference story the team already agreed is a 3?*

**Example 9.3.4 — Planning poker on a Field App story.**

*Scenario.* The Field App's three Developers estimate the offline-sync story from Example 9.3.2 (attach a
geotagged photo offline; queue and sync automatically on reconnection). The team's agreed scale is
1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20.

*Required.* Show one round of planning poker reaching consensus.

*Working.* Round 1, simultaneous reveal: Developer A = 8 (has built offline queues before, sees the conflict-
resolution edge case as the main risk); Developer B = 13 (unfamiliar with the local sync library, weights the
uncertainty higher); Developer C = 5 (focuses on the happy path, has not yet considered the conflict case).
The highest (B, 13) and lowest (C, 5) explain their reasoning: B raises the conflict-resolution edge case
(two devices editing the same item offline); C had not considered it and revises upward. Round 2, simultaneous
reveal: A = 8, B = 8, C = 8 — consensus.

*Answer.* The story is sized at **8 points**.

*Interpretation.* The number itself (8) is less important than what produced it: the group surfaced a real
technical risk (the conflict-resolution edge case) during estimation, before any code was written — the
conversation is planning poker's primary value, the point value its by-product.

**Mini-case — affinity estimating a 30-item backlog batch.** Ahead of a quarterly planning session, the Field
App's Product Owner and Developers affinity-estimate 30 newly refined backlog items in under 30 minutes:
each item is silently placed on a wall relative to items already placed, forming five visible clusters. Point
values are attached to the clusters only once sorting settles: the smallest cluster (7 items) is called 2;
the next (11 items) is called 5; the next (8 items) is called 8; the next (3 items) is called 13; the largest
(1 item) is called 20 and flagged for splitting before it can enter a Sprint (it fails INVEST's Small
criterion, 9.3.2). Affinity estimating a batch this size via planning poker's full round-and-reveal process
for every item individually would have taken several hours; the trade-off is coarser precision per item,
acceptable this far from the items actually being worked on (9.3.1's DEEP principle).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Allowing a senior team member to speak, or visibly react, before the simultaneous reveal — this defeats
  the anchoring-bias protection that is planning poker's entire mechanism.
- Debating every item to the same level of depth regardless of size — spending as long estimating a
  trivial 1-point item as a genuinely uncertain 13-point item wastes the team's limited refinement time.
- Using t-shirt sizes or affinity clusters as the final Sprint Backlog unit — both are coarse-sizing tools
  for backlog-level triage; items should still be pointed (or re-estimated with planning poker) once they
  are near enough to Sprint Planning that precision matters (9.3.1).

**AI in this topic.** An AI assistant, trained on a team's own historical estimation data, can flag when a
new story's description resembles a previously mis-estimated item, or suggest a starting cluster for affinity
estimating a large batch (cross-reference KA 13.5). It cannot participate in the shared technical-risk
discussion that Example 9.3.4 shows planning poker actually surfaces — that discussion, and the resulting
number, remain the Developers' own judgement.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.3.3 (why points are relative, not time-based); KA 9.3.1 (DEEP and when coarse
sizing is appropriate); KA 9.2 (the Developers as the accountable estimators).

### 9.3.5 Velocity: computing it, using it, and why it must never become a target

**Definition & purpose.** **Velocity** is defined, per the style spine, as **story points completed per
sprint** — specifically, points for items that meet the team's Definition of Done (KA 9.2) by the end of the
Sprint. It is the empirical bridge between the abstract, relative points of 9.3.3–9.3.4 and a real forecast
of time and cost: because velocity is measured, not estimated, it converts "how big is the backlog" into
"how long will it take," without ever assigning an hours value to a point.

**Formula.**

`Velocity (Sprint n) = story points accepted as "Done" in Sprint n` (points/sprint)

`Average velocity (over N sprints) = (Σ points delivered across the N sprints) / N` (points/sprint)

`Rolling velocity (last k sprints) = (Σ points delivered in the most recent k sprints) / k` (points/sprint)

**Example 9.3.5 — Computing the Field App's velocity across five sprints.**

*Scenario.* Extending KA 8.6.4's Sprint 1–3 figures (16, 19, 21 points, reused exactly), the Field App
delivers Sprint 4 = 24 points and Sprint 5 = 15 points.

*Required.* (a) The cumulative points delivered and per-sprint velocity after each sprint; (b) the five-
sprint average velocity; (c) a 3-sprint rolling average at each point it can be computed, and what it shows.

*Working.*

| Sprint | Points delivered (velocity) | Cumulative points |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 16 | 16 |
| 2 | 19 | 35 |
| 3 | 21 | 56 |
| 4 | 24 | 80 |
| 5 | 15 | 95 |

(b) `Average velocity = (16+19+21+24+15)/5 = 95/5 = 19.0 points/sprint`.

(c) `Rolling(Sprints 1–3) = (16+19+21)/3 = 56/3 = 18.7 points/sprint`; `Rolling(Sprints 2–4) =
(19+21+24)/3 = 64/3 = 21.3 points/sprint`; `Rolling(Sprints 3–5) = (21+24+15)/3 = 60/3 = 20.0 points/sprint`.

*Answer.* Cumulative points to Sprint 5 = **95**; five-sprint average velocity = **19.0 points/sprint**; the
3-sprint rolling average moves 18.7 → 21.3 → 20.0, tracking close to the whole-history average throughout.

*Interpretation.* Sprint 5's single-sprint figure (15) is the lowest of the five and, read alone, might
prompt concern; the rolling average (20.0, still above the five-sprint average of 19.0) shows the team's
underlying pace has not actually slipped — a single sprint is noisy and should not be read as a trend on its
own (developed further in the pitfalls below). **Using velocity.** The team's Sprint Planning event (KA 9.2)
uses this same average or rolling figure as its primary capacity input: with recent velocity around 19–20
points/sprint, the Developers should be cautious about pulling materially more than that into Sprint 6's
Sprint Backlog, adjusted only for known one-off factors (e.g., a public holiday falling inside the Sprint)
that apply to that Sprint specifically.

**Mini-case — why velocity cannot be compared across teams.** Meridian's PMO, reviewing three small
in-house software teams including the Field App's, notices Team X averages 30 points/sprint against the
Field App's 19.0 and starts to ask whether the Field App team is under-performing. It is not a valid
comparison: each team calibrated its own point scale independently during its own planning-poker history
(9.3.4) — Team X's "30" and the Field App's "19" are not drawn from the same ruler. The only sound
comparison is a team's velocity against *its own* history over time, exactly as computed above.

**Fig 9.3.5 — The Field App's velocity chart, Sprints 1–5.**
Chart type: bar chart, Sprint 1–5 on the horizontal axis, story points delivered (0–30) on the vertical axis;
one bar per sprint in brand blue `#1D4ED8`; an overlaid line series in a lighter blue tint plotting the
3-sprint rolling average, starting at Sprint 3; a dashed horizontal reference line at the five-sprint average
(19.0).

*Underlying data:* the Sprint 1–5 points-delivered table above; rolling averages 18.7 (Sprint 3), 21.3
(Sprint 4), 20.0 (Sprint 5); average-velocity reference line at 19.0.

**Animation 9.3.5 — "Bars rise, the average line steadies" [digital-only].** Frame 1: Sprint 1's bar rises
alone. Frames 2–5: each further sprint's bar rises in turn, its value called out. Frame 4 onward: the rolling-
average line traces across the top of the bars, visibly less jumpy than the bars themselves. Frame 6: the
dashed 19.0 reference line fades in across the whole chart. Narration cue: "One sprint tells you almost
nothing. Five sprints, read as a trend, tell you a great deal."

**Common pitfalls — velocity as a target.**
- **Turning velocity into a productivity target.** The moment a team is told to "hit 22 points next sprint,"
  the number stops measuring delivered value and starts measuring the team's ability to inflate point
  estimates — a direct instance of **Goodhart's Law** ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a
  good measure"). Velocity is a *planning input*, derived from past outcomes; it is not a *performance
  target* to be set in advance and chased.
- Comparing velocity across teams (the mini-case above) or using it as an individual's performance metric —
  velocity is a team-level, self-calibrated planning figure, never an individual productivity measure.
- Reacting to a single low sprint (like Sprint 5 here) as a trend break without checking the rolling average
  or investigating a specific cause (holidays, an unplanned production incident) first.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based dashboards can compute rolling velocity automatically from a sprint-tracking
tool (Jira-class systems and similar) and flag statistically unusual swings for a human to investigate
(cross-reference KA 13.5). Such tools must never be configured to feed velocity directly into individual
performance ratings or team rankings — doing so manufactures exactly the Goodhart's-Law dysfunction above,
and no governance policy should permit an automated pipeline from velocity into compensation or appraisal
decisions without a professional's review of what is actually driving the number.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.2 (Sprint Planning, Definition of Done); KA 9.3.8 (using velocity to forecast a
release); KA 9.3.9 (which metrics a controls professional should rely on); KA 9.5 (AgileEVM's direct reuse of
this same points-and-run-rate arithmetic); KA 8.6.4 (the inverted iron triangle this metric serves).

### 9.3.6 Burndown charts: reading in-sprint (and release) progress

**Definition & purpose.** A **burndown chart** plots *remaining* work against time, trending toward zero as
work is completed. A **sprint burndown** tracks the Sprint Backlog's remaining points (or, in some teams,
remaining task-hours) day by day across a single Sprint; a **release burndown** does the same across a
sequence of Sprints against the whole release scope. Its purpose is to make in-progress trend visible daily,
rather than waiting for the Sprint Review to discover whether the Sprint Goal will be met.

**Formula.**

`Remaining work (day d) = Sprint backlog (points) − Σ points completed through day d`

`Ideal daily burn rate = Sprint backlog (points) / number of working days in the Sprint` (points/day)

**Example 9.3.6 — Reading the Field App's Sprint 5 burndown.**

*Scenario.* Sprint 5's Sprint Backlog totals 15 points (matching the 15 points actually delivered, per
9.3.5) across a 10-working-day Sprint. Stories are completed in bursts, not evenly: 5 points complete by Day
4, none complete between Day 4 and Day 6, 6 points complete by Day 8, and the final 4 points complete by Day
10.

*Required.* (a) The ideal daily burn rate; (b) a table of actual remaining points at Days 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and
10; (c) whether the completed-points sequence reconciles with the Sprint's 15-point total.

*Working.* (a) `Ideal burn rate = 15/10 = 1.5 points/day`, so the ideal remaining line runs
`15 − 1.5d` at day `d`: 15, 12, 9, 6, 3, 0 at days 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10.

(b)

| Day | Points completed since previous checkpoint | Cumulative completed | Actual remaining | Ideal remaining |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | — | 0 | 15 | 15 |
| 2 | 0 | 0 | 15 | 12 |
| 4 | 5 | 5 | 10 | 9 |
| 6 | 0 | 5 | 10 | 6 |
| 8 | 6 | 11 | 4 | 3 |
| 10 | 4 | 15 | 0 | 0 |

(c) `0 + 5 + 0 + 6 + 4 = 15` — reconciles exactly with Sprint 5's total delivered points (9.3.5).

*Answer.* The chart's actual line sits **above** the ideal line for most of the Sprint (flat through Days
0–2 and 4–6) and only reaches zero exactly on Day 10.

*Interpretation.* The flat stretches do not necessarily mean no work was happening — testing, integration
and code review can occupy days without a story crossing the Definition of Done — but a burndown line that
stays flat for an extended period, with no acceleration visible by roughly the Sprint's midpoint, is exactly
the signal that should prompt the Scrum Master to ask whether the Sprint Goal is genuinely still achievable,
well before the Sprint Review makes the answer unavoidable.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Panicking at a flat early-Sprint burndown — a "staircase" pattern (flat, then a step down) is normal for
  many teams and does not by itself indicate trouble; a flat line that persists past the Sprint's midpoint
  is the more meaningful signal.
- Re-estimating a story's remaining points mid-Sprint to make the chart look better — the chart should
  reflect completed-or-not status against the Definition of Done, not a running renegotiation of size.
- Reading a burndown chart alone as a scope-integrity check — it cannot show *why* the line is not falling
  as fast as planned (blocked work vs. scope added mid-Sprint); a burnup chart is needed for that
  distinction (9.3.7).

**AI in this topic.** Sprint-tracking tools generate burndown charts automatically and in real time from
task-status updates, and some AI-based dashboard features can flag when a chart's trajectory statistically
diverges from the team's own historical burndown pattern (cross-reference KA 13.5) — useful as an early
prompt. Automated generation does not remove the need for a human to check *why* the line looks the way it
does; a chart that "looks fine" mechanically can still hide, for example, work that was marked done without
meeting the Definition of Done.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.2 (the Sprint Backlog and Definition of Done a burndown tracks against); KA 9.3.5
(velocity, the sprint-over-sprint counterpart to this within-sprint chart); KA 9.3.7 (burnup, which shows
what a burndown cannot).

### 9.3.7 Burnup charts and cumulative flow: separating "no progress" from "growing scope"

**Definition & purpose.** A **burnup chart** plots cumulative work *completed* against time, alongside a
second series showing the *total scope* (the "ceiling") at that point in time. Unlike a burndown chart's
single falling line, a burnup chart's two lines let a reader distinguish two situations a burndown alone
cannot: work stalling (the completed line flattens while the ceiling stays put) versus scope growing (the
ceiling line itself rises, even while the completed line keeps climbing at its usual rate).

**Formula.** No single formula; a burnup chart is two running totals plotted together:

`Cumulative completed (Sprint n) = Σ points delivered, Sprints 1…n`

`Scope ceiling (Sprint n) = total backlog points currently in scope as of Sprint n` (constant only if no
items have been added to or removed from the backlog since the release began)

**Mini-case — what a scope increase would look like (illustrative variant, not part of the base-case
forecast below).** Suppose, unlike the Field App's actual 200-point backlog carried through this KA, a
client requested an additional 20-point feature partway through delivery. On a burnup chart this appears as
a step **up** in the ceiling line at the sprint the request was accepted — visibly distinguishable from a
flattening of the completed line, which would instead signal the team was not delivering. A burndown chart
of the same event would only show the "days remaining to zero" estimate getting worse, with no way to tell,
from the chart alone, whether that was because the team slowed down or because the target moved. This is
exactly the distinction KA 8.6.4 flags as a classical-EVM pitfall carried into agile: a re-prioritised or
grown backlog is not automatically an adverse variance, and a burnup chart's second line is what makes that
visible at a glance.

**Cumulative flow (brief — full treatment in KA 9.4).** A **cumulative flow diagram (CFD)** is a Kanban-
native relative of the burnup chart: instead of one "completed" line, it stacks bands for each workflow state
(e.g. To Do, In Progress, In Review, Done), each band's cumulative count plotted over time. A widening band
signals work piling up in that state (a bottleneck); a band's horizontal thickness at any point shows
work-in-progress in that state at that moment. KA 9.4 develops the CFD, work-in-progress limits and the flow
metrics (cycle time, throughput) that go with it in full; here it is enough to recognise a CFD as a burnup
chart's multi-state cousin, useful once a team's flow — rather than only its Sprint-boundary output — is the
question being asked.

**Fig 9.3.7 — Release burnup chart: the Field App, Sprints 1–5 actual, ceiling constant at 200 points.**
Chart type: line chart, cumulative story points (0–220) on the vertical axis, Sprint 1–12 on the horizontal
axis. A solid brand-blue `#1D4ED8` line rises through the five actual data points (16, 35, 56, 80, 95); a
flat dashed line at 200 marks the scope ceiling across the whole horizontal range (no scope change in this
base case). From Sprint 5, three thinner dotted forecast lines fan out and upward at gradients of 24, 19.0
and 15 points/sprint respectively (9.3.8), each meeting the 200-point ceiling at its own forecast sprint.

*Underlying data:* the cumulative-points table from Example 9.3.5 (16, 35, 56, 80, 95); the constant 200-
point ceiling; the three forecast trend lines detailed fully in Example 9.3.8.

**Animation 9.3.7 — "One line climbs, a second line waits" [digital-only].** Frame 1: the 200-point ceiling
line draws in first, flat and dashed. Frame 2: the completed-points line rises through Sprints 1–5, point by

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reading a flattening burnup completed-line as scope trouble when it is actually a delivery slowdown, or
  vice versa — the two lines must be read together, not the completed line alone.
- Omitting the ceiling line entirely and calling the result a "burnup chart" — without the scope series, the
  chart degenerates into a cumulative version of a burndown and loses its distinguishing value.
- Treating a CFD's widening band as automatically bad — some widening (a healthy buffer ahead of a
  downstream bottleneck) is normal; KA 9.4 develops the diagnostic reading in full.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based dashboard tools can auto-generate burnup charts and flag, in near real time,
whenever the scope-ceiling line moves — a genuinely useful early-warning feature, since a manual monthly
roll-up (KA 8.6.3) might otherwise miss a scope change until well after it happened (cross-reference KA
13.5). Such a flag tells a controls professional *that* scope changed; it cannot judge *whether* the change
was a legitimate re-prioritisation or the "agile-in-name-only"-adjacent pitfall of undisciplined scope creep
(KA 8.6.3) — that judgement, and any resulting client conversation, remains the professional's.

**Cross-references.** KA 9.3.6 (burndown, the single-line chart this topic contrasts with); KA 9.3.8

### 9.3.8 Forecasting a release from velocity: sprint and date ranges

**Definition & purpose.** Once a team has a measured velocity, that same figure answers the sponsor's core
question — *how many more sprints, and how much more will it cost, to finish the backlog?* — as a genuine
forecast rather than a guess. Because a single average velocity carries uncertainty, a professional
forecast is stated as a **range** (optimistic/likely/pessimistic), not a single number, exactly as KA 9.5's
AgileEVM and KA 6.3's classical EAC family both insist on stating assumptions rather than a false-precision
point estimate.

**Formula.**

`Sprints remaining = ⌈ Remaining backlog (points) / Velocity (points/sprint) ⌉` (rounded up — a fractional
sprint cannot be delivered)

`Forecast completion date = current sprint's end date + (Sprints remaining × sprint length)`

`Forecast total cost = (Sprints elapsed + Sprints remaining) × cost per sprint`

**Example 9.3.8 — Forecasting the Field App's release from a velocity range, at the Sprint-5 data date.**

*Scenario.* Continuing Example 9.3.5: 95 points delivered across Sprints 1–5 against the 200-point backlog;
observed sprint velocities across that history range from a low of 15 (Sprint 5) to a high of 24 (Sprint 4),
with a five-sprint average of 19.0. Each sprint is two weeks, costs $15,000 (SAR 56,250), and Sprint 5 ends
15 March 2026.

*Required.* (a) The remaining backlog; (b) sprints remaining under an optimistic (best-observed velocity),
likely (average velocity) and pessimistic (worst-observed velocity) scenario; (c) the forecast completion
date and total cost under each scenario.

*Working.*

(a) `Remaining backlog = 200 − 95 = 105 points`.

(b)

| Scenario | Velocity used (points/sprint) | Sprints remaining (105 / velocity, rounded up) |
|---|---|---|
| Optimistic | 24 (best sprint observed) | `105/24 = 4.375 → 5 sprints` |
| Likely | 19.0 (five-sprint average) | `105/19.0 = 5.53 → 6 sprints` |
| Pessimistic | 15 (worst sprint observed) | `105/15 = 7.00 → 7 sprints` |

(c)

| Scenario | Total sprints (5 elapsed + remaining) | Completion date (Sprint 5 ends 15 March 2026 + n × 2 weeks) | Total cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Optimistic | `5 + 5 = 10` | `15 Mar 2026 + 10 weeks = 24 May 2026` | `10 × 15,000 = $150,000` (SAR 562,500) |
| Likely | `5 + 6 = 11` | `15 Mar 2026 + 12 weeks = 7 June 2026` | `11 × 15,000 = $165,000` (SAR 618,750) |
| Pessimistic | `5 + 7 = 12` | `15 Mar 2026 + 14 weeks = 21 June 2026` | `12 × 15,000 = $180,000` (SAR 675,000) |

*Answer.* The Field App is forecast to complete its 200-point backlog in **10 to 12 sprints total (5 to 7
more from Sprint 5)**, between **24 May 2026 and 21 June 2026**, at a total cost of **$150,000 to $180,000
(SAR 562,500 to 675,000)**, with the likely case landing at **11 sprints, 7 June 2026, $165,000
(SAR 618,750)**.

*Interpretation.* This range-based, Sprint-5 forecast both **reproduces and sharpens** KA 8.6.4's earlier,
single-point Sprint-3 forecast (11 sprints, $165,000): the central estimate has not moved even though two
further sprints of evidence — including Sprint 5's comparatively weak 15-point outcome — have gone into it,
and it is now bounded by an evidenced $30,000 range rather than reported as one number with no stated
uncertainty. This is precisely why a controls professional should report a *range* whenever one is
available: the likely figure alone would have looked identical at Sprint 3 and Sprint 5 by coincidence, but
only the Sprint-5 figure comes with a defensible confidence band around it. **Using the range, not the
midpoint, to plan around.** A sponsor asking "will it be done by 31 May 2026?" should be told the honest
answer — no, not under the likely or pessimistic case, only under the optimistic one — rather than being
given the single 7 June "likely" date stripped of the range that shows how close that date is to the
sponsor's target.

**Fig 9.3.8 — Forecast range: sprints remaining and cost under three velocity scenarios.**
Chart type: horizontal range ("tornado") bar chart, three rows (Optimistic, Likely, Pessimistic), each row a
bar running from 5 elapsed sprints to its own total-sprints endpoint (10, 11, 12), in brand blue `#1D4ED8`
with a lighter tint for the elapsed portion common to all three; a secondary axis or data label on each bar
shows total cost ($150,000 / $165,000 / $180,000) and completion date (24 May / 7 June / 21 June 2026).

*Underlying data:* the scenario table in part (c) above.

**Animation 9.3.8 — "Three scenarios, one shared past" [digital-only].** Frame 1: a single bar segment for
"Sprints 1–5 (elapsed)" appears, identical across all three rows. Frame 2: the Optimistic row's remaining

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting only the "likely" figure to a sponsor and dropping the range — this restates the false-precision
  problem the range exists to avoid, and leaves the sponsor unaware of genuine downside risk.
- Using the single best-ever sprint (here, 24 points) as the *planning* assumption rather than the
  *optimistic bound* — this is the agile-forecasting equivalent of budgeting to a best-case cost estimate
  (KA 3.2) and will read as an adverse variance almost every subsequent sprint.
- Failing to update the forecast as new sprints complete — a forecast computed once at Sprint 3 and never
  refreshed is stale by the time it matters; recomputing at every sprint (as this example does relative to
  KA 8.6.4) is what makes it a *forecast*, not a one-off estimate.

**AI in this topic.** AI/ML-based forecasting tools can go further than a manual three-point range, running
a probabilistic simulation over a team's full velocity history to produce a fuller distribution (e.g., "70%
confidence of finishing by early June") rather than three discrete scenarios — a genuine value-add some
modern PM-suite and agile-analytics platforms offer (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). The professional
still chooses which confidence level is appropriate to report to a given sponsor or contract, and remains
accountable for that forecast once reported — a probabilistic model widens the evidence base, it does not
transfer the accountability. **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

### 9.3.9 Which agile metrics a controls professional should — and should not — rely on

**Definition & purpose.** This closing topic consolidates a governance question that has run through every
topic above: agile delivery produces a rich set of numbers, and not every number that *can* be measured
*should* be used the way it is often, informally, put to use. A controls professional's job is to select
metrics that inform a decision or a forecast, and to actively resist metrics that look informative but
principally invite gaming.

**The should / should-not table.**

| Should rely on | Why | Should not rely on | Why not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Velocity trend (rolling average, several sprints) for capacity planning and release forecasting (9.3.5, 9.3.8) | Empirical, self-calibrating, team-specific | Velocity as a cross-team comparator | Points are not calibrated to a common scale across teams (9.3.5's mini-case) |
| Burndown/burnup trend for in-flight transparency (9.3.6–9.3.7) | Shows trajectory before the Sprint Review makes it unavoidable | Velocity as an individual performance or bonus metric | Invites point inflation (Goodhart's Law), not genuine output growth |
| The burnup scope-ceiling line, specifically, to distinguish slowdown from scope growth (9.3.7) | The one series that makes that distinction visible at all | Story points converted directly to cost or hours | Points were designed to avoid exactly this link (9.3.3) |
| Cycle time and throughput (KA 9.4) as flow-health indicators alongside velocity | Independent of point-scale calibration; comparable over time within one workflow | A single sprint's velocity, read in isolation, as a trend signal | One data point is noise; 9.3.5's rolling average exists precisely to smooth it |
| Definition of Done adherence rate, as a quality gate on what counts toward velocity | Prevents "done" from being redefined to protect the chart | Forecast midpoints reported without their range (9.3.8) | Strips out exactly the uncertainty information a sponsor needs |

**Mini-case — what happens when the "should not" column is ignored.** A programme manager overseeing three
small in-house teams, including the Field App's, begins ranking teams by raw velocity each quarter and
links the highest-ranked team lead's rating to that ranking. Within two quarters, the ranked teams' reported
average story-point size for functionally similar work has quietly risen — the same class of story that
was called a 5 a year earlier is now routinely called an 8 — while the teams' cycle time and throughput
(KA 9.4), and the volume of stakeholder-visible functionality actually shipped, have stayed essentially flat.
This is Goodhart's Law playing out exactly as 9.3.5 warns: the measure (velocity) became a target, and the
teams optimised for the number rather than for delivered value, without anyone needing to act in bad faith —
point estimates simply drift upward under sustained comparative pressure. The correction is not to abandon
velocity, but to stop using it as a ranking or reward metric and restore it to a planning input, cross-
checked against a metric harder to inflate, such as cycle time.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Adding more metrics rather than better-governed ones — a dashboard with ten agile metrics and no stated
  purpose for each is harder to misuse well than one with three metrics each tied to a specific decision.
- Applying classical EVM's CV/SV/CPI/SPI (Domain 6) directly to a variable-scope agile backlog without
  AgileEVM's adaptation (KA 9.5) — this is the single most consequential metric-selection error a controls
  professional can make on an adaptive scope, already flagged in KA 8.6.4 and developed fully in KA 9.5.
- Assuming a metric's governance risk is fixed once decided — a metric introduced innocently for planning
  (velocity) can be repurposed for performance management by a later manager unfamiliar with why it was
  never meant for that use; periodic review of *how* metrics are actually being used, not just which
  metrics exist, is part of the professional's ongoing responsibility (cross-reference KA 9.6, KA 13.6).

**AI in this topic.** AI-based analytics tools can scan reporting history for patterns consistent with
metric gaming — for example, flagging when average story-point size rises for a stable category of work
while cycle time and throughput do not correspondingly improve, exactly the pattern in the mini-case above
(cross-reference KA 13.5, KA 13.6). Such a flag is a valuable audit aid. It cannot itself decide
organisational policy on what agile metrics may be used for compensation, appraisal or team comparison —
that is a governance decision for organisational leadership, informed by, never delegated to, an AI-
generated flag. **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 9.3.5 (velocity, the most commonly misused metric); KA 9.3.7 (the burnup ceiling
line's specific diagnostic value); KA 9.4 (cycle time and throughput, in full); KA 9.5 (AgileEVM as the
governed way to reuse EVM-style rigour on an agile backlog); KA 9.6 (agile governance and assurance, in
full); KA 13.6 (AI governance, ethics, risk and assurance, in full).

### Key terms

- **Product Backlog** — the single, ordered list of everything known to be needed in the product; emergent,
  never complete.
- **Backlog refinement** — the ongoing activity of adding detail, estimates and order to backlog items.
- **DEEP** — a mnemonic (Detailed appropriately, Estimated, Emergent, Prioritised) describing a healthy
  Product Backlog's characteristics.
- **MoSCoW** — a prioritisation technique: Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have (this time).
- **WSJF (Weighted Shortest Job First)** — a SAFe-associated prioritisation technique, Cost of Delay ÷ Job
  Size (awareness level).
- **User story** — a short, structured requirement statement ("As a `<role>`, I want `<capability>`, so that
  `<benefit>`").
- **INVEST** — a checklist for a well-formed story: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small,
  Testable.
- **Acceptance criteria** — the specific, checkable conditions a single story must satisfy to be accepted as
  complete.
- **Story point** — an abstract, relative measure of size, complexity and uncertainty, calibrated only
  against a team's own other stories.
- **Ideal day** — a time-based sizing unit: the duration a story would take with zero interruption.
- **#NoEstimates** — a minority agile school of thought favouring throughput tracking over size estimation
  (awareness level).
- **Planning poker** — a simultaneous-reveal relative-estimation technique using a Fibonacci-like card scale.
- **T-shirt sizing / affinity estimating** — coarse, rapid relative-sizing techniques for triaging a large or
  early-stage backlog.
- **Velocity** — story points completed per sprint (per the style spine's canonical definition).
- **Rolling velocity** — a moving average of velocity over the most recent k sprints, used to smooth
  single-sprint noise.
- **Burndown chart** — a chart plotting remaining work against time, trending to zero.
- **Burnup chart** — a chart plotting cumulative completed work against time, alongside a total-scope
  ("ceiling") series.
- **Cumulative flow diagram (CFD)** — a Kanban-native, multi-state relative of the burnup chart (full
  treatment KA 9.4).
- **Goodhart's Law** — the principle that a measure, once made a target, ceases to reliably measure what it
  was designed to measure.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 9.3.1 [topic 9.3.2, level: recall]**
Which of the following acceptance criteria best satisfies INVEST's "Testable" requirement?
A. "The app should work well offline"
B. **"Given no network connection, when a photo is attached to a punch-list item, then it queues locally and
   syncs automatically once connectivity returns, with no data loss"** ✔
C. "The offline feature should be user-friendly"
D. "Offline support is a top priority for this release"
*Rationale:* B states an objectively checkable condition (Given/When/Then); A and C are subjective and
unverifiable; D states a priority, not a testable condition at all.

**MCQ 9.3.2 [topic 9.3.3, level: recall]**
Why are story points not simply "hours in disguise"?
A. Because 1 point is always defined as exactly one working day
B. **Because points are a relative, team-calibrated measure of size, complexity and uncertainty, converted
   to time only empirically, after the fact, via velocity** ✔
C. Because points are assigned by management, not the team
D. Because points apply only to bugs, not to user stories
*Rationale:* per 9.3.3, converting points to hours as a planning input reintroduces the estimation bias
points are designed to avoid; any hours-per-point relationship is observed after delivery, never set in
advance. A, C and D are unsupported by the definition.

**MCQ 9.3.3 [topic 9.3.5, level: application — numerical]**
A team's last five sprints delivered 16, 19, 21, 24 and 15 story points. What is its five-sprint average
velocity?
A. 18.7 points/sprint
B. **19.0 points/sprint** ✔
C. 24.0 points/sprint
D. 95.0 points/sprint
*Rationale:* `(16+19+21+24+15)/5 = 95/5 = 19.0`. A is the 3-sprint rolling average for Sprints 1–3 only, not
the 5-sprint average; C mistakes the best single sprint for the average; D reports the cumulative total
without dividing by the number of sprints.

**MCQ 9.3.4 [topic 9.3.8, level: application — numerical]**
A team has 105 story points remaining and its best-ever single-sprint velocity is 24 points/sprint. Using
this as the optimistic scenario, how many further sprints are forecast (rounded to a whole sprint)?
A. 4 sprints
B. **5 sprints** ✔
C. 4.375 sprints
D. 24 sprints
*Rationale:* `105/24 = 4.375`, rounded **up** to 5 sprints because a fractional sprint cannot be delivered
(9.3.8's formula). A rounds down incorrectly; C leaves the answer as a fraction instead of a deliverable
sprint count; D confuses the velocity figure itself with a sprint count.

**MCQ 9.3.5 [topic 9.3.5, level: analysis]**
A programme manager ranks three teams by raw velocity and ties the highest-ranked team lead's bonus to that
ranking. What is the most likely consequence, per Goodhart's Law?
A. Genuine, sustained productivity improvement across all three teams
B. **Story-point inflation for similar work, with no corresponding rise in cycle time or throughput** ✔
C. No effect, because story points are an objective unit of size
D. Improved cross-team benchmarking of true output
*Rationale:* as the 9.3.9 mini-case shows, making velocity a target typically produces point inflation, not
genuine output growth, because points are team-calibrated and easily re-anchored upward under pressure. A
and D assume a benefit velocity-as-target does not reliably deliver; C contradicts 9.3.3's definition of
points as relative, not objective.

**MCQ 9.3.6 [topic 9.3.7, level: application]**
A team's cumulative burnup "completed" line stays flat for two sprints while, in the same period, its scope
"ceiling" line rises by 15 points. What does this most likely indicate?
A. The team has stopped delivering any work
B. **New scope was added to the backlog (a re-prioritisation), which a burndown chart alone would not have
   distinguished from a delivery slowdown** ✔
C. A data-entry error that should be ignored
D. The sprint length has changed
*Rationale:* per 9.3.7, the ceiling line moving is specifically how a burnup chart shows scope change,
independent of whether the completed line is also flat; a burndown chart's single line could not make this
distinction. A over-interprets a flat completed line without checking the ceiling; C and D are unsupported
assumptions not indicated by the data described.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. In your own words, explain why velocity should never be used to compare two different teams, and connect
   your answer to how story points are calibrated (cross-reference 9.3.3–9.3.5).
2. A team's last four sprints delivered 14, 18, 22 and 26 story points against a 220-point backlog. Calculate
   the average velocity and forecast how many further sprints are needed to complete the backlog.
3. Distinguish a burndown chart from a burnup chart, and state one specific situation in which a burnup chart
   reveals something a burndown chart would hide (cross-reference 9.3.6–9.3.7).
4. Name one agile metric a controls professional should rely on for progress reporting, and one that should
   never be used as a performance target, explaining why in each case (cross-reference 9.3.9).

**Answers**

1. Velocity is measured in story points, and story points are a *relative* unit calibrated only against a
   given team's own reference stories during its own planning-poker (or similar) history — two teams' point
   scales are not drawn from the same ruler. A team reporting "30 points/sprint" may be doing objectively
   less work than a team reporting "15 points/sprint" if the first team's "1 point" simply describes a
   larger unit of work than the second team's. The only sound comparison is a team's velocity against its
   own history over time (9.3.5).
2. `Average velocity = (14+18+22+26)/4 = 80/4 = 20.0 points/sprint`. `Remaining backlog = 220 − 80 = 140
   points`. `Sprints needed = 140/20.0 = 7.0 → 7 sprints` (no rounding required — the division is exact).
3. A burndown chart plots *remaining* work trending to zero on a single line; a burnup chart plots
   *cumulative completed* work against a separate *scope ceiling* line. A burnup chart reveals a scope
   increase mid-release (the ceiling line stepping up) as visibly distinct from a delivery slowdown (the
   completed line flattening) — a burndown chart's single line cannot make that distinction; a worsening
   burndown trend looks identical whether the cause is the team slowing down or the target scope growing.
4. Should rely on: velocity's *rolling average* (or the burnup scope-ceiling line) for capacity planning and
   release forecasting — both are empirical, team-specific, and not easily gamed by a single sprint's
   noise. Should never be a target: raw, single-sprint velocity used as an individual or cross-team
   performance metric — per Goodhart's Law, making it a target invites point inflation rather than genuine
   output growth (9.3.5, 9.3.9's mini-case).
