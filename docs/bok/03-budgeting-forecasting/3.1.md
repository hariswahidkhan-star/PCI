# Domain 3 — Budgeting & Forecasting

## KA 3.1 — Budgeting fundamentals

Every cost report, variance analysis and earned-value calculation a project controls professional produces
is measured against one reference point: the budget. This Knowledge Area builds that reference point from
first principles — what a budget is and why it exists, the two directions from which it can be built
(top-down and bottom-up), a discipline-focused alternative (zero-based budgeting), how a base estimate
becomes a formally approved cost baseline, and how contingency reserve and management reserve protect that
baseline against known and unknown risk without silently inflating it. A single running example — the
**Northgate Water Treatment Upgrade** — carries numbers through the whole chapter so the budgeting
hierarchy is seen as one connected build-up rather than a set of disconnected percentages. Everything
downstream in Domain 3 depends on this KA: KA 3.2 develops the cost-estimating methods that produce the
base estimate; KA 3.3 spreads the approved cost baseline across the schedule to build Planned Value (PV);
KA 3.4 forecasts against it; KA 3.5 turns it into a cash-flow plan. It also supplies the Budget at
Completion (BAC) that Domain 6's earned value management is built on, and links forward to Domain 12's
quantitative risk analysis, which is one legitimate way of sizing the contingency introduced here.

### 3.1.1 The purpose of budgeting and the budgeting hierarchy

**Definition and purpose.** A **budget** is the authorised, quantified plan of expenditure for a project,
expressed in monetary terms and tied to a defined scope and schedule. Budgeting is the process — described
as part of Cost Management in PMI's PMBOK Guide (the "Determine Budget" process aggregates estimated costs
of individual work packages into an authorised cost baseline) and as a core step in AACE International's
Total Cost Management (TCM) Framework, following cost estimating and preceding cost control — of turning a
cost **estimate** (a prediction, KA 3.2) into a **budget** (an authorisation) and then a **baseline** (a
fixed reference point for measuring performance). A budget serves four distinct purposes for a project
controls professional, and confusing them is a common source of poor practice:

1. **Authorisation** — the budget is the sanctioned spending limit management has approved; work should not
   be committed beyond it without formal change control (cross-reference KA 5.4, KA 8.4).
2. **Control** — actual costs (AC) are tracked against the budget throughout execution (KA 5.2).
3. **Performance measurement** — the time-phased budget becomes Planned Value (PV/BCWS), the yardstick
   against which Earned Value is compared (cross-reference KA 3.3, KA 6.1).
4. **Funding and cash-flow planning** — the budget, spread over time, tells the organisation or client how
   much funding must be available, and when (cross-reference KA 3.5).

**The underlying principle — the budgeting hierarchy.** A single "budget" figure is rarely just one number.
A defensible project budget is built in layers, each with a distinct purpose and a distinct owner:

- The **base estimate** — the cost of the defined scope of work, with no allowance for risk (KA 3.2).
- **+ Contingency reserve** — an allowance for identified risks *within* the defined scope (§3.1.6).
- **= Cost baseline** — the approved, time-phased budget against which performance is measured; this is
  the **Budget at Completion (BAC)** used identically throughout this BoK, including Domain 6.
- **+ Management reserve** — an allowance for unforeseen events *outside* the defined scope (§3.1.6).
- **= Total project budget** — the full amount authorised and funded for the project.

**Formula.**

`Cost baseline (BAC) = Base estimate + Contingency reserve`
`Total project budget = Cost baseline + Management reserve = Base estimate + Contingency reserve + Management reserve`

where all terms are expressed in the same currency (USD, with an SAR illustration where useful) and to the
same scope and date.

**Example 3.1.1 — the budgeting hierarchy, previewed.**

*Scenario.* The Northgate Water Treatment Upgrade — a 50 megalitres-per-day (MLD) capacity upgrade — has a
base estimate of $7,500,000, a contingency reserve of $750,000, and a management reserve of $412,500
(each fully derived from first principles in §3.1.5–3.1.6).

*Required.* The cost baseline (BAC) and the total project budget.

*Working.*
`Cost baseline = 7,500,000 + 750,000 = 8,250,000`
`Total project budget = 8,250,000 + 412,500 = 8,662,500`

*Answer.* **Cost baseline (BAC) = $8,250,000; Total project budget = $8,662,500.**

*Interpretation.* Three numbers, three different governance meanings: $7,500,000 is what the defined scope
is estimated to cost; $8,250,000 is what the project is authorised to be measured against for performance;
$8,662,500 is the full amount the organisation has set aside. A professional who reports "the budget" without
saying which of these three figures they mean is a common, avoidable source of confusion in project
reporting.

**Mini-case — why the hierarchy matters in practice.** Two contractors each win a $600,000 work package
with a genuine, identified risk of adverse ground conditions. Contractor A budgets a single, undifferentiated
figure of $660,000 ("$600,000 plus a bit for safety") with no record of what the extra $60,000 is for or who
may authorise its use. Contractor B budgets a base estimate of $600,000 plus an explicit $60,000 contingency
reserve, recorded against the specific ground-conditions risk and delegated to the site manager to draw down
if — and only if — that risk occurs. When the ground conditions turn out as expected (no adverse conditions),
Contractor A's team, having no formal record of what the $60,000 was for, is under quiet pressure to "find a
use" for it before year-end, and often does — a phenomenon informally called **contingency creep** or
**gold-plating**. Contractor B's controls team can demonstrate, with the risk register as evidence, that the
$60,000 was never triggered and can be released back to the client or reallocated by formal decision. The
difference is not the size of the reserve — it is the discipline of the hierarchy.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating "the budget" as one undifferentiated number, which makes it impossible to
answer "is that cost overrun eating into contingency, or has it already reached management reserve?" (2)
Setting a single "safety margin" percentage without recording what specific risks it responds to — this is
the root cause of contingency creep in the mini-case above. (3) Confusing the **estimate** (a prediction, KA
3.2) with the **budget** (an authorisation) — an estimate can be revised freely as more information
emerges; a budget can only change through formal, approved change control (KA 5.4).

**AI in this topic.** Budget-drafting assistants integrated into ERP and project-controls platforms can
auto-populate a proposed hierarchy from a cost estimate and a risk register, saving substantial drafting
time. They cannot decide the *right* level of contingency or management reserve for a specific project's
risk appetite — that remains a judgement made by the project controls professional and the sponsor,
informed by, but not delegated to, the tool. AI proposes a draft structure; the professional disposes by
approving the authorised figures.

### 3.1.2 Top-down budgeting

**Definition and purpose.** **Top-down budgeting** sets the overall project budget first — typically by
senior management, a sponsor or a portfolio/PMO function — using historical benchmarks, high-level
parametric relationships, or a percentage of a comparable reference project, and then allocates that total
downward to major categories or phases. It corresponds to the earliest, least-detailed cost-estimate
classes in AACE International's *Cost Estimate Classification System* (Recommended Practice 18R-97,
referenced in depth in KA 3.2) — typically a **Class 5** order-of-magnitude or **rough order of magnitude
(ROM)** estimate — produced before detailed design or a mature Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) exists.

**The underlying principle.** Top-down budgeting trades accuracy for speed: it is available very early,
when a sponsor needs a number for a business case or a portfolio decision, precisely because it does not
require the detailed scope definition that a bottom-up build (§3.1.3) needs. Its accuracy is bounded by how
representative the historical benchmark or analogous project really is — a parametric factor calibrated on
a different climate, labour market or technology base will systematically mislead.

**Formula.**

`Budget (top-down, parametric) = Driver quantity × Unit-cost benchmark`

where the *driver quantity* is a measurable size parameter of the new project (capacity, floor area, route
length) and the *unit-cost benchmark* is a $-per-unit rate drawn from completed, comparable projects.

**Example 3.1.2 — a parametric top-down budget for Northgate.**

*Scenario.* Before detailed design, the sponsor needs an early budget figure for a capital-approval
business case. The Northgate Water Treatment Upgrade is sized at 50 MLD of treatment capacity. Internal
benchmarking of three recently completed, comparable water-treatment upgrades gives an average unit-cost
benchmark of $170,000 per MLD of capacity.

*Required.* The top-down (ROM) budget.

*Working.* `Budget (top-down) = 50 MLD × $170,000/MLD = $8,500,000`

*Answer.* **Top-down (ROM) budget = $8,500,000.**

*Interpretation.* This figure is good enough to support a capital-approval decision, but — being a Class
5-type estimate — it carries a wide expected accuracy range (illustrative, see Fig 3.1.2) and must not be
mistaken for a definitive budget once detailed design begins.

**Mini-case — allocating the top-down total, and where it diverges from the eventual detail.** Before
detailed engineering starts, the PMO allocates the $8,500,000 ROM figure across major categories using
historical percentage splits from the same benchmark projects: Civil works 30%, Mechanical & process
equipment 40%, Electrical & instrumentation 14%, Project management & engineering 12%, and a built-in
contingency allowance of 4%.

| Category | % of ROM | Allocated ($) |
|---|---|---|
| Civil works | 30% | 2,550,000 |
| Mechanical & process equipment | 40% | 3,400,000 |
| Electrical & instrumentation | 14% | 1,190,000 |
| Project management & engineering | 12% | 1,020,000 |
| Contingency (built-in) | 4% | 340,000 |
| **Total** | **100%** | **8,500,000** |

Once detailed design matures and the bottom-up base estimate is built (§3.1.3), the same four categories
total $7,500,000 (excluding contingency, which §3.1.6 treats separately and rigorously) — $660,000 (8.8%)
lower than the $8,160,000 top-down allocation excluding its built-in contingency line. The gap is not spread
evenly: civil works alone is overstated by $150,000, mechanical by $250,000, electrical by $140,000, and
project management/engineering by $120,000 — summing to the full $660,000 difference. A professional
receiving both figures does not average them or default to either one; the divergence is investigated
category by category, and the top-down figure's historical percentage split is checked for whether it truly
reflects this project's technology and site conditions (§3.1.3 continues this reconciliation).

*Quick reference — top-down vs bottom-up:*

| | Top-down | Bottom-up (§3.1.3) |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Historical benchmark, parametric/ratio factor | Detailed, resource-loaded work-package estimates |
| Typically available | Very early (pre-design, Class 5/4) | Later (mature WBS, Class 2/1) |
| Speed | Fast | Slow, effort-intensive |
| Accuracy | Wide range, benchmark-dependent | Narrower range, but only as good as the WBS's completeness |
| Ownership/buy-in | Set by sponsor/PMO | Built by control-account owners who will be held to it |
| Typical use | Business case, capital sanction, portfolio screening | Baseline for execution and performance measurement |

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Presenting a Class 5 top-down ROM to a client or steering committee as if it
carries the precision of a definitive estimate. (2) Selecting a unit-cost benchmark from a project with
materially different technology, site or labour-market conditions without adjusting it. (3) Failing to
revisit and formally reconcile the top-down allocation once bottom-up detail becomes available, leaving two
conflicting "budgets" in circulation.

**AI in this topic.** Machine-learning parametric-estimating tools can regress unit-cost benchmarks across a
much larger historical project database than a manual review typically can, and can flag when a proposed
new project's parameters fall outside the range the model was trained on (cross-reference KA 13.5). The
model's output is only as sound as the comparability of its training projects; it cannot itself judge
whether a given historical project is a fair analogue for a new site's ground conditions, regulatory
environment or labour market — that comparability judgement remains the estimator's, and the resulting
figure must still be presented with an honest accuracy range, not false precision.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2 (AACE cost-estimate classes and estimating methods in depth); §3.1.3
(bottom-up budgeting and the reconciliation continued); §3.1.5 (assembling the reconciled figures into the
cost baseline).

### 3.1.3 Bottom-up budgeting

**Definition and purpose.** **Bottom-up budgeting** builds the budget from the most granular level upward:
individual work packages are estimated using detailed, resource-loaded methods (AACE Class 2/1, "definitive"
estimates — KA 3.2), summed into control accounts, and control accounts summed into the total base
estimate. It requires a mature Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) and is the method PMI's PMBOK Guide describes
within its "Estimate Costs" and "Determine Budget" processes as aggregating cost estimates for individual
activities or work packages to establish the cost baseline.

**The underlying principle.** Because each figure is built and owned at the level of the person actually
responsible for that work package, bottom-up budgets tend to be more accurate and carry stronger buy-in
than a top-down allocation — the people who will be held accountable for the number built it. The trade-off
is time and effort: a bottom-up build is only possible once the WBS, design and resourcing are developed
enough to estimate at that level of detail, which is why it typically follows, rather than replaces, an
early top-down figure (§3.1.2).

**Formula.**

`Base estimate = Σ (work-package cost estimates) = Σ (control-account totals)`

**Example 3.1.3 — building Northgate's base estimate from the WBS upward.**

*Scenario.* Detailed design for Northgate is now complete enough to estimate every work package. The Civil
works control account comprises three work packages:

| Work package | Estimate ($) |
|---|---|
| Earthworks | 600,000 |
| Foundations | 900,000 |
| Structures | 900,000 |
| **Civil works control account** | **2,400,000** |

The remaining three control accounts are built the same way:

| Control account | Work packages | Estimate ($) |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical & process equipment | Equipment 2,100,000 + Installation 1,050,000 | 3,150,000 |
| Electrical & instrumentation | Cabling 450,000 + Instrumentation 600,000 | 1,050,000 |
| Project management & engineering | PM staff 600,000 + Engineering services 300,000 | 900,000 |

*Required.* The total base estimate.

*Working.*
`Base estimate = 2,400,000 (Civil) + 3,150,000 (Mechanical) + 1,050,000 (Electrical) + 900,000 (PM & Eng)
= 7,500,000`

*Answer.* **Base estimate = $7,500,000.**

*Interpretation.* This is the same $7,500,000 introduced in Example 3.1.1, now shown built up from thirteen
individually-owned figures rather than asserted as a single total — the level of traceability a bottom-up
budget is expected to provide, and the level a reviewer or auditor should be able to request at any time.

**Mini-case — a double-counting error caught at reconciliation.** During roll-up review, the cost-control
lead notices the Earthworks work package was originally submitted at $650,000, not the $600,000 shown
above. Investigation finds that the estimator had included $50,000 of general site-mobilisation cost
inside Earthworks — the same $50,000 that was *also* included, correctly, within the Project Management &
Engineering control account's "Engineering services" line. Left uncorrected, this double-count would have
inflated the base estimate to $7,550,000. Removing the duplicated $50,000 from Earthworks restores it to
$600,000 and the base estimate to the correct $7,500,000. This is precisely the kind of error a trial-
balance-style check on total debits and credits (KA 1.1) cannot catch and a bottom-up roll-up must catch
by design: every cost element should appear in exactly one control account, and a reconciliation pass
across all control-account owners — checking for items that could plausibly belong in more than one place
(mobilisation, temporary works, preliminaries) — is a standard, necessary control before a bottom-up
estimate is accepted as the base for baselining.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Double-counting a cost element across two control accounts (the mini-case above).

after scope is re-defined, wasting the effort the method is meant to reward.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted estimating tools can cross-check a bottom-up submission for likely
double-counted or omitted cost elements by comparing work-package descriptions and cost codes against a
historical cost database or against the WBS/chart-of-accounts structure (cross-reference KA 1.5, KA 13.5) —
a genuinely useful control, particularly at scale across hundreds of work packages. It flags *candidates*
for review, as in the mini-case above; it does not itself know, without human confirmation, whether a given
cost genuinely belongs in one account, the other, or is legitimately shared and needs to be split.

the foundation of the cost baseline).

### 3.1.4 Zero-based budgeting

**Definition and purpose.** **Zero-based budgeting (ZBB)** is a budgeting technique in which every cost
line for the coming period must be justified from a zero base — built up from the specific activities and
resources actually required — rather than adjusted incrementally from the prior period's approved budget.
It contrasts directly with **incremental budgeting**, the more common default in many organisations, where
a new budget is simply derived by applying a percentage or flat adjustment to the prior period's figure.
The technique was popularised in corporate and public-sector budgeting from the 1970s onward and is applied,
at project or programme level, most usefully to recurring **overhead or indirect cost pools** — site
establishment, project-office running costs, home-office support — that would otherwise simply roll forward,
period to period, unchallenged.

**The underlying principle.** Incremental budgeting assumes the prior period's spending pattern was broadly
correct and only needs a small adjustment; it therefore systematically preserves obsolete cost lines and can
be slow to fund genuinely new needs. ZBB forces an explicit answer to "why does this cost exist, and at what
level, for the period ahead?" for every line — which surfaces costs that are no longer justified (an
activity that has ended, a resource no longer required) as well as costs that need to grow (a new
requirement that an incremental adjustment would never capture).

**Formula.** No single arithmetic formula — the "formula" is the discipline itself:

`ZBB period budget = Σ (cost of each justified activity/resource for the period, built from zero)`

as opposed to `Incremental period budget = Prior-period actual × (1 + adjustment %)`.

**Example 3.1.4 — Northgate site overhead, Year 1 to Year 2.**

*Scenario.* Northgate's Year 1 actual site overhead (a recurring indirect cost pool, separate from the
capital base estimate) was:

| Line item | Year 1 actual ($) |
|---|---|
| Site management staff | 220,000 |
| Portable/temporary offices | 60,000 |
| Utilities | 50,000 |
| Site security | 45,000 |
| Site welfare | 70,000 |
| Vehicles & plant hire | 65,000 |
| Insurance & permits | 30,000 |
| **Total** | **540,000** |

*Required.* (a) The incremental Year 2 budget, applying a standard flat 5% adjustment; (b) the Year 2
budget built by zero-based justification, given that permanent site offices are now commissioned (removing
the need for temporary offices), site management headcount rises from four to five due to added scope, and
a new perimeter-fencing requirement raises the security need.

*Working.*

(a) Incremental: `540,000 × 1.05 = 567,000`

(b) Zero-based — each line rejustified from Year 2 conditions:

| Line item | Justification | Year 2 ZBB ($) |
|---|---|---|
| Site management staff | 5 staff × $55,000 | 275,000 |
| Portable/temporary offices | No longer required — permanent offices commissioned | 0 |
| Utilities | Additional permanent-office consumption | 55,000 |
| Site security | New perimeter-fencing requirement | 65,000 |
| Site welfare | Increased headcount | 75,000 |
| Vehicles & plant hire | Marginal increase, added scope | 70,000 |
| Insurance & permits | Marginal increase | 32,000 |
| **Total** | | **572,000** |

`ZBB total = 275,000 + 0 + 55,000 + 65,000 + 75,000 + 70,000 + 32,000 = 572,000`

*Answer.* **Incremental Year 2 budget = $567,000; Zero-based Year 2 budget = $572,000.**

*Interpretation.* The two totals happen to land close together ($5,000, 0.9% apart) — but for entirely
different reasons. The incremental figure is a single blind percentage applied to last year's actual,
including a $60,000 temporary-office cost that no longer has any justified activity behind it. The ZBB
figure removes that $60,000 entirely and, from a clean base, funds $92,000 of genuinely new or increased
needs (management headcount, security, utilities, welfare, vehicles, insurance). A close total should never
be read as evidence that "the incremental method was good enough" — see the mini-case below.

**Mini-case — why the coincidence is dangerous, not reassuring.** Had the removed obsolete cost ($60,000)
and the new/increased genuine needs ($92,000) not happened to roughly offset, the incremental budget
($567,000) would have been materially wrong — either badly underfunding the new perimeter-security and
headcount requirements, or continuing to fund a temporary-office cost no longer needed, or both — while
still *looking* like a small, unremarkable 5% increase to anyone reviewing only the total. This is the core
argument for applying ZBB, at least periodically, to overhead cost pools even where the totals seem
stable: the risk incremental budgeting carries is invisible in the total figure and only visible in the
line-by-line composition, which is exactly what ZBB, and only ZBB, forces to be reviewed.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Applying full zero-based rigour to every cost line in every period — the effort is
usually reserved for overhead/indirect pools, or applied on a rotating multi-year cycle, not the entire
project cost base every year. (2) Treating a ZBB exercise as a mandate to cut costs — its purpose is
justification, not minimisation; a genuinely justified line can, and often does, increase. (3) Assuming a
ZBB total that resembles the incremental total means the incremental method would have been adequate (the
mini-case above).

**AI in this topic.** GenAI tools can accelerate a ZBB exercise by drafting a first-pass justification for
each cost line from historical activity data, contracts and correspondence, and by flagging lines with weak
or missing supporting evidence for reviewer attention (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.5). The judgement of
whether a proposed level is genuinely justified — is five site managers really required, is the security
uplift proportionate to the actual perimeter risk — remains with the budget owner and approver; an
AI-drafted justification is a starting point for review, not a substitute for it.

**Cross-references.** §3.1.2–3.1.3 (top-down and bottom-up, the two directions from which the *capital*
base estimate, as opposed to overhead, is typically built); KA 5.2 (the cost control cycle); KA 4.2
(variance analysis, where an unexamined incremental budget most often shows up as a surprise).

### 3.1.5 The project budget baseline

**Definition and purpose.** The **cost baseline** — also referred to throughout this BoK as **Budget at
Completion (BAC)**, identically to its use in KA 6.1 — is the approved, time-phased budget used to measure
and control cost performance for the remainder of the project. It is formed by adding contingency reserve to
the base estimate (§3.1.1) and, once approved, becomes fixed: it changes only through formal, documented
baseline change control (cross-reference KA 5.4, KA 8.4), never informally. A critical convention, carried
consistently across this BoK: **management reserve sits outside the cost baseline.** The cost baseline (BAC)
is the figure used for earned value calculations in Domain 6; the total project budget (cost baseline plus
management reserve) is a wider, funding-level figure that is not, by itself, the performance measurement
baseline.

**The underlying principle.** A budget only becomes a *baseline* once it has been (a) fully scoped against
an approved WBS, (b) reconciled between top-down and bottom-up views where both exist (§3.1.2–3.1.3), (c)
loaded with a properly sized contingency reserve (§3.1.6), and (d) formally approved and time-phased across
the schedule (KA 3.3). Skipping any of these steps produces a budget that looks authoritative but cannot
reliably support performance measurement — the exact failure mode illustrated in the top-down/bottom-up
mini-case of §3.1.2, where a $660,000 gap would otherwise have been silently baselined.

**Formula.**

`Cost baseline (BAC) = Base estimate + Contingency reserve`
`Total project budget = Cost baseline (BAC) + Management reserve`

**Example 3.1.5 — assembling the Northgate cost baseline and total project budget.**

*Scenario.* Following the reconciliation in §3.1.2–3.1.3, Northgate's approved base estimate is $7,500,000.
A contingency reserve of $750,000 has been sized from the project risk register (fully derived in §3.1.6),
and a management reserve of 5% of the resulting cost baseline has been set by the sponsor.

*Required.* The cost baseline (BAC) and the total project budget, in USD and, illustratively, in SAR at
SAR 3.75 = USD 1 (illustrative rate only, not a live rate).

*Working.*

| Component | USD | SAR (illustrative, ×3.75) |
|---|---|---|
| Base estimate | 7,500,000 | 28,125,000 |
| Contingency reserve | 750,000 | 2,812,500 |
| **Cost baseline (BAC)** | **8,250,000** | **30,937,500** |
| Management reserve (5% of BAC) | 412,500 | 1,546,875 |
| **Total project budget** | **8,662,500** | **32,484,375** |

`Cost baseline = 7,500,000 + 750,000 = 8,250,000`
`Management reserve = 0.05 × 8,250,000 = 412,500`
`Total project budget = 8,250,000 + 412,500 = 8,662,500`

*Answer.* **Cost baseline (BAC) = $8,250,000 (SAR 30,937,500); Total project budget = $8,662,500
(SAR 32,484,375).**

*Interpretation.* Once approved, $8,250,000 becomes the figure spread across the schedule in KA 3.3 to
build PV, and the figure carried into every EVM calculation in Domain 6. The additional $412,500 of
management reserve exists, is funded, and is real — but it deliberately sits above the performance
measurement baseline until, and unless, a formal change transfers part of it in (§3.1.6 shows exactly how).

**Mini-case — funding-limit awareness (a preview of KA 3.5).** Even a correctly built total project budget
of $8,662,500 is not, by itself, sufficient: the client has advised that no more than $3,000,000 can be
released in the first project year regardless of how the work is scheduled. If Northgate's time-phased
cost baseline (built in full in KA 3.3) required, say, $3,600,000 of spend in Year 1, the schedule and
resourcing plan would need to be revisited *before* baselining — a **funding-limit reconciliation** — since
no amount of contingency or management reserve solves a cash-availability constraint. This topic is
developed fully in KA 3.5 (cash flow forecasting); it is flagged here because the budget baseline and the
funding profile must be checked against each other before either is finalised.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Quoting the total project budget ($8,662,500) as if it were the BAC used in EVM

**AI in this topic.** Baseline-assembly tools within ERP/EPC-suite platforms can automatically total base

management-reserve percentages applied are appropriate for this project's specific risk profile — those
remain judgements for the professional and sponsor to make and formally approve before a baseline is

### 3.1.6 Contingency reserve and management reserve

**Definition and purpose.** **Contingency reserve** is the allowance, held *within* the cost baseline, for
identified risks that lie within the project's defined scope — AACE International's Recommended Practice
40R-08, *Contingency Estimating — General Principles*, describes contingency as addressing cost variation
arising from foreseeable, quantifiable risk within the current scope of work, sized by methods that range
from a simple percentage allowance to expected-value or Monte Carlo-based quantitative risk analysis
(cross-reference KA 12.2). **Management reserve** is the allowance, held *outside* the cost baseline, for
genuinely unforeseen events — scope the project could not reasonably have anticipated, or approved scope
growth — and is controlled at sponsor, PMO or steering-committee level, accessible only through formal,
approved baseline change (PMBOK's Cost Management knowledge area draws this same distinction between the
two reserve types and their differing levels of authority).

**The underlying principle.** The distinction is not about size — it is about **what kind of uncertainty
each fund is built to cover, and who is accountable for releasing it.** Contingency answers "known-unknowns":
risks the team has already identified, assessed and, in effect, pre-approved a response budget for — so a
project controls professional or project manager, within delegated authority, can draw it down when the
specific, pre-identified risk occurs, without renegotiating the budget. Management reserve answers
"unknown-unknowns" and genuine scope change: things nobody could reasonably have listed in the risk
register — so accessing it requires the same formal change-control discipline as any other baseline change,
because using it changes the cost baseline itself (§3.1.5).

**Formula(e).**

*Contingency, expected-value (probabilistic) method:*
`Contingency = Σ (Probability_i × Impact_i)`, summed across identified risks i in the project risk register
(cross-reference KA 12.2 for the full quantitative risk-analysis treatment, including Monte Carlo
simulation).

*Contingency, deterministic (percentage) method:* `Contingency = x% × Base estimate` — commonly used at
early project stages, or as a policy cross-check against a risk-based figure.

*Management reserve:* typically `Management reserve = y% × Cost baseline`, where y is a management-judged
policy rate (not a risk-register calculation, since it responds to unforeseen, unlisted events by
definition) — cross-reference KA 12.3 for the fuller risk-to-budget linkage.

**Example 3.1.6 — sizing Northgate's contingency reserve from the risk register.**

*Scenario.* Northgate's quantitative risk register (three risks identified for the current scope) is:

| Risk | Probability | Impact if it occurs ($) | Expected value ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adverse ground conditions | 40% | 1,000,000 | 400,000 |
| Equipment price escalation | 30% | 500,000 | 150,000 |
| Weather-related delay costs | 50% | 400,000 | 200,000 |
| **Total** | | | **750,000** |

*Required.* The contingency reserve (expected-value method) and its ratio to the base estimate.

*Working.*
`Contingency = (0.40 × 1,000,000) + (0.30 × 500,000) + (0.50 × 400,000) = 400,000 + 150,000 + 200,000 =
750,000`
`Ratio to base estimate = 750,000 / 7,500,000 = 10.0%`

*Answer.* **Contingency reserve = $750,000 (10.0% of the base estimate)** — matching, in this instance, the
policy cross-check rate the organisation applies at this project stage, which reinforces (rather than
substitutes for) the risk-based build-up.

*Interpretation.* Because the figure is built from named, quantified risks rather than an unexplained flat
percentage, it can be individually drawn down risk by risk as the mini-case below demonstrates — and any
unused portion at project close can be identified and released with equal precision (§3.1.1's mini-case).

**Mini-case — the full budget build-up, contingency vs management reserve, drawn down.** Northgate's
baseline is established as in Example 3.1.5: base estimate $7,500,000, contingency $750,000, cost baseline
(BAC) $8,250,000, management reserve $412,500, total project budget $8,662,500.

*Step 1 — a known risk materialises, funded from contingency.* During construction, the adverse
ground-conditions risk occurs. The actual additional remediation cost is $380,000. Because this risk was
already identified, quantified and included in the cost baseline, the project controls lead approves the
drawdown from contingency, within delegated authority — no change to the total project budget or the cost
baseline is required.

`Contingency remaining = 750,000 − 380,000 = 370,000`
`Base-estimate-equivalent spend on this risk = 7,500,000 + 380,000 = 7,880,000 (effective)`
`Check: 7,880,000 + 370,000 = 8,250,000 = Cost baseline (unchanged)` ✓

*Step 2 — an approved scope change, funded from management reserve.* Separately, the client requests an
additional instrumentation package not in the original scope, priced at $150,000. This is not a
pre-identified risk within scope — it is scope growth — so it cannot be funded from contingency. Following
formal change control and sponsor approval, $150,000 is transferred from management reserve into the cost
baseline.

`New cost baseline (BAC) = 8,250,000 + 150,000 = 8,400,000`
`Management reserve remaining = 412,500 − 150,000 = 262,500`
`Check: New cost baseline + Management reserve remaining = 8,400,000 + 262,500 = 8,662,500 = Total project
budget (unchanged)` ✓

*Answer.* After both events: **Cost baseline (BAC) = $8,400,000; Contingency remaining = $370,000;
Management reserve remaining = $262,500; Total project budget = $8,662,500 (unchanged throughout).**

*Interpretation.* Two results matter here for a project controls professional. First, drawing down
contingency for a pre-identified, in-scope risk changes nothing about the cost baseline total — it is an
internal reallocation within a fund the risk already belonged to. Second, using management reserve *does*
increase the cost baseline (BAC rises from $8,250,000 to $8,400,000, which matters directly for every EVM
calculation in Domain 6 from that point forward) precisely because it is bringing new, formally approved
scope inside the performance measurement baseline — while the total project budget, the full amount ever
authorised, never moves. This is the single most important distinction this KA teaches, and the one most
often confused in practice.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating management reserve as a general-purpose fund a project manager can access
without formal change control — it exists precisely because that authority sits at sponsor/PMO level. (2)
Funding scope growth from contingency instead of management reserve, which understates true contingency
exposure to the remaining, genuinely in-scope risks. (3) Sizing contingency as an unexplained flat
percentage with no risk-register support, making it impossible to defend, audit, or correctly release
unused contingency at project close. (4) Double-counting risk — pricing the same risk both inside an
individual estimator's "padded" work-package figure *and* again inside the formal contingency reserve,
which silently inflates the budget beyond what the risk register actually supports.

**AI in this topic.** Quantitative risk-simulation tools (Monte Carlo-based, cross-reference KA 12.2) can
run thousands of probabilistic iterations across a risk register far faster than manual expected-value
arithmetic, producing a full contingency probability distribution (e.g., a P50/P80 contingency value)
rather than a single expected-value figure — a genuine analytical advance over the simple calculation shown
in Example 3.1.6. Historical-database tools can also suggest a typical contingency percentage benchmark
from comparable past projects. Neither can identify a risk that has not been entered into the register in
the first place — by definition, an AI model cannot simulate an unknown-unknown, which is exactly the
category management reserve, not contingency, exists to cover. Sizing management reserve, and authorising
any drawdown from either reserve, remains a professional and sponsor-level judgement and decision — AI
proposes a distribution or a benchmark; the professional and the accountable sponsor dispose of the funding
decision.

### Key terms

- **Budget** — the authorised, quantified plan of expenditure for a project, tied to a defined scope and
  schedule.
- **Base estimate** — the cost of the defined scope of work, with no allowance for risk.
- **Contingency reserve** — an allowance, within the cost baseline, for identified risks within the defined
  scope; sized by a deterministic percentage or a probabilistic (expected-value/Monte Carlo) method.
- **Cost baseline / Budget at Completion (BAC)** — the approved, time-phased budget used to measure and
  control cost performance; base estimate plus contingency reserve; used identically here and in KA 6.1.
- **Management reserve** — an allowance, held outside the cost baseline, for unforeseen events or approved
  scope growth; accessible only through formal, approved baseline change.
- **Total project budget** — cost baseline plus management reserve; the full amount authorised and funded.
- **Top-down budgeting** — setting the overall budget first, from historical benchmarks or parametric
  relationships, then allocating downward.
- **Bottom-up budgeting** — building the budget from individually estimated work packages upward through
  control accounts to a total.
- **Zero-based budgeting (ZBB)** — rebuilding and justifying every cost line from a zero base each period,
  rather than adjusting incrementally from the prior period.
- **Incremental budgeting** — deriving a new period's budget by a flat or percentage adjustment to the
  prior period's actual or budgeted figure.
- **Known-unknowns / unknown-unknowns** — identified, quantifiable risks within scope (contingency) versus
  genuinely unforeseen events or scope growth (management reserve).
- **Baseline change control** — the formal, documented process by which an approved cost baseline may be
  altered; the only legitimate route for management reserve to enter the cost baseline.
- **Funding-limit reconciliation** — checking a time-phased cost baseline against period-by-period funding
  release constraints (cross-reference KA 3.5).
- **Contingency creep / gold-plating** — the informal, undocumented consumption of an unallocated safety
  margin, avoided by tying contingency to specific, named risks.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 3.1.1 [topic 3.1.1, level: recall]**
Which of the following correctly states the relationship between the cost baseline and the total project
budget?
A. Cost baseline = Total project budget + Management reserve
B. Total project budget = Cost baseline + Management reserve ✔
C. Total project budget = Base estimate only
D. Cost baseline and total project budget are two names for the same figure
*Rationale:* B is the defining relationship (§3.1.1, §3.1.5): the cost baseline is base estimate plus
contingency; adding management reserve gives the total project budget. A transposes the terms; C ignores
contingency and management reserve entirely; D collapses a distinction that Domain 6's EVM calculations
depend on.

**MCQ 3.1.2 [topic 3.1.6, level: application — numerical]**
A project has a base cost estimate of $6,000,000. A risk-based contingency of 12% of the base estimate is
added, followed by a management reserve of 5% of the resulting cost baseline. What is the total project
budget?
A. $6,720,000
B. $7,020,000
C. $7,056,000 ✔
D. $7,116,000
*Rationale:* Contingency = 12% × 6,000,000 = 720,000; cost baseline = 6,720,000; management reserve = 5% ×
6,720,000 = 336,000; total = 6,720,000 + 336,000 = 7,056,000. A omits management reserve entirely; B
results from mistakenly applying the 5% management-reserve rate to the base estimate rather than the cost
baseline; D overstates the result.

**MCQ 3.1.3 [topic 3.1.4, level: application — numerical]**
A cost pool's Year 1 actual was $540,000. An incremental budgeting approach applies a flat 5% increase for
Year 2. What is the incremental Year 2 budget?
A. $513,000
B. $540,000
C. $567,000 ✔
D. $594,000
*Rationale:* 540,000 × 1.05 = 567,000. A applies a 5% decrease instead of increase; B applies no adjustment
at all; D applies roughly a 10% increase.

**MCQ 3.1.4 [topic 3.1.6, level: analysis]**
A known risk identified in the risk register (and already priced into the cost baseline) materialises
during construction. Which reserve should fund the additional cost, and why?
A. Management reserve, because any unplanned cost is by definition unforeseen
B. Contingency reserve, because this is a known risk within the defined scope that was already quantified
   and included in the cost baseline ✔
C. Neither — the additional cost should be treated as an unfunded overrun
D. Management reserve, because only the sponsor can authorise spending against a materialised risk
*Rationale:* Contingency exists specifically to fund identified, quantified, in-scope risks (§3.1.6); using
management reserve here would misapply a fund reserved for unknown-unknowns and unnecessarily escalate a
decision that is normally within delegated project-controls authority. C ignores that reserves exist for
exactly this purpose; D misstates the governance split described in §3.1.6.

**MCQ 3.1.5 [topic 3.1.2/3.1.3, level: application — numerical]**
A parametric top-down budget for a project is $8,500,000. A subsequent bottom-up base estimate totals
$7,500,000. By what percentage does the top-down figure exceed the bottom-up base estimate (to one decimal
place)?
A. 10.0%
B. 11.8%
C. 13.3% ✔
D. 15.0%
*Rationale:* (8,500,000 − 7,500,000) / 7,500,000 = 1,000,000 / 7,500,000 = 13.3%. B results from
mistakenly dividing by the top-down figure ($1,000,000 / $8,500,000) instead of the bottom-up base; A and D
are plausible rounding-error distractors.

**MCQ 3.1.6 [topic 3.1.3, level: recall]**
Which characteristic is most associated with bottom-up budgeting compared with top-down budgeting?
A. Faster to produce but generally less accurate
B. Requires a mature WBS and detailed estimates, and typically yields higher accuracy at the cost of more
   time and effort ✔
C. Always produces a lower total budget than a top-down estimate for the same project
D. Cannot meaningfully be reconciled against a top-down estimate
*Rationale:* B correctly describes the trade-off (§3.1.2–3.1.3 quick reference). A describes top-down. C is
false as a general rule — Northgate's bottom-up figure happened to be lower here, but the direction is not
guaranteed and depends on the specific project and benchmark. D is false — reconciling the two is standard,
expected practice.

### Self-check questions

1. Explain the difference between contingency reserve and management reserve, including who typically
   controls each and what type of cost event each is intended to fund. Use the Northgate Water Treatment
   Upgrade example to illustrate.
2. A project's base estimate is $4,200,000. Contingency is set at 8% of the base estimate, and management
   reserve is set at 6% of the resulting cost baseline. Calculate the cost baseline (BAC) and the total
   project budget.
3. Explain why top-down and bottom-up budgets for the same project rarely agree exactly, and describe what
   a project controls professional should do when the two diverge materially.
4. Using the zero-based budgeting mini-case in §3.1.4, explain why the zero-based Year 2 total ($572,000)
   ended up close to the incremental Year 2 total ($567,000) despite being built on a completely different
   basis, and why that similarity should not be read as evidence that the incremental method was adequate.

**Answers**

1. Contingency reserve funds identified, "known-unknown" risks that are already priced into the cost
   baseline and recorded in the risk register; it typically sits within the project manager's/project
   controls' delegated authority to draw down as those specific risks occur (Northgate: $750,000, of which
   $380,000 was drawn down when the ground-conditions risk materialised). Management reserve funds
   unforeseen, "unknown-unknown" events or approved scope growth outside the original baseline; it sits
   outside the cost baseline, is controlled at sponsor/PMO/steering-committee level, and can only be
   accessed through formal, approved baseline change (Northgate: $412,500, of which $150,000 was formally
   transferred into the cost baseline to fund an approved instrumentation scope addition).
2. Cost baseline = 4,200,000 × 1.08 = $4,536,000. Management reserve = 4,536,000 × 0.06 = $272,160. Total
   project budget = 4,536,000 + 272,160 = $4,808,160.
3. Top-down and bottom-up estimates are built from different information bases — top-down from historical
   benchmarks or parametric ratios available early, before design detail exists; bottom-up from detailed,
   resource-loaded work-package estimates only available once the WBS and design mature (cross-reference
   KA 3.2). They rarely agree exactly because the parametric benchmark reflects a different (analogous)
   project's cost structure, not this project's specific conditions. When they diverge materially
   (Northgate: 13.3%), the professional should investigate the gap category by category, as in the
   §3.1.2–3.1.3 reconciliation, rather than average the two figures or default to either one, and should
   use the divergence to sanity-check both the benchmark's applicability and the completeness of the
   bottom-up scope before baselining.
4. The similarity in total is coincidental — the zero-based total is built from genuinely justified line
   items (removing a $60,000 obsolete temporary-office cost while funding $92,000 of real new or increased
   needs), whereas the incremental total is simply last year's actual inflated by a flat percentage with no
   line-by-line justification at all. A similar bottom-line total does not mean the composition is right:
   had the removed and added costs not happened to roughly offset, the incremental budget would have been
   materially wrong (underfunding the new security and headcount needs, or continuing to fund an obsolete
   cost, or both) while still appearing to be a small, unremarkable increase. The value of zero-based
   budgeting is the justified composition, not merely the resulting total.
