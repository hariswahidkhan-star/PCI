# Domain 3 — Budgeting & Forecasting

## KA 3.5 — Cash flow forecasting

Every preceding Knowledge Area in this domain has worked in **budgeted or accrual terms**: KA 3.1 sets the
budget baseline, KA 3.2 estimates cost, KA 3.3 time-phases that cost into Planned Value, and KA 3.4
forecasts the Estimate at Completion from earned performance. None of those figures is *cash*. A project can
be perfectly on-budget, earning value exactly as planned, and forecast to complete under its EAC, while the
organisation delivering it runs out of money — because cost incurred, revenue recognised and cash received
are three different things, measured on three different timelines. This KA closes that gap. It builds a
project cash flow forecast from first principles — the payment profile of outflows, the billing and
collection profile of inflows, and the resulting net and cumulative cash position — and uses it to answer
the question none of the earlier KAs can: **how much external funding does this project need, and when?**
The chapter runs one illustrative contract, the Harborview Distribution Centre fit-out for main contractor
Anchor Bay Contracting, through a complete cash flow build (3.5.2–3.5.4), stress-tests it, sizes the funding
response (3.5.5), and closes with rolling reforecasting and the honest current state of AI-assisted
forecasting in this domain (3.5.6–3.5.7).

### 3.5.1 Project cash flow versus profit and budgeted cost

**Definition & purpose.** Project cash flow forecasting predicts the actual timing and amount of cash
moving into and out of a project, period by period, so that a professional can identify whether — and
when — external financing is needed to keep the delivering organisation solvent. It is distinct from three
cash-*adjacent* figures already met elsewhere in this Body of Knowledge: the time-phased **cost baseline**
(PV/BCWS, KA 3.3), an accrual-basis budgeted figure; **earned value** (EV/BCWP, KA 6.1), the budgeted value
of physical progress, not cash; and **revenue recognised** under IFRS 15 (KA 2.2), an accrual accounting
figure that need not — and usually does not — match cash received in the same period. Cash flow
forecasting asks a narrower, more operational question than any of these: given the timing of costs
actually paid and amounts actually collected, will there be enough cash at every point in the project to
meet obligations as they fall due?

**The underlying principle — a profitable project can still be cash-negative.** Cost is typically incurred
before it is invoiced; a certificate is typically approved and paid weeks after the work it covers was
done; a percentage of every certificate is commonly withheld as retention until much later (KA 7.4); and
early mobilisation costs are often paid before the first certificate is even due. Each of these is a
**timing mismatch**, not a loss — the project can still be, and often is, profitable on an accrual basis.
But timing mismatches must be *funded* by someone, usually the contracting organisation's own working
capital or a credit facility, until collections catch up with costs. A project controls professional who
reports only budget-versus-actual cost and earned value, without a cash flow forecast, can miss a genuine
liquidity crisis on an otherwise healthy project.

**Formula(e).**

`NCF(t) = CI(t) − CO(t)` — Net Cash Flow in period *t* = Cash Inflows(t) − Cash Outflows(t), in $/period.

`CCP(t) = CCP(t−1) + NCF(t)` — Cumulative Cash Position at the end of period *t*, in $ (running total;
`CCP(0) = 0` unless an opening cash balance is stated).

`Peak Funding Requirement (PFR) = −min[CCP(t)]` over the forecast horizon — the largest cumulative cash
deficit the project reaches, expressed as a positive financing amount, in $.

CI(t) is built from the billing and collection schedule (3.5.3); CO(t) from the cost and payment schedule
(3.5.2). These four symbols — CI, CO, NCF, CCP — are used identically throughout this KA.

**Illustrative preview — profitable and cash-short at once.** The Harborview Distribution Centre contract,
built up in full through 3.5.2–3.5.4, carries a contract price of $1,200,000 against a cost budget (BAC) of
$1,000,000 (SAR 3,750,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) — an overall gross margin of $200,000
(16.7% of price). Its cash flow forecast (Example 3.5.4) nonetheless shows a cumulative cash deficit
peaking at **$192,000** during execution. The project is unambiguously profitable and simultaneously
requires the contractor to fund up to $192,000 of working capital before the contract turns cash-positive.
This is the central, recurring lesson of this KA.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Presenting a budget-versus-actual cost report, or an EVM summary (KA 6.1), as if it also answered the
  funding question — it does not; none of PV, EV or AC is a cash figure.
- Assuming a healthy overall margin means no funding risk — the *timing* of that margin's realisation, not
  its size, drives the funding requirement.
- Building a cash flow forecast only once, at the start of the project, and never refreshing it as actuals
  and the schedule/cost forecast change (addressed formally in 3.5.6).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted data-integration tools can pull cost, billing and payment-term data
directly from the ERP, scheduling and invoicing systems that feed a cash flow model, removing much of the
manual assembly effort and reducing transcription error. They cannot decide which payment-term or retention
assumptions are appropriate for a given contract, or judge whether a client's historical payment behaviour
will repeat — those remain professional judgements, developed further in 3.5.7. **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Cross-references.** KA 1.3 (accrual accounting & matching); KA 2.2 (IFRS 15 revenue recognition); KA 3.1,
3.3 (budget baseline and time-phased cost); KA 6.1 (EVM's PV/EV/AC are not cash); KA 7.4, 7.5 (billing,
retention and revenue recognition in the commercial cycle).

### 3.5.2 Cash outflows: the payment profile for project costs

**Definition & purpose.** A cash **outflow** is money actually paid by the project or its delivering
organisation — to labour, materials suppliers, subcontractors, equipment providers, overheads and capital
items — in the period it is paid. This is different from **cost incurred**, the accrual-basis figure used
to build PV (KA 3.3) and AC (KA 6.1), which records cost against the period the work was done or the
resource consumed, regardless of when cash actually leaves the organisation.

**The underlying principle.** Payment terms create a lag between cost incurred and cash paid. Different
cost types typically carry different terms — labour is often paid weekly or monthly with little lag;
materials and subcontract accounts commonly carry 30-, 45- or 60-day credit terms; capital equipment may
involve a deposit followed by staged payments. A cash flow forecast must convert the time-phased cost
profile into a time-phased *payment* profile using the applicable lag(s) for each cost element — using cost
incurred as if it were cash paid materially understates the funding need in the early periods of most
contracts.

**Formula.** `CO(t) = Σ [cost incurred in period (t − L), paid according to lag L for that cost element]`,
summed across all cost elements, each potentially with its own lag L.

**Example 3.5.2 — Building the outflow schedule for Harborview Distribution Centre.**

*Scenario.* Anchor Bay Contracting's time-phased cost budget for the six-month Harborview contract (built
per the KA 3.3 methodology) totals the full $1,000,000 BAC:

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cost incurred ($) | 60,000 | 140,000 | 220,000 | 220,000 | 200,000 | 160,000 | 1,000,000 |

As a simplifying, stated assumption for this worked example, all cost elements are paid on a uniform
30-day term: cost incurred in month *M* is paid in cash in month *M*+1.

*Required.* The cash outflow schedule CO(t) for Months 1–7.

*Working.*
`CO(1) = 0` (no prior month's cost to pay yet)
`CO(2) = cost(1) = 60,000`
`CO(3) = cost(2) = 140,000`
`CO(4) = cost(3) = 220,000`
`CO(5) = cost(4) = 220,000`
`CO(6) = cost(5) = 200,000`
`CO(7) = cost(6) = 160,000`

*Answer.*

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO(t), $ | 0 | 60,000 | 140,000 | 220,000 | 220,000 | 200,000 | 160,000 | **1,000,000** |

*Interpretation.* The outflow schedule sums to the full $1,000,000 BAC — the payment lag only shifts *when*
the cash leaves, not *how much* leaves overall — but it pushes the entire cost profile one month later and,
crucially, extends real cash exposure a full month beyond the last month of physical cost incurrence.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Applying a single uniform lag across all cost types when labour, in particular, is usually paid with a
  much shorter lag than materials or subcontract accounts — understating early-period outflow.
- Omitting capital expenditure or mobilisation lump sums that are often paid up-front, outside the normal
  supplier-terms pattern.
- Confusing a payment *term* (e.g. "30 days") with a payment *date* — invoices dated mid-month typically
  fall due mid-*following*-month, not at the following month-end; a coarse monthly model (as used here)
  is a simplification that a treasury-grade forecast would refine with weekly or daily buckets.

**AI in this topic.** Machine-learning models trained on an organisation's historical accounts-payable data
can predict actual payment timing more precisely than a stated contractual term — many suppliers are paid
slightly early or late relative to nominal terms, and a model can learn the realised pattern. This improves
outflow forecast accuracy but depends entirely on having enough clean historical payment data for the
relevant supplier base; a new supplier or contract type has no track record for the model to learn from, and
the professional must fall back on stated terms with appropriate judgement in that case.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.3 (accrual vs cash); KA 3.3 (time-phased cost baseline); KA 5.2 (the cost control
cycle: commitment → accrual → actual); KA 3.5.4 (combining outflows with inflows).

### 3.5.3 Cash inflows: billing, collection and retention

**Definition & purpose.** A cash **inflow** is money actually received by the contractor from the client (or
from other sources such as advances or loan drawdowns), in the period it is received. This differs from
**revenue recognised** under IFRS 15 (KA 2.2) and from the **certified billing** amount on a payment
application (KA 7.4), both of which record value in the period the work or the certificate relates to,
regardless of when cash is actually collected.

**The underlying principle.** Three factors typically delay and reduce cash inflow relative to certified
billing: (1) a **collection lag** between certification and payment, arising from certificate review,
approval workflow and the client's own payment run cycle; (2) **retention**, a percentage withheld from
each certificate as security, released only later — often only at (or after) practical completion or the
end of a defects-liability period (KA 7.4); and (3) any **mobilisation or other advance**, which — unlike
the first two factors — can bring cash *forward* of the normal billing cycle (developed in 3.5.5).

**Formula.** `CI(t) = Σ [certified billing in period (t − L′) × (1 − retention%)]`, plus any advance
received or retention released in period *t*, where L′ is the collection lag from certification to payment.

**Example 3.5.3 — Building the inflow schedule for Harborview Distribution Centre.**

*Scenario.* The Harborview contract price is $1,200,000 (a 20% mark-up on the $1,000,000 BAC). Progress is
certified monthly using the input/percentage-of-completion method (cost incurred as a proportion of BAC,
cross-reference KA 2.2, KA 7.4), so certified billing tracks the cost profile from Example 3.5.2 in the same
proportion:

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| % of BAC incurred | 6.0% | 14.0% | 22.0% | 22.0% | 20.0% | 16.0% | 100.0% |
| Certified billing ($) | 72,000 | 168,000 | 264,000 | 264,000 | 240,000 | 192,000 | 1,200,000 |

The contract terms specify payment 60 days after certification (a two-month collection lag, reflecting
certificate review and the client's payment-run cycle) and 5% retention withheld from every certificate
until the final account.

*Required.* The cash inflow schedule CI(t) for Months 1–8.

*Working.*
`CI(1) = 0`, `CI(2) = 0` (no certificate old enough to have been paid yet)
`CI(3) = 95% × billing(1) = 0.95 × 72,000 = 68,400`
`CI(4) = 95% × billing(2) = 0.95 × 168,000 = 159,600`
`CI(5) = 95% × billing(3) = 0.95 × 264,000 = 250,800`
`CI(6) = 95% × billing(4) = 0.95 × 264,000 = 250,800`
`CI(7) = 95% × billing(5) = 0.95 × 240,000 = 228,000`
`CI(8) = 95% × billing(6) = 0.95 × 192,000 = 182,400`

*Answer.*

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CI(t), $ | 0 | 0 | 68,400 | 159,600 | 250,800 | 250,800 | 228,000 | 182,400 | **1,140,000** |

*Interpretation.* Total collections of $1,140,000 equal 95% of the $1,200,000 contract price exactly — the
missing $60,000 (5% retention) remains outstanding at Month 8, to be collected only when the final account
and retention release occur, beyond this forecast horizon (cross-reference KA 7.4). Two full months elapse
before any cash is collected at all.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Modelling cash inflow as if it equalled certified billing (or, worse, revenue recognised) in the same
  period — this is the single most common and most damaging error in a project cash flow model.
- Forgetting that retention, once withheld, sits outside the cash flow entirely until a defined future
  release event — it is not simply "collected later automatically" without that event occurring.
- Assuming the collection lag is fixed and contractual when, in practice, certificate approval delays,
  disputes and client payment-run timing routinely extend it (addressed further in 3.5.4's mini-case).

**AI in this topic.** Predictive models trained on a client's historical certificate-to-payment timing can
estimate a realistic collection lag (and its variability) more accurately than relying on the stated
contractual term alone — genuinely useful where an organisation has a payment history with that client.
Such a model cannot anticipate a *first-time* client's behaviour, a dispute-driven delay, or a change in the
client's own financial position, and a plausible-looking historical average can understate risk on an
atypical certificate. Collection-lag assumptions used in a forecast should be reviewed and justified by the
professional, not accepted from a model without scrutiny.

**Cross-references.** KA 2.2 (IFRS 15, the input/POC method); KA 7.4 (interim applications, certification,
retention release); KA 7.5 (contract asset/liability, over-/under-billing); KA 3.5.5 (retention and advances
as funding levers).

### 3.5.4 The project cash flow forecast: net cash flow and the cumulative funding requirement

**Definition & purpose.** This topic combines the outflow schedule (3.5.2) and inflow schedule (3.5.3) into
the project's **net cash flow** and **cumulative cash position** for every period, and reads off the
**peak funding requirement (PFR)** — the largest cumulative deficit the project reaches, and therefore the
minimum amount of external financing the professional must ensure is available, and when.

**The underlying principle.** A single period's net cash flow says little on its own; it is the *running
total* — the cumulative cash position — that reveals whether, and by how much, the project falls into
deficit before recovering. Because the deficit's depth and duration depend on the *relative* timing of
outflows and inflows (not on either schedule alone), the same total margin can produce very different
funding profiles depending on payment terms, retention and collection performance — which is exactly why
this forecast, not the budget or the EVM summary, is the tool that answers the funding question.

**Example 3.5.4 — The Harborview Distribution Centre cash flow forecast.**

*Scenario.* Combine the outflow schedule (Example 3.5.2) and inflow schedule (Example 3.5.3).

*Required.* NCF(t) and CCP(t) for Months 1–8, and the peak funding requirement.

*Working.*

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CI(t), $ | 0 | 0 | 68,400 | 159,600 | 250,800 | 250,800 | 228,000 | 182,400 |
| CO(t), $ | 0 | 60,000 | 140,000 | 220,000 | 220,000 | 200,000 | 160,000 | 0 |
| **NCF(t), $** | 0 | (60,000) | (71,600) | (60,400) | 30,800 | 50,800 | 68,000 | 182,400 |
| **CCP(t), $** | 0 | (60,000) | (131,600) | **(192,000)** | (161,200) | (110,400) | (42,400) | 140,000 |

`NCF(4) = CI(4) − CO(4) = 159,600 − 220,000 = (60,400)`
`CCP(4) = CCP(3) + NCF(4) = (131,600) + (60,400) = (192,000)`

*Answer.* The cumulative cash position is negative from Month 2 through Month 7, reaching its most negative
point, **the peak funding requirement of $192,000 (SAR 720,000 illustrative)**, at the end of Month 4. The
project turns cumulatively cash-positive only during Month 8, closing at **+$140,000** — exactly the
$200,000 overall margin less the $60,000 retention still outstanding at that date (cross-reference 3.5.3).

*Interpretation.* Anchor Bay Contracting must have at least $192,000 of committed funding available by the
end of Month 4 purely to deliver a contract that will ultimately return $200,000 of margin — the funding
need has nothing to do with profitability and everything to do with timing.

#### 3.5.4a Mini-case — sensitivity of the peak funding requirement to a single month's collection delay

Certificate approval delays are common in practice. Suppose the client's collection lag stretches from two
months to **three months** (all other assumptions unchanged) — a single additional month's delay.

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CCP(t) — base case (2-month lag), $ | 0 | (60,000) | (131,600) | (192,000) | (161,200) | (110,400) | (42,400) | 140,000 | — |
| CCP(t) — stressed case (3-month lag), $ | 0 | (60,000) | (200,000) | (351,600) | **(412,000)** | (361,200) | (270,400) | (42,400) | 140,000 |

Both scenarios collect the same total $1,140,000 and reach the same final position (+$140,000) — only the
timing shifts, by construction. But the peak funding requirement more than doubles, from $192,000 to
**$412,000**, and the trough moves from Month 4 to Month 5. A single month's slippage in the client's
payment cycle — entirely plausible, and outside the contractor's direct control — has a disproportionate
effect on the funding the contractor must arrange. This sensitivity, not the base-case number alone, is
what a professional must present to whoever approves project financing (cross-reference 3.5.5).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reading only month-end cumulative figures and assuming the true minimum occurred at a month-end — in a
  finer-grained (weekly or daily) model the intra-month trough can be deeper still.
- Treating the base-case peak funding requirement as a fixed number rather than a central estimate that
  should be stress-tested against realistic delay and cost-overrun scenarios, as above.
- Confusing the peak funding requirement with the *total* cash deficit summed across all negative months —
  it is the single deepest point, not a sum (reinforced in MCQ 3.5.4).

**AI in this topic.** Beyond the single stress scenario shown above, scenario-generation tools can run many
combinations of collection-lag, retention and cost-overrun assumptions automatically and summarise the
resulting distribution of peak funding requirements (developed fully in 3.5.7). This materially widens the
range of scenarios a professional can practically test compared with a handful of manual "what-ifs." It does
not remove the need for the professional to select realistic input ranges and to interpret which scenarios
are actually plausible for this client and this contract — an AI tool exploring an unrealistic input range
produces a confident-looking but meaningless output range.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.5.2, 3.5.3 (the two schedules combined here); KA 4.2 (variance analysis, once
actuals emerge); KA 6.3 (EAC forecasting feeding a revised outflow forecast, 3.5.6); KA 3.5.5 (funding the
requirement identified here).

### 3.5.5 Funding the cash flow gap: sources, sizing and cost

**Definition & purpose.** Once the peak funding requirement is known (3.5.4), the professional must ensure
funding of at least that amount — with appropriate headroom for the sensitivity shown in 3.5.4a — is
arranged and available *before* the trough occurs, not once cash has already run short. This topic covers
the common funding sources available to a contracting organisation and shows, with a worked mini-case, how
one of them — a mobilisation advance — materially changes the funding profile itself, not just how it is
financed.

**The underlying principle.** Funding sources fall into two broad categories: those that **cover** a
funding gap after it has arisen (an overdraft or revolving credit facility (RCF) drawn against the deficit,
invoice discounting/factoring against unpaid certificates, parent-company or shareholder support), and those
that **reduce the gap itself** by changing the underlying cash timing (a mobilisation or other advance
payment from the client, a retention bond substituted for cash retention withheld, or negotiating shorter
client payment terms or longer supplier terms). A facility must be sized to the *stressed*, not merely the
base-case, peak funding requirement, and arranged with enough lead time that it is in place before the
trough — arranging finance during a cash crisis is materially harder, slower and more expensive than
arranging it in advance.

**Example 3.5.5 — Mitigating the peak funding requirement with a mobilisation advance.**

*Scenario.* Anchor Bay Contracting negotiates a 10% mobilisation advance on the Harborview contract:
$120,000 (10% × $1,200,000) paid at contract award (Month 0), recovered by deducting 10% of the gross value
of every subsequent certificate before retention. Retention remains 5%; the collection lag remains two
months (base case).

*Required.* The revised cumulative cash position, and the revised peak funding requirement.

*Working.* Net cash received per certificate becomes `billing × (1 − 10% advance recovery − 5% retention) =
billing × 85%`, collected on the same two-month lag; the $120,000 advance is received up-front at Month 0.

| Month | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CCP(t) — no advance (base case), $ | 0 | 0 | (60,000) | (131,600) | (192,000) | (161,200) | (110,400) | (42,400) | 140,000 |
| CCP(t) — with 10% advance, $ | 120,000 | 120,000 | 60,000 | (18,800) | **(96,000)** | (91,600) | (67,200) | (23,200) | 140,000 |

*Answer.* The peak funding requirement falls from $192,000 to **$96,000** — a reduction of $96,000, or
**50%** — while the final position ($140,000) and total cash collected ($1,140,000, since 85% collected via
certificates + the $120,000 advance = $1,020,000 + $120,000 = $1,140,000) are unchanged.

*Interpretation.* The advance does not change how profitable the contract is; it changes *who* funds the
early-project deficit — bringing forward client cash rather than drawing on the contractor's own facility —
and it happens to roughly halve the peak funding requirement in this case because it front-loads a
significant slice of the client's payment obligation to before the deepest part of the trough.

**Worked note — an illustrative cost of funding.** If the residual (post-advance) funding requirement of up
to $96,000 were drawn on a revolving credit facility at an illustrative rate of 8% per annum for a
representative three months around the trough (Months 3–5), the simplified interest cost is:

`Interest ≈ Peak funding requirement × annual rate × (months drawn / 12) = 96,000 × 8% × (3/12) = $1,920`

This is a deliberately simplified illustration (a treasury function would compute interest on the actual
daily or monthly drawn balance, which fluctuates through the trough, not on a flat peak-for-three-months
assumption) — but it makes the point that funding a cash flow gap is not free, and the saving from securing
an advance (reducing the funded amount from $192,000 to $96,000) also roughly halves this illustrative
interest cost.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Sizing a facility to the unstressed base case only, leaving no headroom for the kind of collection delay
  modelled in 3.5.4a.
- Treating a retention bond or advance as a "free" source of funding — bonds carry a fee, and an advance is
  usually secured against an on-demand guarantee that itself has a cost and a claim risk if the advance is
  not fully recovered through certificates as planned.
- Leaving facility arrangement until the cash position is already deteriorating — lead time to arrange or
  increase a facility must be built into the forecast timeline, not assumed to be instantaneous.

**AI in this topic.** Scenario-generation tools (3.5.7) can test many combinations of funding source, advance
percentage and facility size against a distribution of delay/overrun scenarios far faster than manual
sensitivity testing, helping size a facility with an evidence-based headroom rather than a rule-of-thumb
percentage. The decision to accept a given advance structure, bond cost or facility size — and the
commercial and credit judgement behind it — remains the accountable professional's and the organisation's
treasury/finance function's decision; an AI-generated "optimal" facility size is a starting point for
negotiation, not an instruction.

### 3.5.6 Forecasting methods and rolling reforecasts

**Definition & purpose.** A cash flow forecast built once, at contract award, becomes progressively less
reliable as the project proceeds and actuals diverge from the original plan. A **rolling forecast** is
refreshed at each reporting period, replacing elapsed-period plan figures with actuals and re-projecting the
remaining periods. Two broad methods are used to re-project those remaining periods: **trend-based**
forecasting extrapolates recent actual cash flow patterns forward; **driver-based** forecasting rebuilds the
remaining-period forecast from the current underlying drivers — the latest schedule, the latest
Estimate at Completion (EAC, KA 6.3) and the current billing plan — rather than from the shape of past cash
flow alone.

**The underlying principle.** Trend-based forecasting is quick and requires little additional data, but it
only reflects a change once enough actual periods have passed to move the trend — it is inherently
backward-looking. Driver-based forecasting responds immediately to a known change in an underlying driver
(a re-forecast EAC, an agreed schedule slip, a revised billing plan) even before that change shows up in
realised cash actuals, which makes it the preferred method whenever a material driver is already known to
have changed — precisely the situation KA 6.3's EAC re-forecasting is designed to surface.

**Illustrative mini-case — reforecasting after a cost-driver change.** Suppose that, at the Month 4 status
date, an EAC re-forecast (KA 6.3) indicates the remaining Months 5–6 cost will run 10% above the original
budget (a recognised productivity issue on the balance of the works). The original remaining outflow for
Months 5–6 was $200,000 + $160,000 (paid one month later, per 3.5.2's lag) = $360,000. A driver-based
reforecast updates this immediately:

`Revised remaining outflow = 360,000 × 1.10 = $396,000` — an increase of `396,000 − 360,000 = $36,000` to the
remaining funding requirement, flowing straight into a revised CCP(t) and a revised peak funding requirement
for the remainder of the contract.

A trend-based forecast, extrapolating only the shape of the cash flow actually observed in Months 1–4, would
not capture this $36,000 increase at all until the higher costs had actually been paid and showed up as
worse-than-expected actuals in a later period — by which point the funding response may already be late.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Running a purely trend-based rolling forecast on a project where scope, schedule or cost has materially
  changed, producing a forecast that quietly ignores a known driver.
- Reforecasting cash flow without reference to the latest EAC/ETC (KA 6.3) — the two forecasts should be
  reconciled, not built independently and left to disagree.
- Reforecasting too infrequently for the project's volatility — a stable, late-stage contract may need only
  monthly reforecasting; an early-stage or high-risk contract may need it more often.

**AI in this topic.** Driver-based reforecasting is naturally suited to automation: an AI/RPA workflow can be
configured to pull the latest EAC and schedule status automatically and regenerate the cash flow forecast
without waiting for a manual reforecasting cycle, shortening the lag between a driver changing and the
funding forecast reflecting it. The configuration of which drivers matter, and the validation that an
automatically regenerated forecast is sensible, remain professional responsibilities — an automated
reforecast built on a stale or wrong EAC will simply propagate that error faster.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.4 (forecasting methods and rolling forecasts generally); KA 6.3 (the EAC family in
depth); KA 3.5.7 (AI-based forecasting methods, developed fully next).

### 3.5.7 AI in cash flow forecasting: ML-based forecasting, scenario generation and governance

**Definition & purpose.** This topic addresses, in depth, the three AI capability areas most relevant to
cash flow forecasting flagged for this domain: **ML-based cost/return forecasting** (models trained on
historical project data to predict future cash inflow and outflow patterns), **scenario generation**
(simulating many plausible cash flow paths under varying assumptions, extending the manual stress-testing
shown in 3.5.4a and 3.5.5), and **driver-based forecasting automation** (introduced in 3.5.6). Each genuinely
extends what a manual forecast can practically cover; each carries specific, honest limits that govern how
it may responsibly be used.

**The underlying principle.** All three techniques are fundamentally **pattern-based**: they learn from, or
systematically vary around, historical or assumed data. Their value is proportional to how relevant that
historical data is to the current project, and their risk is greatest precisely where a project is genuinely
novel — a new client, an unusual contract type, an unprecedented market condition — because there is no
representative history for the model to learn from or to centre a scenario range on. This is the same
"garbage in, garbage out" limitation that runs through every AI application in this Body of Knowledge
(cross-reference KA 13.1, 13.2), applied specifically to cash forecasting.

- **ML-based cost/return forecasting.** A regression or time-series model trained on an organisation's past
  projects (cost profiles, payment behaviour, collection lags by client) can predict a new project's likely
  cash flow shape faster, and potentially more accurately, than a manually built analogue-based forecast —
  particularly useful for early, low-detail estimates (cross-reference KA 3.2's analogous/parametric
  estimating). Its accuracy depends on the size and relevance of the training set; a model trained on 40
  prior projects of a similar type and client base is far more trustworthy than one trained on a handful of
  dissimilar projects, or extrapolated to a client or contract type outside its training data.
- **Scenario generation.** Rather than the single manual stress test shown in 3.5.4a, a Monte Carlo or
  similar simulation can vary collection lag, retention timing, cost overrun and other drivers
  simultaneously across thousands of iterations, producing a *distribution* of possible peak funding
  requirements (for example, a P50/P90 range) rather than one or two point estimates. This gives the
  professional a probabilistic basis for facility sizing (extending 3.5.5) that a handful of manual
  scenarios cannot match for breadth. The output is only as trustworthy as the input distributions chosen
  for each driver — an unrealistically narrow assumed range for collection-lag risk will understate the
  true funding risk regardless of how many iterations the simulation runs.
- **Driver-based forecasting automation.** As introduced in 3.5.6, this links the cash forecast directly to
  the latest schedule and EAC data so the forecast updates automatically as those drivers change, rather
  than waiting for a scheduled manual reforecast.

**Illustrative worked note — acting on an AI-flagged risk.** Suppose an ML model, trained on Anchor Bay
Contracting's 40 most recent contracts with public-sector clients, forecasts an 85% probability that this
particular client's certificates will run one month later than the contractually stated term, based on that
client's historical payment pattern. Applied to the Harborview forecast, this is exactly the stress scenario
already quantified in 3.5.4a: a peak funding requirement of **$412,000** rather than $192,000, at 85%
estimated likelihood rather than as a bare possibility. The appropriate professional response is not to
adopt the AI point forecast automatically as the new funding target, but to weigh the 85% probability
alongside other evidence (any known relationship or commercial factors with this specific client, current
public-sector payment conditions) and decide, with the treasury/finance function, how much facility headroom
is warranted — informed by, not dictated by, the model's output.

**Governance and limits.** Every AI output in this topic is a **decision-support input**, never a financing
decision in itself:
- **Data quality and relevance** — a model is only as good as its training data's relevance to the current
  project; stale, thin, or dissimilar-project training data produces a confident-looking but unreliable
  forecast (cross-reference KA 13.2).
- **Hallucination and overconfidence** — a GenAI tool asked to narrate or explain a forecast can produce
  plausible-sounding commentary that overstates certainty or invents a rationale not actually supported by
  the underlying model; any AI-drafted commentary on a cash flow forecast must be checked against the actual
  data before it is issued (cross-reference KA 13.3, 4.4).
- **Auditability** — the assumptions, training data and scenario ranges behind an AI-generated forecast must
  be recorded and traceable, so that a financing decision based on it can be explained and defended later
  (cross-reference KA 13.6).
- **Accountability** — the decision to accept a given facility size, funding source or reforecast remains
  with the accountable professional and the organisation's finance/treasury governance, not with the model.
  **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a single AI point forecast as a guaranteed outcome rather than one estimate within an uncertain
  range — always prefer a probabilistic range (P10/P50/P90) where the tool supports it.
- Feeding a scenario-generation tool unrealistically narrow or optimistic input ranges, producing a narrow
  and falsely reassuring output range.
- Applying a model trained on one client base, sector or contract type to a materially different project
  without re-validating its relevance.
- Allowing an automated driver-based reforecast to post or be relied upon without a periodic human sanity
  check against the underlying EAC and schedule status.

**Cross-references.** KA 13.1, 13.2 (AI/ML foundations and data quality); KA 13.4 (forecasting/ML platform
tool categories); KA 13.5 (AI applied across the lifecycle, including estimating and forecasting workflows in
depth); KA 13.6 (AI governance, ethics and assurance in full); KA 6.3 (EAC forecasting, the primary cost
driver feeding this forecast); KA 12.2 (quantitative risk analysis and Monte Carlo simulation, the same
technique applied to risk registers).

### Key terms

- **Cash inflow (CI) / cash outflow (CO)** — cash actually received/paid in a given period, as distinct from
  revenue recognised, certified billing, or cost incurred on an accrual basis.
- **Net cash flow (NCF)** — inflows minus outflows for a single period.
- **Cumulative cash position (CCP)** — the running total of net cash flow from the start of the forecast.
- **Peak funding requirement (PFR)** — the largest cumulative cash deficit reached during the forecast; the
  minimum external financing that must be available, and by when.
- **Payment lag / collection lag** — the time between cost incurred and cash paid (payment lag), or between
  billing certified and cash received (collection lag).
- **Retention** — a percentage withheld from each certified payment as security, released on a defined later
  event (cross-reference KA 7.4).
- **Mobilisation advance** — an up-front payment from the client, recovered against future certificates,
  used to bring cash forward and reduce the peak funding requirement.
- **Funding curve** — the plotted cumulative cash position over time; typically dips below zero mid-project
  before recovering (an "S" or "banana" shape).
- **Revolving credit facility (RCF) / overdraft** — a financing facility drawn to cover a cash deficit as it
  arises, sized to at least the (stressed) peak funding requirement.
- **Trend-based forecasting / driver-based forecasting** — re-projecting future cash flow from extrapolated
  past patterns, versus rebuilding it from current schedule/cost/billing drivers (e.g. a revised EAC).
- **Rolling forecast** — a forecast refreshed at each reporting period as actuals and drivers change.
- **Scenario generation** — simulating many cash flow paths under varying assumptions to produce a
  probabilistic range of outcomes (e.g. P10/P50/P90 peak funding requirement).

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 3.5.1 [topic 3.5.1, level: recall]**
Which statement best explains why a profitable project can still face a cash shortfall during execution?
A. Profit and cash are always identical in a well-run project
B. **Timing differences between cost incurrence, billing, collection and retention release can create a cash deficit even though the project earns a margin overall** ✔
C. Cash flow forecasting is unnecessary once the budget is approved
D. A project with positive earned value can never have negative cumulative cash
*Rationale:* Cash and accrual profit diverge because of payment lags and retention (3.5.1) — B is correct.
A is false; C ignores the funding risk a budget alone cannot reveal; D confuses EV, a budgeted measure, with
actual cash.

**MCQ 3.5.2 [topic 3.5.3, level: application — numerical]**
A contractor certifies $168,000 of progress billing in a given month. Retention is 5%, and payment terms
mean cash is collected two months later, net of retention. What net cash amount is collected two months
later?
A. $151,200
B. **$159,600** ✔
C. $160,000
D. $168,000
*Rationale:* `168,000 × (1 − 0.05) = 159,600`. A wrongly applies a 10% deduction; C is an arithmetic slip; D
ignores retention entirely.

**MCQ 3.5.3 [topic 3.5.4, level: application — numerical]**
At the end of Month 3, a project's cumulative cash position is −$131,600. In Month 4, cash inflows are
$159,600 and cash outflows are $220,000. What is the cumulative cash position at the end of Month 4?
A. −$60,400
B. **−$192,000** ✔
C. −$131,600
D. $28,000
*Rationale:* Net cash flow(4) = 159,600 − 220,000 = −60,400; CCP(4) = −131,600 + (−60,400) = −192,000
(Example 3.5.4). A is only the period's net flow, not the cumulative; C repeats the prior month; D reverses
a sign.

**MCQ 3.5.4 [topic 3.5.4, level: analysis]**
In a cumulative cash-position table running from Month 1 to Month 8, which single value represents the
project's peak funding requirement?
A. The cumulative cash position in the final month
B. The sum of all monthly net cash flows
C. **The most negative value reached by the cumulative cash position at any point in the forecast** ✔
D. The largest single monthly outflow
*Rationale:* The peak funding requirement is the deepest cumulative deficit (3.5.1, 3.5.4) — the amount that
must be financed — not the ending balance (A), the total net flow (B, which is the same as the ending
balance from zero), or a single period's outflow (D).

**MCQ 3.5.5 [topic 3.5.5, level: application — numerical]**
Without a mobilisation advance, a project's peak funding requirement is $192,000. A 10% mobilisation advance
(recovered proportionally against certificates) reduces this to $96,000. By how much, in percentage terms,
has the peak funding requirement been reduced?
A. 10%
B. 25%
C. **50%** ✔
D. 96%
*Rationale:* `(192,000 − 96,000) / 192,000 = 96,000 / 192,000 = 50%` (Example 3.5.5). A mistakes the advance
rate for the outcome; D mistakes the absolute reduction for a percentage.

**MCQ 3.5.6 [topic 3.5.7, level: analysis]**
A machine-learning model trained on 40 historical projects forecasts an elevated risk of collection delay on
a current contract. What is the appropriate professional response, consistent with "AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable"?
A. Adopt the AI forecast as the final funding requirement without further review, since the model is
   data-driven
B. Ignore the AI output entirely, since ML forecasts have no value in cash flow forecasting
C. **Treat the AI output as one input, investigate the drivers behind the flagged risk, and use professional judgement to decide whether to increase facility headroom** ✔
D. Replace the finance function's sign-off process with the AI model's recommendation
*Rationale:* AI forecasts are decision-support inputs, not substitutes for judgement and accountable sign-off
(3.5.7). A and D cede accountability inappropriately; B discards a genuinely useful signal.

### Self-check questions

1. Using the Harborview Distribution Centre base-case forecast (Example 3.5.4), explain in your own words why
   the project's cumulative cash position is negative from Month 2 to Month 7 even though the contract
   carries an overall margin of $200,000.
2. If the contract's retention rate were 10% instead of 5% (all other assumptions unchanged), recompute the
   peak funding requirement. By how much does it change, and why?
3. Explain the difference between trend-based and driver-based cash flow forecasting (3.5.6), and give one
   reason a project controls professional might prefer a driver-based approach immediately after a material
   cost or schedule change.

**Answers**

1. Costs are paid one month after being incurred, but certificates are only collected two months after being
   raised and are further reduced by 5% retention; because collections lag behind payments and are also
   discounted for retention, cash paid out temporarily runs ahead of cash collected, producing a cumulative
   deficit even though the contract's total billed value, once fully collected, exceeds total cost by
   $200,000.
2. The peak funding requirement rises from $192,000 to **$204,000**, an increase of **$12,000**. The two
   certificates already collected by Month 4 (Months 1–2 billing, $72,000 + $168,000 = $240,000) each now
   have an extra 5 percentage points of retention withheld: `5% × 240,000 = $12,000`, deepening the trough by
   exactly that amount.
3. Trend-based forecasting extrapolates the shape of recently observed actual cash flow forward; driver-based
   forecasting rebuilds the remaining-period forecast directly from the current schedule, EAC/ETC and billing
   plan. A driver-based approach is preferable immediately after a known change (e.g. a re-forecast EAC
   showing a 10% cost increase, 3.5.6) because it reflects that change immediately, whereas a trend-based
   forecast would not pick it up until enough new actuals had accumulated to move the trend — by which time
   the funding response may be late.
