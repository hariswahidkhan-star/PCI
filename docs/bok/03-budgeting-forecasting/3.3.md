# Domain 3 — Budgeting & Forecasting

## KA 3.3 — The time-phased budget / cost baseline (BCWS/PV)

A project budget only becomes a control tool once it is spread across the schedule. A single figure — say,
the $1,000,000 total authorised for a project (the **Budget at Completion, BAC**) — cannot, on its own, tell
a controls professional whether the project is on track in June rather than in December: it says nothing
about *when* that money is supposed to be earned. This Knowledge Area builds the missing link — the
**time-phased budget**, also called the **cost baseline** or, in earned-value terms, **Planned Value (PV)**
/ **Budgeted Cost of Work Scheduled (BCWS)** — turning an approved estimate (KA 3.2) and an approved
schedule and budget baseline (KA 3.1) into a period-by-period, cumulative spend plan. Plotted cumulatively,
that plan traces the characteristic **S-curve** that appears on almost every cost report, funding
application and board pack in project controls. KA 3.3 covers why the curve is shaped the way it is, the
methods used to build it — from simple linear spreads to resource-loaded, bottom-up control-account builds
and top-down percentage-distribution tables — how it is formally baselined as the **Performance Measurement
Baseline (PMB)** once management reserve is excluded, and how it is used (and misused) in practice.
Everything here is a direct prerequisite for **KA 6.1**, where this same PV/BCWS curve becomes one of the
three foundational EVM measures compared against Earned Value (EV) and Actual Cost (AC); it also feeds cash
flow forecasting (KA 3.5) and the forecasting techniques of KA 3.4.

### 3.3.1 From BAC to the time-phased budget: why a single total is not enough

**Definition & purpose.** **Time-phasing** is the process of allocating an approved total budget (BAC) to
the individual reporting periods (weeks, months) of the project schedule, so that a budgeted value exists
for *every* period, not just for the project as a whole. The cumulative sum of these period values, at any
point in time *t*, is **Planned Value (PV)**, also called **Budgeted Cost of Work Scheduled (BCWS)** — the
same canonical measure introduced in KA 6.1 and used identically throughout this BoK.

**The underlying principle.** BAC answers "how much, in total, is this project authorised to cost?" That
question is necessary but not sufficient for control: two projects can share an identical BAC and still be
in completely different positions at any given reporting date if their money is meant to be earned on
different timelines. Time-phasing converts a static total into a **plan artefact with a time dimension** —
the yardstick against which physical progress (EV) and actual spend (AC) will later be compared (KA 6.1).
Without it, "on budget" is meaningless mid-project; it can only be assessed at completion, which is far too
late for control purposes.

**Formula.** `PV(t) = Σ (budgeted cost of work scheduled to be completed, period 1 → t)`, expressed
cumulatively in the project's reporting currency (USD, with an SAR illustration where useful), where *t* is
the reporting/data date. The **period (incremental) budget** for period *i* is simply `PV(i) − PV(i−1)`.

**Example 3.3.1 — Same total, different time-phasing.**

*Scenario.* Two work packages, WP-X and WP-Y, each carry an identical BAC of $240,000 (SAR 900,000 at an
illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) over a 4-month duration, but WP-X is planned to spend evenly while WP-Y is
planned to spend heavily up-front:

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| WP-X — period budget ($) | 60,000 | 60,000 | 60,000 | 60,000 | 240,000 |
| WP-Y — period budget ($) | 120,000 | 60,000 | 40,000 | 20,000 | 240,000 |

*Required.* Cumulative PV for each work package at the end of Month 1 and Month 2.

*Working.*
`PV_X(1) = 60,000`; `PV_X(2) = 60,000 + 60,000 = 120,000`
`PV_Y(1) = 120,000`; `PV_Y(2) = 120,000 + 60,000 = 180,000`

*Answer.* At Month 1: **PV_X = $60,000** vs **PV_Y = $120,000** — a $60,000 gap on identical total budgets.
At Month 2: **PV_X = $120,000** vs **PV_Y = $180,000**.

*Interpretation.* Both packages carry the same BAC and will both reach $240,000 by Month 4, but a
month-1 actual spend of, say, $100,000 would read as *over* plan for WP-X (against PV of $60,000) and
*under* plan for WP-Y (against PV of $120,000) — an identical actual cost, two opposite conclusions, purely
because of how the same total was time-phased. This is why BAC alone is never an adequate control baseline.

**Mini-case — why lenders and clients care about the curve, not just the total.** A project financed by a
$240,000 draw-down facility is contractually restricted to drawing no more than the *cumulative approved
schedule* in any given month (a common funding covenant). If the facility was approved against WP-X's even
profile (cumulative $60,000 at Month 1) but the contractor instead executes to WP-Y's front-loaded profile
and requests a $120,000 draw at Month 1, the request exceeds the approved Month-1 ceiling by $60,000 —
triggering a funding exception even though the *total* facility ($240,000) is more than sufficient for the
work. Re-phasing a budget without re-approving the underlying funding schedule is a real and common source
of cash-flow friction on funded projects (cross-reference KA 3.5).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting only BAC and actual-cost-to-date, with no time-phased PV — this cannot support any mid-project
  "are we on track" judgement, only an end-of-project one.
- Assuming a project is "on plan" because cumulative actual cost is close to *some* point on the curve,
  without checking it is close to the *correct* period's PV.
- Treating the time-phased budget as fixed forever — it must be formally re-baselined when scope or
  schedule changes are approved (3.3.5).

**AI in this topic.** Historical-analogue tools can propose a plausible time-phasing shape for a new project
by pattern-matching similar completed projects' actual spend curves (cross-reference KA 13.5) — useful for
an early, pre-detailed-schedule estimate. Such a tool has no knowledge of *this* project's specific logic,
funding covenants or contractual milestones, and an unreviewed AI-suggested curve can produce exactly the
funding-covenant mismatch illustrated above. The professional must confirm the proposed phasing against the
actual schedule and any funding agreement before it is relied upon — **AI proposes; the professional
disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 (the budget baseline, contingency and management reserve); KA 3.2 (the
estimate that BAC is drawn from); KA 3.5 (cash flow and funding); KA 6.1 (PV/BCWS as a foundational EVM
measure — this topic supplies its inputs).

### 3.3.2 Methods of spreading cost across the schedule

**Definition & purpose.** Once a project has an approved schedule (KA 10.1) and an approved cost estimate
(KA 3.2), the cost must be **loaded** onto that schedule using one of a small number of standard spreading
methods. The method chosen determines how the budget is apportioned across the periods each activity or
control account (KA 5.3) spans, and — in aggregate — the shape of the whole-project PV curve.

**The underlying principle.** Every method is an approximation of how money is genuinely expected to be
earned within an activity's duration. A resource that works at a constant rate (steady labour crew,
recurring supervision) is well approximated by a uniform (linear) spread; a resource that is genuinely
front-loaded (mobilisation, a large deposit on an order) or back-loaded (final testing, demobilisation)
needs a weighted spread; and where no detailed, activity-level schedule yet exists, a generic top-down
percentage table is used instead. Choosing a method that does not reflect the real profile of work distorts
the PV curve just as surely as a wrong EV measurement method distorts EV (cross-reference KA 6.1.4).

*Quick-reference — spreading methods:*

| Method | Basis | Best suited to | Weakness if misapplied |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uniform / linear (per activity) | Activity budget ÷ duration, apportioned by the days/weeks/months of the activity falling in each period | Steady-rate activities (e.g. supervision, repetitive fabrication) | Ignores genuine ramp-up/ramp-down within one long activity |
| Front-loaded / back-loaded (weighted) | A pre-agreed uneven split within the activity's duration | Activities with a real uneven effort profile (mobilisation-heavy or commissioning-heavy work) | Can be used to artificially inflate early PV if the weighting does not reflect real planned effort |
| Standard S-curve % distribution table | BAC × a pre-defined percentage table across the project's duration (top-down) | Early-stage budgets, before a detailed resource-loaded schedule exists | Generic; does not reflect this project's specific logic, sequencing or float |
| Resource/cost-loaded schedule (bottom-up) | Sum of each control account's own spread, built from the actual network schedule | Projects with a detailed, logic-linked, resource-loaded schedule (3.3.4) | Only as reliable as the schedule and resourcing assumptions feeding it |

**Formula(e).**
Uniform/linear, apportioned by sub-period units (e.g. weeks): `Period budget = Activity budget ×
(activity-weeks falling in the period / total activity-weeks)`.
Standard S-curve % table (top-down): `Period budget = BAC × (% allocated to that period)`, where the table's
percentages sum to 100% across the project duration.

**Example 3.3.2 — Uniform spread with partial-period apportionment.**

*Scenario.* A structural steel erection activity has a budget of $180,000 (SAR 675,000) and a duration of 9
weeks, worked at a constant weekly rate. Of those 9 weeks, 3 fall within Month 1, 4 fall within Month 2, and
2 fall within Month 3.

*Required.* The period budget for each month.

*Working.*
Weekly rate `= 180,000 / 9 = 20,000` per week.
`Month 1 = 3 weeks × 20,000 = 60,000`
`Month 2 = 4 weeks × 20,000 = 80,000`
`Month 3 = 2 weeks × 20,000 = 40,000`
Check: `60,000 + 80,000 + 40,000 = 180,000` ✓ (3 + 4 + 2 = 9 weeks ✓)

*Answer.* **Month 1 = $60,000; Month 2 = $80,000; Month 3 = $40,000**, reconciling exactly to the $180,000
activity budget.

*Interpretation.* Because activities rarely start or finish on a calendar-month boundary, apportioning by
the finer sub-period unit that the schedule is actually built in (weeks, or days) — not by counting whole
months — is what keeps the time-phased budget accurate; rounding to whole months here would have
misallocated real budget between Months 1 and 2.

**Example 3.3.2b — mini-case: top-down % table vs bottom-up build, on the same BAC.** A standard, symmetric
S-curve percentage-distribution table (illustrative, not tied to any specific published index) allocates:

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| % of BAC | 5% | 10% | 15% | 20% | 20% | 15% | 10% | 5% | 100% |

Applied to the same illustrative $1,000,000 BAC used for the Highbridge Pumping Station example built in
full in 3.3.4 (so the two methods can be compared like-for-like):

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Period budget ($) | 50,000 | 100,000 | 150,000 | 200,000 | 200,000 | 150,000 | 100,000 | 50,000 |
| Cumulative PV ($) | 50,000 | 150,000 | 300,000 | 500,000 | 700,000 | 850,000 | 950,000 | 1,000,000 |

At Month 4, this generic top-down curve reads cumulative PV of **$500,000 (50.0% of BAC)**, against
**$540,000 (54.0%)** from the bottom-up, control-account build in 3.3.4 — a $40,000 (4.0 percentage point)
difference for the *same total budget*. Neither is "wrong": the top-down table is a reasonable early-stage
approximation before a resourced schedule exists; the bottom-up build reflects this project's actual
sequencing once one does. The professional must know which method underlies any PV figure being quoted, and
must re-baseline from the generic table to the bottom-up build as soon as a detailed schedule is available.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using a generic top-down % table throughout project execution instead of migrating to a bottom-up,
  resource-loaded build once the detailed schedule exists — the table was only ever an early-stage proxy.
- Apportioning by whole calendar months when the underlying schedule is defined in weeks or days, silently
  shifting budget between periods (Example 3.3.2).
- Applying a front-loaded weighting to make early PV look larger without a genuine effort-profile
  justification — the cost-side equivalent of EV's "front-loading" pitfall (cross-reference KA 6.1.4).

**AI in this topic.** Scheduling and cost-integration tools increasingly automate the mechanical
apportionment step — reading activity start/finish dates from the schedule and spreading budget uniformly
or to a chosen weighting far faster than a manual build, and some can propose a curve shape from historical
analogues (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). The professional must still choose and approve the *method*
per activity, review the resourcing and calendar assumptions the tool used, and confirm the automated spread
is not silently defaulting to a generic top-down shape where a bottom-up build is now possible — an
unreviewed automated spread is not a substitute for that judgement.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 (the approved schedule and budget baseline this spreads); KA 5.3 (control
accounts/work packages, the level at which spreading is normally applied); KA 6.1.4 (the analogous EV
measurement-method judgement); KA 10.1 (schedule development that this method depends on).

### 3.3.3 The S-curve: shape, drivers and the early/late envelope

**Definition & purpose.** The **S-curve** is the cumulative PV plotted against time. Its name comes from its
characteristic shape: a slow start, a steep middle, and a slow finish — a stretched "S" — rather than a
straight line. The same shape recurs, in outline, on almost every project because of how work is genuinely
sequenced, not because of any deliberate mathematical fitting.

**The underlying principle.** A project rarely has all its activities running simultaneously at full
intensity throughout. Early on, only mobilisation and design-type activities (relatively low-cost) are
under way; through the middle of the project, several major control accounts typically overlap and run
concurrently (the highest cumulative rate of spend); toward the end, fewer, lower-cost activities remain
(testing, demobilisation, close-out). The result — demonstrated numerically in 3.3.4 — is that **even when
every individual control account is spread at a constant, linear rate within its own duration**, the *sum*
of several staggered, overlapping accounts produces a curved, S-shaped cumulative total. The S-curve is
therefore a natural consequence of how work overlaps, not evidence that any single activity was spread
non-linearly.

A related and useful application of the same idea is the **early/late envelope** (sometimes called the
"banana curve"): where an activity carries **total float** (KA 10.2), it can legitimately be time-phased
anywhere between its earliest-start and latest-start position without breaching the schedule logic. Plotting
the cumulative PV assuming every activity starts as early as possible (the **early curve**) and, separately,
assuming every activity starts as late as possible (the **late curve**) produces two boundary curves; the
gap between them is the range within which a legitimately time-phased budget — and, later, legitimate actual
progress — can fall.

**Example 3.3.3 — Constructing an early/late funding envelope.**

*Scenario.* A small three-activity schedule has a total budget of $300,000:

| Activity | Budget ($) | Duration | Total float | Early window | Late window |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | 100,000 | 2 months | 0 (critical) | Month 1–2 | Month 1–2 |
| A2 | 120,000 | 2 months | 1 month | Month 1–2 | Month 2–3 |
| A3 | 80,000 | 1 month | 2 months | Month 2 | Month 4 |

*Required.* The cumulative PV under the early-start assumption and under the late-start assumption.

*Working.*

Early curve (every activity at its earliest window):

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Period budget ($) | 110,000 (A1 50,000 + A2 60,000) | 190,000 (A1 50,000 + A2 60,000 + A3 80,000) | 0 | 0 |
| Cumulative PV ($) | 110,000 | 300,000 | 300,000 | 300,000 |

Late curve (every activity at its latest window):

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Period budget ($) | 50,000 (A1) | 110,000 (A1 50,000 + A2 60,000) | 60,000 (A2) | 80,000 (A3) |
| Cumulative PV ($) | 50,000 | 160,000 | 220,000 | 300,000 |

*Answer.* Both curves reconcile to the same **BAC of $300,000**, but the early curve reaches it by Month 2
while the late curve reaches it only at Month 4.

*Interpretation.* The two curves bound a legitimate funding range, not a cost range — the *total* is
identical in both cases, only the *timing* differs, driven entirely by float. A funder, or a controls
professional setting monthly cash-flow expectations, should treat cumulative actual expenditure that falls
inside this envelope as consistent with the approved schedule logic; expenditure outside it (earlier than
the early curve, or later than the late curve) signals either accelerated working or a schedule problem
worth investigating.

**Fig 3.3.3 — Early and late time-phased budget curves: the funding envelope.**
Chart type: two-line cumulative-cost chart. Horizontal axis: Month 1–4. Vertical axis: cumulative PV, USD.
Two series: "Early curve" (solid, brand blue `#1D4ED8`) and "Late curve" (dashed, neutral grey), both
starting at $0 and converging at $300,000 by Month 4; the area between the two lines shaded lightly and
labelled "funding envelope."

*Underlying data:* the two cumulative rows from Example 3.3.3 — Early: 110,000 / 300,000 / 300,000 /
300,000; Late: 50,000 / 160,000 / 220,000 / 300,000.

**Animation 3.3.3 — "The envelope opens and closes" [digital-only].** Frame 1: both curves start together
at $0. Frames 2–3: the early curve (solid blue) draws quickly and flattens out at $300,000 by Month 2, while
the late curve (dashed grey) lags visibly beneath it. Frame 4: the late curve catches up and meets the early
curve exactly at Month 4, at $300,000; the shaded envelope area, having been widest around Month 2–3,
narrows to zero at that meeting point. Narration cue: "Float doesn't change how much a project costs — only
when the money is expected to move. The envelope is the range of legitimate timing."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assuming the S-curve implies non-linear spending *within* an individual activity — the shape is normally
  an emergent property of overlapping activities, not of any one activity's own profile (3.3.4 demonstrates
  this directly).
- Treating the late curve as a "worst case cost" and the early curve as a "best case cost" — both represent
  the *same* total budget; the envelope is about timing risk, not cost risk.
- Baselining a single curve (usually the early curve, or an agreed intermediate position) and then forgetting
  that legitimate float-driven variation within the envelope is not, by itself, a cause for alarm.

**AI in this topic.** Schedule-risk and Monte Carlo-style simulation tools can generate a probabilistic
funding envelope automatically from a resource-loaded schedule with duration-uncertainty ranges applied
(cross-reference KA 10.3, KA 12.2, KA 13.5), which is considerably faster than manually deriving early/late
curves by hand for a large schedule. The output is only as good as the float and duration-uncertainty
inputs; an AI-generated envelope built on unreviewed, unrealistic duration ranges can look precise while
being materially wrong, and the professional must sanity-check the inputs and resulting envelope against
known project constraints before using it for funding or reporting decisions.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 (budget baseline); KA 6.1 (PV as an EVM input); KA 10.2 (total float, the
Critical Path Method); KA 10.3 (schedule risk and Monte Carlo concepts); KA 12.2 (quantitative risk
analysis).

### 3.3.4 Worked example: building a full time-phased budget and its cumulative S-curve

**Definition & purpose.** This topic assembles everything in 3.3.1–3.3.3 into a single, complete,
bottom-up build: taking a set of approved control accounts, each with its own budget, duration and start
date, and producing (a) the full period-by-period time-phased budget, (b) the cumulative PV/BCWS curve, and
(c) a reconciliation confirming the curve sums exactly to BAC.

**The underlying principle.** A project-level PV curve is the simple sum, period by period, of every
control account's own time-phased budget: `PV(project, t) = Σ PV(control account i, t)` for all *i* in the
cost breakdown structure (cross-reference KA 5.3), at the same reporting date *t*. Because each control
account can use whichever spreading method (3.3.2) genuinely fits its work, and because accounts start and
finish at different points across the schedule, the project-level sum produces the S-shaped cumulative curve
described in 3.3.3 — as this worked example demonstrates directly.

**Example 3.3.4 — Highbridge Pumping Station Upgrade: the full time-phased budget.**

*Scenario.* The Highbridge Pumping Station Upgrade has an approved BAC of $1,000,000 (SAR 3,750,000 at an
illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1), an 8-month schedule, and four control accounts, each spread at a constant
(uniform, linear) monthly rate within its own duration:

| Control account | Budget ($) | Duration | Monthly rate ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| CA-1 Engineering & design | 100,000 | Month 1–2 (2 months) | 50,000 |
| CA-2 Procurement | 240,000 | Month 2–4 (3 months) | 80,000 |
| CA-3 Construction & installation | 400,000 | Month 3–6 (4 months) | 100,000 |
| CA-4 Testing, commissioning & close-out | 260,000 | Month 5–8 (4 months) | 65,000 |
| **Total** | **1,000,000** | | |

*Required.* The period (monthly) time-phased budget, the cumulative PV, and confirmation that the curve
reconciles to BAC.

*Working.* Summing each control account's monthly rate across the months it is active:

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CA-1 | 50,000 | 50,000 | | | | | | |
| CA-2 | | 80,000 | 80,000 | 80,000 | | | | |
| CA-3 | | | 100,000 | 100,000 | 100,000 | 100,000 | | |
| CA-4 | | | | | 65,000 | 65,000 | 65,000 | 65,000 |
| **Period budget ($)** | **50,000** | **130,000** | **180,000** | **180,000** | **165,000** | **165,000** | **65,000** | **65,000** |
| **Cumulative PV ($)** | **50,000** | **180,000** | **360,000** | **540,000** | **705,000** | **870,000** | **935,000** | **1,000,000** |
| Cumulative % of BAC | 5.0% | 18.0% | 36.0% | 54.0% | 70.5% | 87.0% | 93.5% | 100.0% |

Row check (each control account sums to its own budget): CA-1 `50,000+50,000=100,000` ✓; CA-2
`80,000×3=240,000` ✓; CA-3 `100,000×4=400,000` ✓; CA-4 `65,000×4=260,000` ✓.
Column check (period budgets sum to BAC): `50,000+130,000+180,000+180,000+165,000+165,000+65,000+65,000 =
1,000,000` ✓.

*Answer.* **Cumulative PV rises from $50,000 (Month 1) to $1,000,000 (Month 8)**, reconciling exactly to
BAC, with the steepest growth (the middle of the "S") in Months 2–6 where two or more control accounts
overlap, and the shallowest growth at the beginning (Month 1: CA-1 only) and the end (Month 7–8: CA-4 only).

*Interpretation.* No single control account was spread as anything other than a flat, linear rate — yet the
project-level cumulative curve is clearly S-shaped: a period budget of $50,000 in Month 1, rising to a
plateau of $180,000 in Months 3–4 where CA-2 and CA-3 overlap, easing to $165,000 in Months 5–6 where CA-3
and CA-4 overlap, and falling to $65,000 in the final two months. This is the mechanism referred to in
3.3.3: the S-shape is an emergent property of overlapping, staggered control accounts, not of any single
account's own (linear) spread.

Chart type: combination chart — grey bars for the period (incremental) budget on the left vertical axis,
overlaid with a brand-blue `#1D4ED8` cumulative-PV line on the same horizontal axis (Month 1–8), read against
a right-hand vertical axis in USD; a dashed horizontal reference line at $1,000,000 labelled "BAC," which the
cumulative line meets exactly at Month 8.

*Underlying data:* the full period-budget and cumulative-PV rows from Example 3.3.4 above (Months 1–8:
period 50,000 / 130,000 / 180,000 / 180,000 / 165,000 / 165,000 / 65,000 / 65,000; cumulative 50,000 /
180,000 / 360,000 / 540,000 / 705,000 / 870,000 / 935,000 / 1,000,000).

**Animation 3.3.4 — "Building the S-curve, control account by control account" [digital-only].** Frame 1: an
empty chart, axes only. Frame 2: CA-1's two bars (Months 1–2, $50,000 each) appear, and a short cumulative
line segment draws beneath them rising gently to $180,000. Frame 3: CA-2's bars fade in and stack onto Months
2–4, and the cumulative line's slope visibly steepens through that stretch. Frame 4: CA-3's bars stack onto
Months 3–6, the cumulative line at its steepest through the overlap with CA-2. Frame 5: CA-4's bars stack
onto Months 5–8; the cumulative line's slope eases as CA-3 finishes and only CA-4 remains. Frame 6: the
cumulative line reaches and touches the dashed $1,000,000 BAC line exactly at Month 8. Narration cue: "Each
bar is a straight, linear spread. Stack four of them, staggered in time, and the total draws an S — not
because any one account curves, but because they overlap."

**Mini-case — a schedule slip re-shapes the curve without changing BAC.** Suppose CA-4 (Testing,
commissioning & close-out) is delayed by one month — still $260,000 over 4 months at $65,000/month, but now
running Month 6–9 instead of Month 5–8 — while CA-1 to CA-3 are unchanged. Month 5 no longer has any CA-4
contribution, so its period budget falls from $165,000 to just CA-3's $100,000 — a $65,000 dip — and the
project now finishes in Month 9 rather than Month 8. Cumulative PV at Month 5 falls from $705,000 (54.0% +
16.5% = the original profile) to $640,000 (`50,000+130,000+180,000+180,000+100,000`), a $65,000 (6.5% of
BAC) reduction at that reporting date, even though **BAC is unchanged at $1,000,000** and the total scope has
not moved. This is why a schedule slip must trigger a formal re-baseline of PV before EVM comparisons
resume (KA 6.1) — comparing actual progress against the *original* Month-5 PV of $705,000 after CA-4 has
genuinely slipped would report a schedule variance that reflects a known, approved change, not a new
problem.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Summing control-account budgets without confirming the reporting boundary between accounts (e.g. double
  counting a hand-off activity claimed in both CA-2 and CA-3).
- Failing to re-derive the period-budget row after re-baselining (3.3.5) — the cumulative row must always be
  a running sum of the *current* period row, not a patched-together mix of old and new figures.
- Reading the period-budget bars in isolation as "monthly cost," when they are the *scheduled* cost, not yet
  compared with what will actually be earned (EV) or spent (AC) — that comparison belongs to KA 6.1–6.2.

**AI in this topic.** Spreadsheet and BI-integrated AI assistants can automatically build the control-account
× period grid and the resulting S-curve chart directly from a schedule export, materially speeding up the
mechanical build shown above (cross-reference KA 13.4). They do not know whether the control-account
boundaries are correctly defined, whether an activity has been inadvertently double-counted across two
accounts, or whether the spread method assigned to each account genuinely reflects the planned work — the
professional must review the control-account structure and the row/column reconciliation (as performed
above) before the curve is baselined, exactly as an unreviewed automated build can silently misallocate
budget between periods.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 (the budget baseline this build supports); KA 3.2 (the estimate the control
account budgets derive from); KA 5.3 (control accounts, cost breakdown structure); KA 6.1 (this curve becomes
PV/BCWS in the EVM data set); KA 10.1–10.2 (the schedule and network logic this build is loaded onto).

### 3.3.5 Baselining the time-phased budget: the Performance Measurement Baseline, contingency and management reserve

**Definition & purpose.** Once approved, the time-phased budget is formally frozen as the **Performance
Measurement Baseline (PMB)** — the time-phased budget for all currently authorised, planned project work,
against which EV and AC will later be measured (KA 6.1). By common convention (confirm against the
organisation's own EVM system description — practice can vary), **BAC = PMB** for earned-value purposes:
**Management Reserve (MR)** — the budget held by the project sponsor for unforeseen work within the approved
scope (cross-reference KA 3.1) — sits *outside* the PMB and is not time-phased into any control account
until it is formally released. The full authorised project budget is therefore `Total Project Budget = PMB
(= BAC) + MR`.

**The underlying principle.** MR exists precisely because not everything can be planned with certainty; but
an amount that has not been assigned to specific, scheduled work cannot be *time-phased*, because there is
no work package or date to attach it to. Including MR inside the PV curve would overstate how much work is
"supposed" to have happened by any given date, corrupting every subsequent schedule and cost comparison in
KA 6.1–6.4. A related concept, **Undistributed Budget (UB)**, covers budget for scope that *is* authorised
but not yet broken down into control accounts in enough detail to time-phase — also temporarily excluded
from the detailed PV curve until planning catches up, but (unlike MR) still counted within BAC/PMB once a
placeholder time-phasing is assigned.

**Formula.** `PMB = Total Project Budget − MR`; for earned-value purposes, `BAC = PMB`. When an approved
change releases MR into the PMB: `New PMB = Old PMB + Amount released`; `New MR = Old MR − Amount released`;
`Total Project Budget` is unchanged by the release (it is a transfer between two components of the same
total, not new funding).

**Example 3.3.5 — Establishing the PMB for Highbridge Pumping Station.**

*Scenario.* The Highbridge Pumping Station Upgrade's sponsor has authorised a **Total Project Budget of
$1,050,000** (SAR 3,937,500), of which **$50,000 is held as Management Reserve**, undistributed to any
control account. The remaining amount was time-phased in full in Example 3.3.4.

*Required.* The PMB, and confirmation it matches the BAC used in 3.3.4.

*Working.* `PMB = 1,050,000 − 50,000 = 1,000,000`

*Answer.* **PMB = $1,000,000**, exactly matching the BAC used to build the time-phased budget and S-curve in
3.3.4 — confirming that the curve built there correctly excludes MR.

*Interpretation.* The $50,000 MR is real, approved project funding — but it is not yet earned-value-eligible
budget, because it has not been assigned to specific, scheduled scope. Any EVM report at this stage should
show BAC $1,000,000, not $1,050,000; the $50,000 remains visible only in the project's overall funding
summary until (and unless) it is released.

**Mini-case — releasing management reserve into the baseline.** A $40,000 scope addition (extra
instrumentation testing) is approved mid-project and funded by drawing down MR, distributed $20,000 each to
Months 5 and 6 of the existing testing scope (CA-4).

`New MR = 50,000 − 40,000 = 10,000`
`New PMB = 1,000,000 + 40,000 = 1,040,000`
Check: `New PMB + New MR = 1,040,000 + 10,000 = 1,050,000` — unchanged from the original Total Project
Budget ✓.

The re-baselined monthly and cumulative PV from Month 5 onward becomes:

| Month | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Period budget ($) | 185,000 | 185,000 | 65,000 | 65,000 |
| Cumulative PV ($) | 725,000 | 910,000 | 975,000 | 1,040,000 |

Cumulative PV at Month 8 now reconciles to the **new PMB of $1,040,000**, not the original $1,000,000 — a
formal re-baseline, fully traceable through change control (cross-reference KA 5.4, KA 8.4), not a quiet
revision.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Spreading MR across control accounts "just in case," pre-emptively — this defeats its purpose as an
  undistributed reserve for genuinely unforeseen work and silently inflates PV for scope not yet approved.
- Treating an EAC or forecast adjustment (KA 3.4, KA 6.3) as equivalent to releasing MR into the PMB — a
  forecast changes the *expected outcome*; only a formally approved change releases MR and moves the
  baseline itself.
- Losing the audit trail between the original PMB, each MR release, and the resulting re-baselined PV — the
  professional must be able to reconstruct, at any time, exactly which approved changes produced the current
  baseline.

**AI in this topic.** Workflow and governance tools can track MR drawdown requests, route them for approval,
and automatically flag when cumulative releases approach a threshold of the original reserve — a genuine
control benefit at programme scale (cross-reference KA 13.6). These tools manage the *process*; the decision
to approve a release, and the judgement that a proposed change is genuinely unforeseen work rather than a
scope creep item that should have gone through a different governance route, remains the accountable
professional's decision — AI can flag and route a request; it cannot approve it.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 (budget baseline, contingency and management reserve — full treatment); KA 5.4
(change control and cost impact); KA 6.1 (BAC as an EVM input); KA 6.3 (EAC/ETC, which forecast outcomes
without moving the baseline); KA 8.4 (integrated change control within Monitoring & Controlling).

### 3.3.6 Using the time-phased budget in practice: cadence, granularity and cash flow

**Definition & purpose.** The time-phased budget is only useful if it is reported at a sensible **cadence**
(how often — weekly, monthly) and **granularity** (how finely broken down — by control account, by cost
element, by the whole project) for its audience, and if it is not confused with a related but distinct
artefact: the **cash flow forecast**.

**The underlying principle.** PV/BCWS is expressed at **budget (standard) rates**, timed to when work is
*scheduled*, regardless of when cash actually moves. A cash flow forecast is timed to when money is actually
expected to be received or paid — which typically lags PV because of invoicing cycles, payment terms and
**retention** withheld until later release (cross-reference KA 3.5, KA 7.4). The two curves serve different
purposes: PV is the performance-measurement yardstick (KA 6.1); the cash flow forecast is a treasury and
funding tool. Reporting cadence and granularity should match how the audience will use the figure — too
coarse a breakdown hides an emerging problem within an aggregate; too fine a breakdown is costly to maintain
and can bury the signal in noise (cross-reference KA 4.3, KA 4.4).

**Formula.** `Planned % complete(t) = PV(t) / BAC × 100`.

**Example 3.3.6 — Planned % complete, and PV versus cash flow.**

*Scenario.* Using Highbridge Pumping Station's original schedule (3.3.4): PV(4) = $540,000 against
BAC $1,000,000. The client's contract terms require 30-day payment following certification, and 10%
retention is withheld from each certified amount until final completion (cross-reference KA 7.4).

*Required.* (a) Planned % complete at Month 4. (b) An illustrative estimate of the Month-4 cash outflow the
client would actually expect to pay, and when.

*Working.*
(a) `Planned % complete(4) = 540,000 / 1,000,000 × 100 = 54.0%`
(b) `Net certified-payment value = 540,000 × (1 − 10%) = 540,000 × 0.90 = 486,000`, expected to be paid
approximately one month later (around Month 5) once certification and the 30-day payment term have run.

*Answer.* **Planned % complete at Month 4 = 54.0%.** The corresponding **cash outflow the client actually
pays is approximately $486,000, timed roughly a month later than the PV figure it is derived from** — not
$540,000 in Month 4.

*Interpretation.* PV and cash flow are related but are not the same curve: PV tells the performance story
(what work is scheduled to be worth by when); cash flow tells the funding story (what money actually moves
and when), net of retention and payment-term lag. Quoting a PV figure as if it were a cash requirement — or
vice versa — is a common and avoidable error (full cash flow forecasting treatment is KA 3.5).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting the PV curve to a treasury or funding audience as if it were the cash flow forecast, without
  adjusting for retention and payment-term lag.
- Choosing a reporting cadence (e.g. quarterly) too coarse to catch a control account slipping badly within
  the quarter, or a granularity so fine that the underlying data cannot realistically be kept current and
  accurate at every refresh.
- Re-issuing a "PV" figure that has quietly drifted from the approved PMB without a formal baseline change
  (3.3.5) — every PV figure in circulation should trace to one, current, approved baseline.

**AI in this topic.** Dashboard and reporting tools can refresh an S-curve automatically from the scheduling
and cost systems on whatever cadence is configured, and can auto-generate both a PV view and, separately, a
cash-flow view once payment-term and retention rules are correctly configured (cross-reference KA 4.4,
KA 13.5). The configuration step is the risk: a dashboard that has not been told about retention or payment
terms will silently present PV as if it were cash flow, and a viewer without EVM literacy may not notice.
The professional must verify which curve a report is actually showing before it goes to a funding or
treasury audience — AI can automate the refresh; it cannot verify that the underlying configuration is
correct.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.4 (forecasting methods that build on this baseline); KA 3.5 (cash flow
forecasting in depth); KA 4.3 (management reporting cadence and audience); KA 6.1 (PV/BCWS as a foundational
EVM measure); KA 7.4 (retention and interim payment applications).

### Key terms

- **Time-phasing** — allocating an approved total budget (BAC) across the periods of the approved schedule.
- **Time-phased budget / cost baseline / Planned Value (PV) / Budgeted Cost of Work Scheduled (BCWS)** — the
  cumulative, period-by-period budgeted cost of work scheduled to date (cross-reference KA 6.1).
- **S-curve** — the characteristic slow-start/steep-middle/slow-finish shape of a cumulative PV curve,
  typically an emergent property of overlapping, staggered control accounts.
- **Control account** — the level at which a budget is spread and progress is measured (cross-reference
  KA 5.3).
- **Uniform/linear spread, front/back-loaded (weighted) spread, standard S-curve % distribution table,
  resource/cost-loaded schedule (bottom-up)** — the standard methods for spreading cost across a schedule.
- **Early curve / late curve / funding envelope ("banana curve")** — the boundary cumulative curves produced
  by scheduling every activity at its earliest, and latest, position within its total float.
- **Total float** — the amount an activity's timing can shift without delaying the project end date
  (cross-reference KA 10.2).
- **Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB)** — the time-phased budget for all currently authorised, planned
  work; by common convention, BAC = PMB.
- **Management Reserve (MR)** — budget held by the sponsor for unforeseen work within scope, excluded from
  the PMB until formally released.
- **Undistributed Budget (UB)** — authorised budget not yet broken down into control accounts in enough
  detail to time-phase in full.
- **Planned % complete** — `PV(t) / BAC × 100`.
- **Cash flow forecast** — the timing of actual cash receipts/payments, distinct from PV (cross-reference
  KA 3.5).

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 3.3.1 [topic 3.3.1, level: recall]**
What does "time-phasing" the budget mean in project controls?
A. Recording all project costs at the moment they are invoiced
B. **Spreading the total authorised budget across the periods of the approved schedule so that a budgeted value exists for each reporting period** ✔
C. Converting the budget from one currency to another at each reporting date
D. Adjusting the total budget for inflation over the life of the project
*Rationale:* B is the definition of time-phasing / PV(BCWS) construction. A describes Actual Cost (AC)
capture; C describes FX translation, which is unrelated; D describes an estimating/escalation adjustment
(KA 3.2), not time-phasing.

**MCQ 3.3.2 [topic 3.3.2, level: application — numerical]**
An activity has a budget of $180,000 spread at a constant weekly rate over a 9-week duration. If 4 of those
9 weeks fall within Month 2, what is Month 2's share of the budget?
A. $60,000
B. **$80,000** ✔
C. $90,000
D. $40,000
*Rationale:* Weekly rate `= 180,000 / 9 = 20,000`; `4 weeks × 20,000 = 80,000`. A is the 3-week (Month 1)
value; C wrongly assumes a half-share; D is the 2-week (Month 3) value.

**MCQ 3.3.3 [topic 3.3.3, level: analysis]**
A schedule contains activities with total float. One cumulative PV curve is built assuming every activity
starts at its earliest possible date; a second is built assuming every activity starts at its latest
possible date. What do the two resulting curves together represent?
A. Two independent forecasts that should never be reconciled against actual performance
B. The best-case and worst-case total cost of the project
C. **A funding envelope: cumulative actual expenditure is expected to fall between the two curves as long as activities execute within their float** ✔
D. A duplicate of the Performance Measurement Baseline, since only one curve is ever formally baselined
*Rationale:* Both curves sum to the same BAC — only timing differs, driven by float — so B is wrong (there
is no cost range, only a timing range). A is wrong because comparing actual progress against the envelope is
exactly its purpose. D is incomplete: while typically only one position is formally baselined as the PMB,
the envelope remains a valid planning reference distinct from a "duplicate" baseline.

**MCQ 3.3.4 [topic 3.3.4, level: application — numerical]**
Using the Highbridge Pumping Station time-phased budget (Example 3.3.4), what is the cumulative PV at the
end of Month 3?
A. $180,000
B. **$360,000** ✔
C. $540,000
D. $300,000
*Rationale:* `Cumulative PV(3) = 50,000 + 130,000 + 180,000 = 360,000`. A is the Month-2 figure; C is the
Month-4 figure; D does not appear on the schedule.

**MCQ 3.3.5 [topic 3.3.5, level: application — numerical]**
A project's Total Project Budget is $1,050,000, of which $50,000 is held as Management Reserve, undistributed
to any control account. What is the Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB), used as BAC for EVM purposes?
A. $1,050,000
B. **$1,000,000** ✔
C. $950,000
D. $1,100,000
*Rationale:* `PMB = Total Project Budget − MR = 1,050,000 − 50,000 = 1,000,000`. A wrongly includes MR; C
incorrectly subtracts MR twice; D wrongly adds MR instead of excluding it.

**MCQ 3.3.6 [topic 3.3.6, level: application — numerical]**
Using Highbridge Pumping Station's original time-phased budget (BAC $1,000,000; cumulative PV at Month 6 =
$870,000), what is the planned % complete at the end of Month 6?
A. 70.5%
B. **87.0%** ✔
C. 93.5%
D. 100.0%
*Rationale:* `Planned % complete = 870,000 / 1,000,000 × 100 = 87.0%`. A is the Month-5 value; C is the
Month-7 value; D is the completion value.

### Self-check questions

1. Using the Highbridge Pumping Station control-account table (3.3.4), explain why the project-level
   cumulative time-phased budget curve is S-shaped even though each individual control account (CA-1 to
   CA-4) is spread at a constant, linear rate within its own duration.
2. Using the original Highbridge Pumping Station figures, calculate planned % complete at the end of
   Month 6. Then recalculate it after the $40,000 scope change described in 3.3.5 has been baselined
   (new cumulative PV at Month 6 = $910,000; new PMB = $1,040,000).
3. Explain, in your own words, why a project's PV curve is not the same thing as its cash flow forecast,
   using retention and payment terms in your explanation.

**Answers**

1. Each control account starts and finishes at a different point in the schedule and overlaps with others
   for part of its duration. Early on, only CA-1 is active (a low, flat rate); through the middle of the
   project, two accounts overlap at a time (CA-2/CA-3, then CA-3/CA-4), giving the highest combined monthly
   rate; toward the end, only CA-4 remains. Summing several linearly-spread, staggered accounts produces a
   slow-start/steep-middle/slow-finish cumulative total — the S-curve — without any single account being
   spread non-linearly.
2. Original: `870,000 / 1,000,000 × 100 = 87.0%`. After the change is baselined: `910,000 / 1,040,000 × 100
   = 87.5%`. Even though absolute cumulative PV rose (from $870,000 to $910,000), planned % complete moved
   only slightly, because both the numerator (PV) and the denominator (PMB/BAC) increased together.
3. PV/BCWS is expressed at budget (standard) rates, timed to when work is *scheduled* to happen, regardless
   of when cash moves. A cash flow forecast reflects when money is actually expected to be received or paid,
   which typically lags PV because of invoicing cycles and payment terms, and is reduced by any retention
   withheld until later release. The two curves answer different questions — performance timing versus
   funding timing — and should never be substituted for one another (KA 3.5).
