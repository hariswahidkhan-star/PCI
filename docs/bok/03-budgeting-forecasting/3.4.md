# Domain 3 — Budgeting & Forecasting

## KA 3.4 — Forecasting

A budget answers one question — "what did we plan to spend, and by when?" — and, once approved, it stays
fixed until a formal change is incorporated (KA 3.1, KA 3.3). A **forecast** answers a different question —
"given everything we now know, what do we actually expect the final cost, and the completion date, to be?"
— and, unlike the budget, it is meant to move: a good forecast is refreshed every reporting period as actual
cost, progress and productivity data accumulate. This Knowledge Area covers the discipline of forecasting in
project controls: the methods available (judgement-based, analogous, driver-based, trend/time-series and
index-based), the practice of **rolling forecasts** that keep the prediction current rather than stale, and
the introduction of the **Estimate at Completion (EAC)** family — the specific, EVM-based forecasting
formulae that translate cost and schedule performance into a single forecast total cost. EAC, ETC and VAC
are defined here to the level a controls professional needs to compute and interpret a basic forecast; the
full comparative treatment of the EAC family — multiple scenarios, when each formula is defensible, and its
integration with the complete EVM data set — is developed in depth in **KA 6.3**, once PV, EV, AC, CPI and
SPI have themselves been built up from first principles in KA 6.1–6.2. Forecasting also connects forward to
cash flow forecasting (KA 3.5), variance analysis (KA 4.2), change control (KA 5.4) and, for adaptive
delivery, to AgileEVM forecasting from velocity and burnup (KA 9.5).

### 3.4.1 The forecast: definition, purpose and the forecast register

**Definition & purpose.** A **forecast** (also called the **Latest Forecast**, or, once expressed as a
single total-cost figure, the **Estimate at Completion (EAC)**) is a current, periodically produced
prediction of a project's likely final outturn — cost, and often also duration — based on actual performance
and progress to date plus a professional judgement about the work still to be done. It is distinct from two
other figures with which it is often confused:

- The **estimate** — a pre-project, one-off (or infrequently revised) prediction developed before or at the
  start of work, at a defined accuracy class (AACE Class 5 to Class 1 — KA 3.2).
- The **budget (BAC / PV)** — the *authorised*, time-phased baseline against which performance is measured
  (KA 3.1, KA 3.3); it changes only through formal, approved change control, never simply because a new
  forecast has been produced.

The forecast sits between the two: it reuses the estimate's methodology and the budget's structure, but is
live, non-authorised, and updated as often as the controls cycle requires.

**The underlying principle.** Every forecast decomposes into two parts: what has already happened (a fact,
not a prediction) and what is still to come (a genuine prediction, carrying uncertainty). Formally:

`Latest Forecast (EAC) = AC + ETC`

where **AC** = Actual Cost incurred to date (USD; the known part) and **ETC** = Estimate to Complete, the
forecast cost of the *remaining* work only (USD; the predicted part). Because AC is a known fact at the data
date, all forecasting skill and judgement is really concentrated in estimating ETC — the different
forecasting methods in this KA (3.4.2–3.4.5) are, at heart, different disciplined ways of arriving at ETC.
Once EAC is stated, two further canonical figures follow directly: `ETC = EAC − AC` (restating the
definition above; useful when EAC has instead been derived directly, e.g. via an index-based formula — KA
3.4.5) and `VAC = BAC − EAC` — the **Variance at Completion**, the forecast gap against the original
authorised budget, in USD, shown in parentheses when adverse per this book's convention.

**Formula(e).**
`EAC = AC + ETC` — Estimate at Completion, USD.
`ETC = EAC − AC` — Estimate to Complete, USD.
`VAC = BAC − EAC` — Variance at Completion, USD (adverse if negative).

**Example 3.4.1 — a project's forecast register at a status date.**

*Scenario.* A facility fit-out programme comprises four work packages, each independently tracked. At the
current status date, the cost engineer responsible for each package has assessed the actual cost to date
and the estimate of the cost remaining (ETC):

| Package | Budget (BAC, $) | Actual to date (AC, $) | ETC — cost to complete ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — Mechanical | 400,000 | 380,000 | 30,000 |
| B — Electrical | 250,000 | 100,000 | 170,000 |
| C — Fit-out & finishes | 600,000 | 550,000 | 40,000 |
| D — Commissioning | 150,000 | 20,000 | 140,000 |
| **Total** | **1,400,000** | **1,050,000** | **380,000** |

*Required.* The Latest Forecast (EAC) for each package and for the programme, and the total VAC.

*Working.*
Package A: `EAC = 380,000 + 30,000 = 410,000`. Package B: `EAC = 100,000 + 170,000 = 270,000`. Package C:
`EAC = 550,000 + 40,000 = 590,000`. Package D: `EAC = 20,000 + 140,000 = 160,000`.
Programme EAC (two equivalent routes): summing the package EACs, `410,000 + 270,000 + 590,000 + 160,000 =
1,430,000`; or, from the totals row, `EAC = 1,050,000 + 380,000 = 1,430,000` — the two routes must agree,
and here they do.
`VAC = BAC − EAC = 1,400,000 − 1,430,000 = (30,000)`.

*Answer.* **Programme EAC = $1,430,000; VAC = ($30,000), adverse.**

*Interpretation.* The programme is forecast to overrun its $1,400,000 budget by $30,000 (2.1%). That total
masks very different stories at package level: Package A is heading for a modest $10,000 overrun; Package C
for a $10,000 overrun on a much larger base (a smaller relative miss); Packages B and D are both forecasting
comfortably within budget. A forecast register is only useful when read at the level that drives management
action — the total is the headline, the package rows are where the explanation and any corrective action
live (cross-reference KA 4.2, KA 4.3).

**Fig 3.4.1 — Forecast register: Budget, Actual and Latest Forecast (EAC) by package.**

Programme: 1,400,000 / 1,430,000 / (30,000).

showing only the Budget column. Frame 2: the Actual-to-date column fills in, package by package, each bar

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating the forecast register as a one-off exercise rather than a live document — an ETC assessed once
  and never revisited becomes stale within a reporting cycle or two (developed fully in 3.4.4).
- Reporting only the programme-level EAC and VAC without the package-level detail that explains it, leaving
  management unable to target corrective action.
- Confusing "forecast" with "budget" in conversation or in a report — asserting the forecast *is* the new
  budget without going through formal change control (KA 3.1, KA 5.4).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted tools can auto-populate the Actual-to-date column directly from the
cost ledger/ERP and flag any package whose ETC has not been reassessed within a defined number of reporting
periods — a genuinely useful "stale forecast" control (cross-reference KA 13.5). What such tools cannot do
is judge whether a given ETC figure is itself realistic: that remains a professional assessment by the
engineer or cost controller who owns the package, informed by the methods in 3.4.2–3.4.5. An AI-populated
register speeds up assembly; it does not validate the judgement inside it — **AI proposes; the professional
disposes.**

### 3.4.2 Forecasting methods: judgement, analogy and driver-based approaches

**Definition & purpose.** Because ETC (3.4.1) is a prediction, not a fact, project controls uses a small
family of recognised methods to derive it, each suited to different data availability and different stages
of a project. The main quantitative and semi-quantitative methods are:

| Method | Basis | Data needed | Typical use | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bottom-up (expert) re-estimate | The responsible engineer/control-account owner re-estimates the remaining scope directly | Detailed scope knowledge, current productivity | High-value or high-risk packages; late-stage forecasting | High |
| Analogous | Scale a comparable, more advanced package's actual outturn or unit rate | A genuinely comparable historical or parallel package | Early-stage, limited own data (cross-ref KA 3.2) | Low–medium |
| Driver-based (parametric) | ETC = a cost driver rate × remaining driver quantity | A reliable rate from actuals-to-date, and a known remaining quantity | Repetitive, measurable work (installation, excavation, fabrication) | Medium |
| Trend/time-series | Extrapolate a historical run-rate or trend forward | A sufficiently long, stable actual-cost or productivity history | Mature, steady-state phases of work (3.4.3) | Medium |
| Index-based (EVM) | Apply a cost/schedule performance index (CPI, SPI) to the remaining budget | A validated EV data set (PV, EV, AC) | Whole-project or whole-account forecasting once EVM is established (3.4.5, KA 6.3) | Low (mechanical), once EVM is set up |

**The underlying principle.** No single method is universally best; each embeds a different assumption about
what the future will look like, and a professional forecast is one where the method — and its assumption —
is stated, not left implicit. Bottom-up re-estimation is generally the most accurate for a single package
because it uses current, specific knowledge, but it is expensive to repeat every reporting period across an
entire project; index-based and trend methods are cheap to recompute and well suited to frequent, whole-
project re-forecasting (3.4.4), but they assume that a past pattern (a performance index, a run-rate) will
hold into the future, which is not always true. Driver-based forecasting sits between the two: it is
disciplined and reproducible like an index method, but — because it is built from a physical driver rather
than a financial ratio — it can expose a genuine rate change that a purely financial extrapolation might
mask.

**Formula.** `ETC (driver-based) = remaining driver quantity × current driver rate`, where the current driver
rate is normally `AC to date ÷ quantity completed to date` (USD per unit of the driver, e.g. USD/metre,
USD/m³, USD/drawing).

**Example 3.4.2 — driver-based forecast for a pipe-installation package.**

*Scenario.* A pipe-installation package has a total scope of 800 metres, originally budgeted at a rate of
$1,125 per metre (`BAC = 800 m × $1,125/m = $900,000`). To date, 450 metres have been installed at an actual
cost of $540,000.

*Required.* The current actual rate per metre, the driver-based ETC and EAC for the remaining 350 metres,
and the resulting VAC.

*Working.*
Actual rate to date: `$540,000 ÷ 450 m = $1,200/m`.
Remaining quantity: `800 m − 450 m = 350 m`.
`ETC = 350 m × $1,200/m = $420,000`.
`EAC = AC + ETC = $540,000 + $420,000 = $960,000`.
`VAC = BAC − EAC = $900,000 − $960,000 = ($60,000)`.

*Answer.* **EAC = $960,000; VAC = ($60,000), adverse** — a $1,200/metre actual rate against a $1,125/metre
budgeted rate, carried forward across the whole 800 m scope.

*Interpretation.* The driver-based method has isolated the true cause of the forecast overrun: a **rate**
increase (6.7% above the budgeted $1,125/m), not a change in scope (the 800 m quantity is unchanged). A
purely financial trend extrapolated from spend alone might not have separated "spending faster because
costs are rising" from "spending faster because more metres are being installed" — the driver-based method
does, because it forecasts through the physical quantity, not around it.

**Mini-case — three methods, one control account, three different answers.** A separate $400,000-budget
control account is forecast independently by three specialists, each using a different method: the
bottom-up (expert) re-estimate gives $420,000; the analogous method (scaled from a comparable completed
package) gives $460,000; the index-based method (BAC/CPI, computed mechanically from the account's EV data)
gives $395,000. The spread across the three — $65,000, or roughly 16% of the lowest figure — is itself
informative: it signals genuine uncertainty about this account that a single-method forecast would have
hidden behind one falsely precise number. Rather than picking whichever figure is most convenient to report,
the professional's task is to understand *why* the methods disagree (does the analogous comparator really
match this package's conditions? is the index-based CPI representative, or distorted by an early measurement
artefact — cross-reference KA 6.1's front-loading discussion?) before settling on a reported figure — a
reconciliation approach developed fully in 3.4.6.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using an analogous rate from a package that superficially resembles the one being forecast but differs in
  a material way (ground conditions, crew experience, weather exposure) — the comparability judgement is the
  entire value of the method, and if it is wrong, the forecast is wrong.
- Treating a driver-based rate calculated from a very small quantity-to-date as statistically reliable — a
  rate derived from only the first few units completed can be volatile and unrepresentative of steady-state
  productivity.
- Reporting a single method's output as "the forecast" without disclosing which method was used, making it
  impossible for a reviewer to judge the figure's reliability or compare it with a later reforecast made on a
  different basis.

**AI in this topic.** Machine-learning models can support driver-based and analogous forecasting at scale —
for example, by matching a new package against a library of historical analogues on multiple attributes
simultaneously (not just "similar description"), or by continuously recalculating driver rates as new
production data arrives (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). These tools widen the pool of comparators a
human could realistically review manually, which is a genuine strength. Their limit is that comparability
judgement — *is this historical package actually a fair analogue for this one?* — is a domain judgement an
algorithm cannot make reliably from pattern-matching alone; an AI-suggested analogue or driver rate is an
input to the professional's forecast, not a substitute for the professional's sign-off on it.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2 (estimate classes, analogous and parametric estimating methods reused here for
forecasting); KA 6.1 (EV measurement methods, relevant to why an index-based CPI can itself be distorted);
KA 3.4.3 (trend/time-series methods); KA 3.4.5 (index-based EAC in full).

### 3.4.3 Trend analysis and time-series forecasting

**Definition & purpose.** Trend analysis derives a forecast by extrapolating a pattern observed in
historical project data — most often cost run-rate, but also productivity, defect rates, or any other
metric that behaves consistently enough over time to be projected forward. The two simplest, most widely
used techniques are the **simple moving average** (which smooths short-term volatility by averaging the
most recent *n* periods) and the **linear trend** (which projects the average period-on-period change
forward, capturing directional momentum that a moving average can lag behind).

**The underlying principle.** Trend methods assume the immediate past is the best available guide to the
immediate future — a reasonable assumption for a steady-state phase of work with no known upcoming change,
and a poor one across a genuine step-change (new work front opening, a new subcontractor mobilising, a
change in weather season). The choice between a moving average and a linear trend is itself a judgement
about the data: a moving average is more robust to a single noisy period but responds slowly to a genuine,
sustained shift; a linear trend responds faster to a sustained shift but is more sensitive to being distorted
by a single unusual period.

**Formula(e).**
`Moving average forecast, MA(n) = (Σ actuals, most recent n periods) / n` (USD per period).
`Linear trend forecast for the next period = last actual + average period-on-period change`, where
`average period-on-period change = (Σ consecutive period differences) / (number of differences)` (USD per
period).

**Example 3.4.3 — trending a cost run-rate to a full-project forecast.**

*Scenario.* The Coastal Substation Upgrade is a 12-month project with BAC $2,400,000 (SAR 9,000,000 at an
illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1), budgeted on a simplified straight-line spread of $200,000 per month (the full
S-curve, resource-loaded time-phasing methodology is built in KA 3.3; a straight-line spread is used here
only so the forecasting arithmetic is not obscured by it). Actual monthly cost for the first six months has
been:

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Actual cost, period ($) | 150,000 | 180,000 | 210,000 | 230,000 | 270,000 | 310,000 | 1,350,000 |

*Required.* A three-month moving-average forecast for Month 7, and a trended full-project forecast assuming
that rate continues flat for the remaining six months.

*Working.*
`MA(3) = (230,000 + 270,000 + 310,000) / 3 = 810,000 / 3 = 270,000` per month.
Remaining six months (7–12) at a flat $270,000/month: `6 × 270,000 = 1,620,000`.
Trended EAC: `1,350,000 (actual, months 1–6) + 1,620,000 (trended, months 7–12) = 2,970,000`.

*Answer.* **Trended EAC ≈ $2,970,000** (SAR 11,137,500), against BAC $2,400,000 — an implied VAC of
approximately ($570,000).

*Interpretation.* The moving average smooths the month-to-month volatility (costs have in fact been rising
each month, not flat) to produce a single, stable run-rate. A linear trend on the same data tells a more
alarming story: the month-on-month increases are 30,000, 30,000, 20,000, 40,000, 40,000 — averaging
`160,000 / 5 = 32,000` per month — projecting Month 7 at `310,000 + 32,000 = 342,000`, well above the moving
average's flat $270,000. Because the data shows a genuine upward trend rather than random noise around a
flat level, the linear-trend method is arguably the more honest read here; the moving-average result is
shown first because it is the simpler, more commonly applied technique, but a professional should not apply
it mechanically without checking, as this example shows, whether the underlying data is actually trending.

**Mini-case — trending a schedule/productivity metric, not just cost.** Trend analysis is not limited to
cost. A separate work front records wall-erection productivity of 40, 45, 50 and 55 metres in four
successive weeks (190 m completed; average 47.5 m/week) against a remaining scope of 300 m. At the average
observed rate, the remaining work is projected to take `300 ÷ 47.5 ≈ 6.3 weeks` — in practice, planned as 7
working weeks, since partial weeks are rarely schedulable. This is the same trending logic applied to a
schedule/productivity metric rather than a cost run-rate, and is a common input to forecasting a completion
*date*, which then feeds cash flow forecasting (KA 3.5) and schedule control (KA 10.4).

shown. Series 1 (solid, neutral): actual monthly cost, Months 1–6. Series 2 (dashed, brand blue `#1D4ED8`):

**Common pitfalls.**
- Extrapolating a trend through a known, upcoming step-change (e.g. a new, more expensive work front about
  to open) without adjusting for it — trend methods have no awareness of information the analyst has but the
  historical data does not yet reflect.
- Using too short a window (e.g. a one-month "moving average") so the forecast simply chases the most recent
  data point and amplifies noise; using too long a window so a genuine recent shift is diluted away.
- Presenting a trended forecast with false precision (e.g. "$2,970,000" to the dollar) when the underlying
  method is a simplification — round appropriately and disclose the method and window used.

**AI in this topic.** Statistical and machine-learning forecasting models (from classical time-series
methods through to modern ML-based forecasting) can fit far more sophisticated trend and seasonality
patterns than a manual moving average or linear trend, and can process many packages or cost lines
simultaneously (cross-reference KA 13.5). Their outputs are still only as good as the historical data
they are trained or fitted on, and — like the manual methods above — they generally have no inherent
awareness of a known, upcoming change that has not yet appeared in the data (a new work front, a
contract variation, a resequenced schedule) unless that information is explicitly fed in as a separate
input. A model's trend line is a starting hypothesis for the professional to test against known project
information, not a forecast to be issued unreviewed.

run-rate); KA 3.4.4 (rolling forecasts — re-trending each period); KA 10.4 (schedule control, productivity
trending); KA 13.5 (ML-based forecasting across the lifecycle).

### 3.4.4 Rolling forecasts and periodic re-forecasting

**Definition & purpose.** A **rolling forecast** is a forecast that is refreshed at a fixed, defined cadence
(commonly monthly, sometimes quarterly) using the latest available actual cost and progress data, with each
new forecast formally superseding the previous one — as distinct from a forecast produced once (for example,
at project mid-point) and never revisited. This is a related but different idea from **rolling-wave
planning** (progressive elaboration of scope/schedule detail as a project moves forward — cross-reference
KA 8.2, KA 10.1): rolling-wave planning is about the *level of detail available to plan with*; a rolling
forecast is about *keeping the predicted outturn current*.

**The underlying principle.** Any of the methods in 3.4.1–3.4.3 and 3.4.5 produces a forecast that is only
as current as the data behind it — and both progress and cost performance change from period to period.
A forecast that is calculated once and left in a report for several months is not merely out of date; it
actively misleads, because a reader has no way of knowing, from the number alone, how stale it is. Rolling
forecasting solves this by making re-forecasting a routine, cadenced control-cycle activity rather than an
occasional, discretionary one, and by explicitly tracking the **forecast movement** between successive
cycles — a signal in its own right, independent of how far the current forecast sits from the original
budget.

**Formula.** `Forecast movement (ΔEAC) = EAC(t) − EAC(t−1)` (USD; a positive ΔEAC signals a worsening
forecast — sometimes called "EAC creep" when it recurs adversely over several successive cycles).

**Example 3.4.4 — a rolling forecast update, Month 6 to Month 7.**

*Scenario.* The Coastal Substation Upgrade (Example 3.4.3; BAC $2,400,000) is tracked using earned value.
At the Month-6 data date: `PV = $1,200,000`, `EV = $1,080,000`, `AC = $1,350,000`, giving
`CPI = EV/AC = 1,080,000/1,350,000 = 0.80` and `SPI = EV/PV = 1,080,000/1,200,000 = 0.90` (the full EV
measurement mechanics behind these three figures are built up in KA 6.1). Using the index-based method
`EAC = BAC/CPI` (developed fully in 3.4.5), `EAC(6) = 2,400,000/0.80 = $3,000,000`.

One month later, Month-7 actuals are in: an additional $330,000 was spent during the month (cumulative
`AC(7) = 1,350,000 + 330,000 = 1,680,000`), the schedule baseline has advanced to `PV(7) = 1,400,000`, and
progress measurement assesses cumulative `EV(7) = 1,260,000`.

*Required.* The revised CPI and SPI at Month 7, the rolling-updated EAC (same method), and the forecast
movement since Month 6.

*Working.*
`CPI(7) = EV(7)/AC(7) = 1,260,000/1,680,000 = 0.75`.
`SPI(7) = EV(7)/PV(7) = 1,260,000/1,400,000 = 0.90`.
`EAC(7) = BAC/CPI(7) = 2,400,000/0.75 = 3,200,000`.
`ΔEAC = EAC(7) − EAC(6) = 3,200,000 − 3,000,000 = +200,000`.
`VAC(7) = BAC − EAC(7) = 2,400,000 − 3,200,000 = (800,000)`, versus `VAC(6) = (600,000)`.

*Answer.* **EAC(7) = $3,200,000 (SAR 12,000,000); the forecast has worsened by $200,000 in a single
reporting cycle**, and VAC has deteriorated from ($600,000) to ($800,000).

*Interpretation.* Schedule performance held steady (SPI unchanged at 0.90), but cost efficiency fell (CPI
0.80 → 0.75), and — because this index-based method ties EAC directly to CPI — the forecast moved adversely
by $200,000 even though nothing about the *original* budget or scope changed. This is precisely the movement
a rolling forecast is designed to surface early: a reader who only saw Month 6's $3,000,000 forecast, and
was not shown Month 7's $3,200,000, would miss that the project's trajectory is actively deteriorating, not
merely "over budget by a stable amount." For comparison, the simpler `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)` method moves
from `EAC(6) = 1,350,000 + (2,400,000 − 1,080,000) = 2,670,000` to
`EAC(7) = 1,680,000 + (2,400,000 − 1,260,000) = 2,820,000` — a smaller movement (+$150,000) because that
method does not project the CPI shortfall onto the remaining work at all (full comparison of methods:
3.4.5).

**Mini-case — choosing a re-forecast cadence.** A monthly rolling forecast gives management the earliest
possible warning of a trend such as the one above, but it also has a cost: each cycle consumes control-
account owners' time, and a cadence that is too frequent relative to how quickly the underlying work
actually changes can produce forecast "noise" — small movements that reflect measurement timing rather than
real change, distracting from genuine signals. A quarterly cadence is cheaper to run and smooths out that
noise, but risks a three-month blind spot on exactly the kind of deterioration shown above. Common practice
ties the cadence to project risk and value: monthly (or even more frequent) rolling reforecasting for
high-value, high-risk, or currently underperforming accounts; quarterly for stable, low-risk, on-track work
— a risk-based cadence, not a single fixed rule for the whole project (cross-reference KA 12.1).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Carrying forward a prior period's EAC unchanged because "nothing major happened," without actually
  recomputing it from the current data — a rolling forecast that isn't actually recomputed each cycle is not
  a rolling forecast.
- Reporting only the current EAC without the prior period's figure, hiding the forecast movement (ΔEAC) that
  is often more informative to management than the absolute VAC.
- Re-forecasting so frequently, or on such thin data, that the reported EAC oscillates from noise rather than
  real change, eroding management's confidence in every forecast, including the ones that matter.

**AI in this topic.** Automated pipelines can recompute EAC, VAC and ΔEAC for every control account the
moment new actual-cost or progress data lands in the source system, effectively making "rolling" forecasting
continuous rather than merely monthly, and can flag accounts with a sustained adverse ΔEAC trend across
several cycles for priority review (cross-reference KA 13.5). This shortens the detection window
considerably. It does not remove the need for a human review before a revised forecast is issued externally:
an automated recompute inherits every weakness of its inputs (an un-accrued cost, a front-loaded EV
measurement — KA 6.1), and a real, one-off event (a single large invoice landing in one period) can produce
a misleading ΔEAC spike that a professional would recognise and explain, but an automated feed would simply
report.

### 3.4.5 The Estimate at Completion (EAC) family and forecasting logic

**Definition & purpose.** The **Estimate at Completion (EAC)** is the forecast total cost of the project —
the specific case of "the forecast" (3.4.1) produced using the project's earned value data set (PV, EV, AC —
fully defined in KA 6.1) rather than a general estimating method. Because different assumptions about the
remaining work are each individually defensible, EVM recognises a small *family* of EAC formulae rather than
a single correct one; which formula is most appropriate depends on what has been learned about the project's
performance so far and what is believed about the remaining work. This topic introduces the family and the
basic mechanics of each; the full comparative treatment — a multi-scenario worked example on a single
project comparing every method side by side, the assumptions each one embeds, and guidance on when each is
defensible — is developed in depth in **KA 6.3**. Two further canonical measures complete the "forecasting
logic" toolkit: **ETC** (Estimate to Complete, `= EAC − AC`, already introduced in 3.4.1) and **TCPI**
(To-Complete Performance Index), which asks the reverse question — *given* a target cost (BAC, or a chosen
EAC), what cost efficiency must the remaining work now achieve to hit it?

**The underlying principle.** Each EAC formula makes an explicit assumption about the remaining work:

- `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)` assumes the **remaining work will be completed at budget** — i.e. that whatever
  caused any variance so far was a one-off, and normal (budgeted) performance will resume. This is the most
  optimistic of the three canonical formulae when cost performance to date has been poor.
- `EAC = BAC / CPI` assumes **past cost efficiency will continue** unchanged for the remainder of the
  project — the most commonly used formula in practice, because it is simple, mechanical and reasonably
  realistic when there is no specific evidence of imminent improvement or deterioration.
- `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) / (CPI × SPI)` assumes **both cost and schedule pressure continue** — used where
  schedule slippage is itself expected to drive further cost growth (e.g. extended time-related overheads),
  making it typically the most conservative (highest) of the three on a project that is behind schedule as
  well as over cost.

A fourth, non-formulaic route — the **bottom-up re-estimate** (3.4.2) — sums a fresh ETC assessed
package-by-package and is often the most accurate for a single account, though the most effort-intensive to
repeat for a whole project every cycle (which is exactly why the mechanical, index-based formulae above are
so widely used for frequent, rolling, whole-project forecasting — 3.4.4).

**Formula(e).**
`EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)` — USD; assumes remaining work proceeds at budget.
`EAC = BAC / CPI` — USD; assumes past cost efficiency continues.
`EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) / (CPI × SPI)` — USD; assumes cost *and* schedule pressure continue.
`EAC = AC + ETC(bottom-up)` — USD; a fresh, package-by-package re-estimate of the remaining work.
`TCPI = (BAC − EV) / (BAC − AC)` — dimensionless index; the cost efficiency required on remaining work to
hit **BAC**. A variant, `TCPI = (BAC − EV) / (EAC − AC)`, asks the same question against a chosen **EAC**
instead of the original BAC.

**Example 3.4.5 — computing the EAC family at Month 6 (Coastal Substation Upgrade).**

*Scenario.* Continuing 3.4.4's Month-6 data set: `BAC = $2,400,000`, `PV = $1,200,000`, `EV = $1,080,000`,
`AC = $1,350,000`, `CPI = 0.80`, `SPI = 0.90`. Separately, the control-account owners have completed a
bottom-up re-estimate of the remaining work, assessing `ETC(bottom-up) = $1,500,000`.

*Required.* EAC under all four methods, and TCPI to hit BAC.

*Working.*
`EAC (at budget) = 1,350,000 + (2,400,000 − 1,080,000) = 1,350,000 + 1,320,000 = 2,670,000`.
`EAC (CPI) = 2,400,000 / 0.80 = 3,000,000`.
`EAC (CPI × SPI) = 1,350,000 + 1,320,000 / (0.80 × 0.90) = 1,350,000 + 1,320,000/0.72 = 1,350,000 +
1,833,333 = 3,183,333` (rounded to the nearest dollar).
`EAC (bottom-up) = 1,350,000 + 1,500,000 = 2,850,000`.
`TCPI (to BAC) = (2,400,000 − 1,080,000) / (2,400,000 − 1,350,000) = 1,320,000 / 1,050,000 = 1.26` (125.7%).

*Answer.* **Four defensible EACs from the same underlying data: $2,670,000 / $2,850,000 / $3,000,000 /
$3,183,333 — a spread of over half a million dollars — and a TCPI-to-BAC of 1.26.**

*Interpretation.* The TCPI result is the key sanity check on the most optimistic formula: hitting the
original $2,400,000 BAC would require the remaining work to be executed at 126% of budgeted efficiency —
26% *better* than the 0.80 CPI actually demonstrated over the first six months. Absent a specific, credible
corrective action already under way, that level of improvement is unlikely, which is why the "remaining work
at budget" formula ($2,670,000) is generally the least defensible of the four here, despite being the lowest
number. As a consistency check: recomputing TCPI against the CPI-based EAC instead of BAC gives
`TCPI (to EAC=3,000,000) = 1,320,000 / (3,000,000 − 1,350,000) = 1,320,000/1,650,000 = 0.80` — exactly equal
to the current CPI. This is not a coincidence: by construction, the `EAC = BAC/CPI` formula assumes the
remaining work continues at *exactly* today's efficiency, so the efficiency required to hit that EAC is,
tautologically, today's CPI. The full guidance on selecting between these methods — including scenarios
where each is the more defensible choice — is developed with a further multi-scenario example in KA 6.3.

**Mini-case — which EAC to report?** With four defensible figures in hand, a project manager under pressure
to show a favourable position asks the cost controller to report the lowest, $2,670,000 ("remaining work at
budget"), to the sponsor. The cost controller's professional position is that, given a demonstrated CPI of
0.80 sustained over six months and no specific, evidenced corrective action yet in place, the "at budget"
assumption is not currently supportable — either the CPI-based figure ($3,000,000) or, given that a genuine
bottom-up re-estimate has actually been done, the bottom-up figure ($2,850,000) is the more defensible number
to report, with the method disclosed. Selecting the most favourable of several mechanically correct EACs,
without evidence that its underlying assumption actually holds, is a form of reporting bias, not a
forecasting judgement — the professional's obligation is to the figure best supported by the evidence, not
the one most comfortable to deliver (cross-reference KA 4.3's discussion of honest management reporting).

**Fig 3.4.5 — Four EAC methods, one EV data set.**
Chart type: bar chart. X-axis: the four methods ("At budget," "Bottom-up," "CPI," "CPI × SPI"). Y-axis: USD.
A horizontal reference line at BAC $2,400,000 (grey, labelled). Bars in brand blue `#1D4ED8`, each labelled
with its value and, in a footnote row, its underlying assumption.

*Underlying data:* At budget $2,670,000; Bottom-up $2,850,000; CPI $3,000,000; CPI×SPI $3,183,333; BAC
reference line $2,400,000.

**Animation 3.4.5 — "One data set, four forecasts" [digital-only].** Frame 1: the shared EV inputs (BAC,
EV, AC, CPI, SPI) appear as a small data card. Frame 2: four arrows fan out from the card, each landing on

Narration cue: "Same data. Same arithmetic rules. Four different assumptions about the future — and four
different answers. The forecast is only as good as the assumption you're willing to defend."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting an EAC without stating which formula (and therefore which assumption) produced it — a bare
  number invites the reader to assume it is *the* forecast, rather than one of several defensible views.
- Defaulting to `EAC = BAC/CPI` in every situation because it is the most common formula, without checking
  whether schedule pressure (captured by `CPI × SPI`) is also a material driver of remaining cost.
- Treating TCPI above roughly 1.10–1.15 as a purely academic result rather than as a practical early warning
  that the target it was computed against (often BAC) is no longer realistic without a demonstrable change
  in performance.

**AI in this topic.** Predictive-analytics and ML-based EAC tools can compute all of the formulae above (and
often additional, more sophisticated statistical forecasts) automatically and continuously as new EV data
arrives, and can highlight which historical projects' performance patterns a current project's trajectory
most resembles (cross-reference KA 13.5). These tools materially reduce the mechanical effort in producing
and comparing EACs. They do not remove the professional judgement in *selecting and defending* a reported
figure, particularly the kind of governance judgement illustrated in the mini-case above — an algorithm has
no stake in, and no visibility into, the organisational pressure to report a favourable number, and cannot
itself distinguish a genuine improvement in performance from an unsubstantiated hope of one. **AI proposes
the family of numbers; the professional disposes by defending one, with its assumption stated.**

worked comparison, assumptions and limits, in depth)**; KA 6.4 (integrating cost and schedule forecasting);

### 3.4.6 Forecast accuracy, range forecasting and reconciling multiple forecasts

**Definition & purpose.** **Forecast accuracy** is how closely a prior forecast, once the relevant work is
actually complete, matched the true outturn — tracked as a **forecast error** (`Actual outturn − Forecast`,
in USD, and as a percentage of the actual outturn). **Range forecasting** presents a forecast as a spread
(optimistic / most likely / pessimistic, or a probabilistic range from quantitative risk analysis —
cross-reference KA 12.2) rather than a single point, making the genuine uncertainty in any forecast visible
rather than hidden behind false precision.

**The underlying principle.** Every method in this KA (3.4.1–3.4.5) can, on the same underlying account,
produce a different number, as the mini-case in 3.4.2 and the four-method comparison in 3.4.5 both showed.
Rather than treating that divergence as a problem to be hidden by picking one figure, mature forecasting
practice treats it as information: a wide spread across methods signals genuine uncertainty (and warrants a
range or a probability-weighted forecast in the report), while a narrow spread signals converging evidence
(and supports more confidence in a single reported figure). One simple, widely used way to blend an
optimistic, most-likely and pessimistic figure into a single expected value is the three-point
(PERT-type) formula, borrowed from schedule-risk estimating (cross-reference KA 10.3, KA 12.2), which
weights the most-likely estimate four times as heavily as either extreme.

**Formula.** `E = (O + 4M + P) / 6` — USD; where O = optimistic forecast, M = most-likely forecast, P =
pessimistic forecast. `Forecast error = Actual outturn − Forecast` (USD); `Forecast error % = Forecast error
/ Actual outturn` (%).

**Example 3.4.6 — reconciling three method-based forecasts, and checking accuracy after the fact.**

*Scenario.* Returning to the $400,000-budget control account from 3.4.2's mini-case, the three method-based
forecasts to complete were: index-based (BAC/CPI) $395,000 (optimistic of the three), bottom-up re-estimate
$420,000 (treated as most likely, being the most current, specific assessment), and analogous $460,000
(pessimistic of the three). Six months later, the package is complete; its true final cost was $430,000.

*Required.* The PERT-type blended forecast, and the forecast error (in USD and %) of each of the three
original methods and the blend, against the actual outturn.

*Working.*
`E = (395,000 + 4×420,000 + 460,000) / 6 = (395,000 + 1,680,000 + 460,000) / 6 = 2,535,000 / 6 = 422,500`.
Forecast errors against the actual outturn of $430,000:
Bottom-up: `430,000 − 420,000 = 10,000` (`10,000/430,000 = 2.3%`, forecast slightly low).
Analogous: `430,000 − 460,000 = (30,000)` (`(30,000)/430,000 = (7.0%)`, forecast too high).
Index-based: `430,000 − 395,000 = 35,000` (`35,000/430,000 = 8.1%`, forecast low).
PERT blend: `430,000 − 422,500 = 7,500` (`7,500/430,000 = 1.7%`, forecast slightly low).

*Answer.* **Blended forecast E = $422,500. The blend's error (1.7%) is smaller than any individual method's
error (2.3%, 7.0% and 8.1%).**

*Interpretation.* No single method was closest by chance alone across enough packages to be relied on
consistently — but blending the three, weighting the most-likely estimate most heavily, produced the most
accurate result on this account. This is the practical case for range/blended forecasting: it is not merely
a presentation style for communicating uncertainty (though it is that too), it can genuinely improve accuracy
by not depending on any single method's assumption holding exactly true. A single instance is illustrative,
not proof — an organisation building genuine confidence in a blending approach should track forecast accuracy
across many packages and periods, which is itself good forecasting governance.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Comparing a forecast against the wrong outturn baseline (e.g. an original estimate rather than the
  specific forecast being accuracy-checked), which produces a misleading accuracy assessment.
- Treating a single accurate (or inaccurate) forecast instance as proof that a method is reliable (or not) —
  accuracy tracking requires a sample across multiple periods and packages before a pattern can be trusted.
- Presenting a range forecast as if the optimistic and pessimistic ends were equally likely outcomes, rather
  than genuine bounds around a most-likely case — inviting a reader to anchor on whichever end suits them.

**AI in this topic.** Forecast-accuracy tracking is a natural fit for automation: a system can log every
issued forecast against its eventual outturn, across hundreds of packages and periods, and surface which
methods (or which forecasters) are systematically over- or under-forecasting — a genuinely powerful
governance tool that would be impractical to maintain manually at scale (cross-reference KA 13.5, KA 13.6).
ML-based forecasting models can also learn organisation-specific bias patterns and adjust for them. The
governance limit is that a model trained on historical forecast-versus-actual patterns will, by construction,
reproduce and can even amplify past biases and blind spots if they are not actively checked for; and a
statistically well-calibrated blended forecast is still a professional's responsibility to defend, not an
output to be issued without review, particularly where it feeds a reported EAC (3.4.5) or a cash flow
forecast with funding implications (KA 3.5).

**Cross-references.** KA 3.4.2 (the three original method forecasts blended here); KA 3.4.5 (EAC and its

### Key terms

- **Forecast / Latest Forecast** — a current, periodically updated prediction of a project's likely final
  outturn, distinct from the (fixed) budget and the (pre-project) estimate.
- **Estimate at Completion (EAC)** — the forecast total cost of the project; several formulae exist, each
  embedding a different assumption about the remaining work (basic methods here; full family in KA 6.3).
- **Estimate to Complete (ETC)** — the forecast cost of the remaining work only; `ETC = EAC − AC`.
- **Variance at Completion (VAC)** — the forecast gap against the original budget; `VAC = BAC − EAC`.
- **To-Complete Performance Index (TCPI)** — the cost efficiency required on the remaining work to hit a
  target (BAC or a chosen EAC); `TCPI = (BAC − EV)/(BAC − AC)` or `(BAC − EV)/(EAC − AC)`.
- **Forecast register** — a tracked table of Budget, Actual-to-date and Latest Forecast (EAC), by package
  and in total.
- **Bottom-up (expert) re-estimate** — a fresh, package-by-package re-estimate of the remaining work.
- **Analogous forecasting** — scaling a comparable historical or parallel package's outturn or rate.
- **Driver-based (parametric) forecasting** — `ETC = remaining driver quantity × current driver rate`.
- **Trend/time-series forecasting** — extrapolating a historical run-rate (moving average or linear trend).
- **Rolling forecast** — a forecast refreshed at a fixed cadence, each cycle formally superseding the last.
- **Forecast movement (ΔEAC)** — the change in EAC between successive forecast cycles; "EAC creep" describes
  a sustained adverse trend across cycles.
- **Range forecasting / three-point estimate** — presenting a forecast as optimistic/most-likely/pessimistic
  (or a probabilistic range) rather than a single point; `E = (O + 4M + P)/6`.
- **Forecast accuracy / forecast error** — how closely a prior forecast matched the eventual actual outturn.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 3.4.1 [topic 3.4.1, level: recall]**
Which statement correctly distinguishes a budget from a forecast in project controls?
A. A forecast is the authorised baseline; a budget is a live, updated prediction
B. **A budget is the authorised, time-phased baseline; a forecast is a live, periodically updated prediction
of the likely outturn** ✔
C. A budget and a forecast are two names for the same figure
D. A forecast can only be produced once, at project completion
*Rationale:* The budget (BAC/PV) is fixed until formally changed; the forecast (EAC) is expected to move as
new data arrives. A reverses the definitions; C wrongly conflates them; D contradicts the whole rationale
for rolling, periodic forecasting (3.4.4).

**MCQ 3.4.2 [topic 3.4.3, level: application — numerical]**
A project's actual cost for the last three months was $230,000, $270,000 and $310,000. Using a three-month
simple moving average, what is the trend-based forecast for the next month?
A. **$270,000** ✔
B. $290,000
C. $310,000
D. $810,000
*Rationale:* `MA(3) = (230,000+270,000+310,000)/3 = 810,000/3 = 270,000`. C wrongly repeats the most recent
actual instead of averaging; D is the un-divided three-month sum; B is not a correct average of the series.

**MCQ 3.4.3 [topic 3.4.5, level: application — numerical]**
A project has BAC $2,400,000 and CPI 0.80. Using the assumption that past cost performance will continue for
the remaining work, what is the EAC?
A. $1,920,000
B. **$3,000,000** ✔
C. $2,400,000
D. $12,000,000
*Rationale:* `EAC = BAC/CPI = 2,400,000/0.80 = 3,000,000`. A wrongly multiplies BAC by CPI instead of
dividing; C ignores CPI entirely (assumes no variance); D divides by (1 − CPI), a common formula confusion.

**MCQ 3.4.4 [topic 3.4.5, level: analysis]**
A project's CPI to date is 0.80, and its TCPI (to-complete performance index) required to hit the original
BAC is 1.26. What does this combination best indicate?
A. The project is certain to finish within BAC, because both figures are reasonably close to 1.0
B. **Hitting the original BAC would require the remaining work to be executed roughly 26% more efficiently
than the average performance achieved to date, which is unlikely without a demonstrated corrective action**
✔
C. The project has already exceeded BAC, so no further forecasting is required
D. A TCPI above 1.0 confirms that `EAC = BAC/CPI` is the only correct forecasting method to use
*Rationale:* TCPI 1.26 against a demonstrated CPI of 0.80 is a realism check flagging that the "at-budget"
EAC assumption is unlikely to hold (3.4.5). A misreads the numbers as both being reassuring; C is factually
wrong — BAC has not been exceeded, only EV/AC performance is behind; D over-generalises what TCPI shows.

**MCQ 3.4.5 [topic 3.4.4, level: application]**
Between two successive monthly re-forecasts, a project's EAC (calculated as BAC/CPI) rises from $3,000,000
to $3,200,000, while BAC remains unchanged at $2,400,000. What does this rolling-forecast movement indicate?
A. The project's cost performance improved during the period
B. **The project's cost performance deteriorated during the period, and the forecast has moved further from
BAC (a more adverse VAC)** ✔
C. The forecast is now more accurate simply because it is based on more recent data
D. BAC must automatically be revised to match the new EAC
*Rationale:* A rising `BAC/CPI` EAC means CPI has fallen — cost efficiency has worsened — and VAC (BAC−EAC)
has become more adverse (3.4.4). A is the opposite of what occurred; C wrongly assumes recency guarantees
accuracy; D confuses the forecast with the baseline, which only changes through formal change control (KA
3.1, KA 5.4).

**MCQ 3.4.6 [topic 3.4.6, level: application — numerical]**
A control account has three forecasts to complete: optimistic $395,000, most likely $420,000, pessimistic
$460,000. Using the three-point (PERT-type) formula `E = (O + 4M + P)/6`, what is the blended forecast?
A. $425,000
B. **$422,500** ✔
C. $420,000
D. $458,333
*Rationale:* `E = (395,000 + 4×420,000 + 460,000)/6 = 2,535,000/6 = 422,500`. C wrongly reports the
most-likely figure alone, ignoring the optimistic/pessimistic inputs; A and D are arithmetic slips in
applying the weighting or the divisor.

### Self-check questions

1. In your own words, explain the difference between a budget, an estimate and a forecast, and explain why a
   rolling forecast is more useful to management than a forecast produced once, at project mid-point, and
   never revisited.
2. A control account's cumulative actual cost for the last four months was $180,000, $210,000, $230,000 and
   $270,000. Using a two-month simple moving average of the two most recent months, calculate the
   trend-based forecast for the next month.
3. A project has BAC $1,000,000, EV $400,000 and AC $500,000. Calculate CPI, then the EAC using the
   `EAC = BAC/CPI` method, then VAC. State whether the VAC is favourable or adverse.

**Answers**

1. The estimate is the pre-project, largely one-off prediction made before or at the start of work; the
   budget is the authorised, time-phased baseline (BAC/PV), fixed until formally changed through change
   control; the forecast (EAC) is a live, non-authorised prediction of the likely outturn, expected to be
   updated as actual performance data accrues. A forecast produced once and never revisited becomes stale
   and can actively mislead a reader who has no way of knowing how out of date it is; a rolling forecast,
   refreshed at a fixed cadence, keeps the prediction current and also makes the *forecast movement* (ΔEAC)
   between cycles visible — itself a valuable early-warning signal (3.4.4).
2. `MA(2) = (230,000 + 270,000)/2 = 500,000/2 = 250,000`.
3. `CPI = EV/AC = 400,000/500,000 = 0.80`. `EAC = BAC/CPI = 1,000,000/0.80 = 1,250,000`.
   `VAC = BAC − EAC = 1,000,000 − 1,250,000 = (250,000)` — **adverse**.
