# Domain 1 — Foundations of Accounting for Project Controls

## KA 1.2 — Components of the financial statements

KA 1.1 built the ledger from first principles and proved it mechanically in balance through the trial
balance. This Knowledge Area takes that trial balance and turns it into the artefacts a project controls
professional actually reads, presents and is measured against. Under IAS 1 *Presentation of Financial
Statements*, a complete set of financial statements comprises the Statement of Financial Position, the
Statement of Profit or Loss and Other Comprehensive Income, the Statement of Changes in Equity, the
Statement of Cash Flows (governed in detail by IAS 7 *Statement of Cash Flows*), and the notes — together
with comparative information for the preceding period. Each statement answers a different question about
the same underlying entity: what it owns and owes at a point in time, how it performed over a period, why
its equity moved, and where its cash actually went. None stands alone, and a professional who reads only
one in isolation will misread the entity's position. This chapter treats the four statements and their
notes as one connected system — exactly as KA 1.1 treated the journal, ledger and trial balance as one
system — and closes by building a complete, simple set of statements from a single trial balance, precisely
as a professional would at a period end. It continues the worked case of **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**
from KA 1.1, using its trial balance at 31 March 2026 (Example 1.1.5) as the single source from which every
statement in this chapter is built.

### 1.2.1 The Statement of Financial Position

**Definition and purpose.** Under IAS 1, the **Statement of Financial Position** (formerly, and still
commonly, called the "balance sheet") reports an entity's assets, liabilities and equity at a single point
in time — the reporting date. It is the accounting equation (§1.1.1) presented in a structured, classified
format for users — investors, lenders, clients, sureties — who need to assess an entity's resources,
obligations and net worth without access to the underlying ledger.

**The underlying principle — classification into current and non-current.** IAS 1 requires assets and
liabilities to be presented as a **classified** statement, separating **current** from **non-current**,
unless a liquidity-based presentation is more relevant (common for banks and similar institutions —
awareness only). An asset is current if the entity expects to realise it, or intends to sell or consume it,
within its normal operating cycle or within twelve months of the reporting date, or if it is cash or a cash
equivalent; everything else is non-current. A liability is current if the entity expects to settle it
within its normal operating cycle or within twelve months of the reporting date, **or if the entity does
not have an unconditional right to defer settlement for at least twelve months** at the reporting date;
everything else is non-current. That last clause matters enormously in practice (Example 1.2.1b): a loan
is classified by the entity's *contractual right* at the reporting date, not by what it merely intends or
expects to happen. This distinction drives working-capital analysis, which a project controls professional
uses constantly: an entity can be profitable yet run short of cash if its current asset base (receivables,
work-in-progress) converts to cash more slowly than its current liabilities fall due.

**Formula.**

`Non-current assets + Current assets = Equity + Non-current liabilities + Current liabilities`

`Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities`

`Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities`

Where all figures are stated in the reporting currency at the reporting date; the current ratio is
expressed as a decimal (or an "x : 1" multiple) with no units — a ratio comfortably above 1.0 indicates
current assets exceed current liabilities.

**Example 1.2.1 — Meridian's classified Statement of Financial Position at 31 March 2026.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's trial balance at 31 March 2026 (Example 1.1.5) carries these closing balances:
Cash $605,000 Dr; Trade receivables $70,000 Dr; Materials inventory $20,000 Dr; Equipment at cost $150,000
Dr; Accumulated depreciation $2,500 Cr; Trade payables $100,000 Cr; Loan payable $200,000 Cr (a three-year
unsecured bank loan, interest-only in year one, principal repayable in full at maturity — no portion is
contractually due within twelve months of the reporting date); Share capital $500,000 Cr; and net profit
for the period of $42,500 (derived in Example 1.1.6, and re-derived from first principles in §1.2.2 below).

*Required.* Present a classified Statement of Financial Position and compute working capital and the
current ratio.

*Working.*

|---|---|

| Property, plant and equipment (150,000 − 2,500) | 147,500 |
| **Current assets** | |

| Cash and cash equivalents | 605,000 |

current ratio 6.95.

*Interpretation.* Total assets and total equity-plus-liabilities tie out exactly, as they must (§1.1.6). A
current ratio of 6.95 is unusually strong for a first month of trading — it mainly reflects the large cash
balance from the share issue and loan drawdown (Example 1.1.3) and would be expected to normalise as
Meridian deploys that cash into the project; a professional reading this figure should look behind it to
the cash flow statement (§1.2.3) rather than treat it as a steady-state liquidity measure.

**Example 1.2.1b — a covenant breach reclassifies debt.** Suppose Meridian's loan agreement contains a
financial covenant tested at each reporting date, and at 31 March 2026 the covenant is breached, giving the
lender an unconditional contractual right to demand repayment of the full $200,000 within twelve months —
even though the lender has given no indication it intends to call the loan. Under IAS 1, the classification
follows the *contractual right*, not management's expectation: the entire loan must now be presented as a
**current** liability. Revised current liabilities = 100,000 + 200,000 = **$300,000**. Revised working
capital = 695,000 − 300,000 = **$395,000**. Revised current ratio = 695,000 / 300,000 = **2.32**. Total
assets and total equity-plus-liabilities are unaffected ($842,500 either way) — only the current/non-current
split within liabilities changes.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Classifying a liability by management's *expectation* of settlement rather than
the entity's *contractual right* at the reporting date — Example 1.2.1b is a standard exam and real-world
trap. (2) Netting an asset against a related liability (e.g. showing cash net of an overdraft facility) when
gross presentation is required. (3) Treating a strong current ratio as proof of good liquidity management
without checking whether it is driven by a one-off event (a capital raise, a loan drawdown) rather than
sustainable operating cash generation (§1.2.3).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted close tools can auto-populate a classified Statement of Financial Position
directly from the trial balance and apply a standard current/non-current mapping rule to each account. The
governance limit is precisely Example 1.2.1b: automatic mapping typically relies on an account's *normal*
classification (a three-year loan defaults to non-current) and will not, on its own, detect a covenant
breach or other contractual event that changes the classification at a specific reporting date unless that
fact is fed to it explicitly. The professional must independently confirm the facts behind any borrowing
arrangement before sign-off — AI proposes the standard mapping; the professional discloses the actual
contractual position.

**Cross-references.** §1.1.1 (the underlying accounting equation); §1.2.6 (tying this statement to the
other three); KA 1.4 (provisions, a further liability category); KA 3.1 (contingency and management
reserve, relevant to reading working capital in a project context).

### 1.2.2 The Statement of Profit or Loss and Other Comprehensive Income

**Definition and purpose.** The **Statement of Profit or Loss and Other Comprehensive Income** reports an
entity's financial performance over a period — a flow, not a point-in-time stock. IAS 1 permits either a
single statement (profit or loss followed by other comprehensive income) or two statements (a separate
income statement, followed by a statement beginning with profit or loss and continuing into other
comprehensive income); this chapter uses the single-statement form throughout. **Profit or loss** captures
income and expenses recognised under the relevant standards (revenue under IFRS 15 — KA 2.2; expenses as
incurred or matched — KA 1.3). **Other comprehensive income (OCI)** captures specific items that IFRS
Accounting Standards require or permit to bypass profit or loss — for example, a revaluation surplus on
property under the IAS 16 revaluation model, remeasurements of a net defined benefit pension liability
under IAS 19, or foreign currency translation differences on a foreign operation under IAS 21. OCI items
are further split between those that will subsequently be **reclassified** ("recycled") to profit or loss
in a later period and those that will not.

**The underlying principle.** Profit or loss is deliberately narrower than total economic performance:
some gains and losses are considered too unrealised, too volatile, or too disconnected from the period's
operating activity to sit in the "headline" profit figure that drives dividends, bonuses and many
covenants, yet they are real changes in net assets that must still be reported somewhere. OCI is that
"somewhere." IAS 1 also allows two broad ways to classify expenses within profit or loss: **by nature**
(materials, labour, subcontract cost, depreciation — as used throughout this chapter, and typical for a
project-based contractor) or **by function** (cost of sales, administrative expenses, distribution costs —
more typical of a manufacturer or retailer with distinct functional departments). Both are IFRS-compliant;
neither changes the bottom-line profit figure, only how the expense lines above it are grouped, and an
entity presenting by function must disclose a by-nature breakdown of its expenses in the notes (§1.2.5).

**Formula.**

`Profit for the period = Income − Expenses` (as recognised under the applicable standards)

`Total comprehensive income for the period = Profit or loss for the period ± Other comprehensive income
for the period`

`Net profit margin = Profit for the period / Revenue`

**Example 1.2.2 — Meridian's Statement of Profit or Loss and OCI for the month ended 31 March 2026.**

*Scenario.* From the trial balance (Example 1.1.5): Contract revenue $220,000 Cr; Direct project cost
$60,000 Dr; Labour cost $45,000 Dr; Subcontract cost $70,000 Dr; Depreciation expense $2,500 Dr. Meridian
has no items of other comprehensive income in its first month.

*Required.* Present the statement by nature and compute the net profit margin.

*Working/Answer.*

| | USD |
|---|---|
| Contract revenue | 220,000 |
| Direct project cost | (60,000) |
| Labour cost | (45,000) |
| Subcontract cost | (70,000) |
| Depreciation expense | (2,500) |
| **Profit for the period** | **42,500** |
| Other comprehensive income | – |
| **Total comprehensive income for the period** | **42,500** |

Net profit margin = 42,500 / 220,000 = **19.3%**.

*Interpretation.* Because Meridian has no OCI items this month, profit for the period and total
comprehensive income are identical — this will not generally be true (Example 1.2.2b). Presented **by
function** instead, the same figures would show a single "Cost of sales" line of $60,000 + $45,000 +
$70,000 + $2,500 = $177,500 (all four costs relate directly to delivering the contract, so none is
administrative), giving the identical profit of $42,500 — the regrouping changes only what appears above
the profit line, never the profit itself.

**Example 1.2.2b — Other comprehensive income at a sister entity.** **Atlas Plant & Equipment Co.**, a
plant-hire entity in the same group, measures its owned freehold land under the IAS 16 revaluation model.
For the year, its profit or loss is $180,000. At the year end, an independent valuation increases the
land's carrying amount from a cost of $400,000 to a fair value of $425,000, giving a revaluation surplus of
$25,000, recognised directly in other comprehensive income (this class of surplus is never reclassified to
profit or loss — it transfers directly within equity, typically to retained earnings, only as the asset is
used or disposed of). Total comprehensive income = Profit or loss $180,000 + OCI $25,000 = **$205,000**.

*Interpretation.* A reader who looks only at "profit" of $180,000 misses $25,000 of genuine, IFRS-recognised
performance for the year; a reader who looks only at "total comprehensive income" of $205,000 without the
breakdown cannot tell how much of that is realised trading performance versus an unrealised valuation
movement. Both figures, and the split between them, matter to a professional assessing the entity.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Assuming OCI items are immaterial or optional to consider — total comprehensive
income is the complete performance picture and OCI can be significant (Example 1.2.2b). (2) Assuming
by-nature and by-function presentation give different bottom-line results — they never do; only the
expense groupings above profit differ. (3) Forgetting that some OCI items (e.g. certain cash flow hedge
reserves, or foreign operation translation differences on disposal) do reclassify to profit or loss later,
while others (IAS 16 revaluation surpluses, IAS 19 remeasurements) never do — this distinction affects
trend analysis of profit over time.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI tools are increasingly used to draft management commentary explaining
period-on-period movements in the Statement of Profit or Loss, and to suggest whether a specific gain or
loss belongs in profit or loss or in OCI based on pattern-matching against prior disclosures. The
classification of an item between profit or loss and OCI is a technical accounting judgement with real
consequences (it affects a headline KPI many bonuses and covenants are keyed to), so any AI-suggested
classification — especially for a new or unusual transaction type — requires the qualified accountant's
verification against the relevant standard before it is used in a report. AI drafts the commentary and
proposes a classification; the professional confirms it is technically correct.

depth); KA 2.4 (IAS 16 revaluation model, IAS 19 and IAS 21 awareness); §1.2.4 (profit and OCI both flow
into the Statement of Changes in Equity); §1.2.6.

### 1.2.3 The Statement of Cash Flows

**Definition and purpose.** The **Statement of Cash Flows**, governed by IAS 7, reports an entity's cash
inflows and outflows for a period, classified into **operating**, **investing** and **financing**
activities, and reconciles the opening balance of cash and cash equivalents to the closing balance. Its
purpose is to show liquidity and funding directly: profit (§1.2.2) is an accrual-based measure of
performance and can diverge sharply from cash movement in the same period, which matters intensely to a
project controls professional tracking project funding drawdown, lender covenants and payment timing.

**The underlying principle.** IAS 7 permits two methods for presenting operating activities. The **direct
method** shows major classes of gross cash receipts and payments (cash received from customers, cash paid
to suppliers and employees). The **indirect method** starts from profit for the period and adjusts it for
non-cash items (depreciation, amortisation, impairment) and for movements in working capital (an increase
in a current asset such as receivables or inventory is a *use* of cash; an increase in a current liability
such as payables is a *source* of cash). Both methods must arrive at exactly the same net cash from
operating activities — they are two views of the same underlying cash movements. Investing and financing
activities are always presented gross, regardless of which method is used for operating activities.

**Formula.**

`Net cash from operating activities (indirect) = Profit for the period + Non-cash adjustments ±
Changes in working capital`

`Net increase/(decrease) in cash and cash equivalents = Net cash from operating activities + Net cash
from/(used in) investing activities + Net cash from/(used in) financing activities`

`Closing cash and cash equivalents = Opening cash and cash equivalents + Net increase/(decrease) in cash`

**Example 1.2.3 — Meridian's Statement of Cash Flows for the month ended 31 March 2026, both methods.**

*Scenario.* Profit for the period $42,500 (Example 1.2.2); depreciation expense $2,500 (non-cash); trade
receivables increased from $0 to $70,000; materials inventory increased from $0 to $20,000; trade payables
increased from $0 to $100,000; equipment purchased for cash $150,000; share capital issued for cash
$500,000; bank loan drawn down in cash $200,000. From the ledger (Fig 1.1.4): cash received from the
client $150,000; cash paid to the materials supplier $50,000; cash paid for labour $45,000.

*Required.* Present the Statement of Cash Flows using the indirect method, and confirm the direct method
gives the same operating cash flow.

*Working — indirect method.*

| | USD |
|---|---|
| **Cash flows from operating activities** | |
| Profit for the period | 42,500 |
| Adjustment for depreciation (non-cash) | 2,500 |
| Operating profit before working capital changes | 45,000 |
| Increase in trade receivables | (70,000) |
| Increase in materials inventory | (20,000) |
| Increase in trade payables | 100,000 |
| **Net cash from operating activities** | **55,000** |
| **Cash flows from investing activities** | |
| Purchase of property, plant and equipment | (150,000) |
| **Net cash used in investing activities** | **(150,000)** |
| **Cash flows from financing activities** | |
| Proceeds from issue of share capital | 500,000 |
| Proceeds from bank loan | 200,000 |
| **Net cash from financing activities** | **700,000** |
| **Net increase in cash and cash equivalents** | **605,000** |
| Cash and cash equivalents at 1 March 2026 | 0 |
| **Cash and cash equivalents at 31 March 2026** | **605,000** |

*Working — direct-method cross-check on operating activities.* Cash received from customer $150,000 − cash
paid to supplier $50,000 − cash paid for labour $45,000 = **$55,000**, identical to the indirect-method
result.

*Answer.* Net cash from operating activities **$55,000** (both methods agree); net cash used in investing
**($150,000)**; net cash from financing **$700,000**; net increase in cash **$605,000**; closing cash
**$605,000**.

*Interpretation.* Closing cash of $605,000 ties exactly to the cash balance on Meridian's Statement of
Financial Position (Example 1.2.1) — this is not a coincidence but a required articulation (§1.2.6). Note
that operating cash flow ($55,000) is well below profit ($42,500 is actually lower — in this instance
operating cash flow exceeds profit, because the large increase in payables temporarily funded the
receivables and inventory build; the opposite pattern, shown next, is at least as common and more
dangerous).

**Example 1.2.3b — profit without cash.** A separate illustrative project entity reports profit for the
period of $50,000, with no depreciation or other non-cash adjustments. During the same period its trade
receivables increase by $90,000 (slow-paying client) with no corresponding movement in payables or
inventory. Net cash from operating activities = 50,000 − 90,000 = **($40,000)** — a negative operating cash
flow despite a healthy reported profit.

*Interpretation.* This is the single most important reading skill this topic teaches: a profitable project
can still run out of funding if its receivables (or unbilled work-in-progress) grow faster than cash comes
in. A project controls professional monitoring project funding should never treat a profit figure alone as
evidence the project is adequately funded — the cash flow statement, and specifically the working-capital
movements within it, is where that risk actually shows up.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Equating profit with cash — the central lesson of Example 1.2.3b. (2)

follow the classification of the underlying contract cash flow, or presenting interest paid inconsistently
period to period — IAS 7 allows a choice of classification for interest and dividends but requires
consistency and disclosure). (3) Presenting investing or financing activities net when gross presentation
is required (materially misstates the scale of investing/financing activity even though the net cash
movement is unaffected).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted cash flow forecasting tools can project short-term liquidity from bank
feeds, invoicing schedules and historical patterns, and automated bank-feed categorisation tools can
propose an operating/investing/financing split for each transaction at high volume. The governance limit:
categorisation of genuinely ambiguous items (is a retention payment operating or financing? is an
intercompany loan repayment investing or financing?) is a judgement call that depends on the substance of
the arrangement, not a pattern the model has necessarily seen correctly classified before — misclassified
items do not change the total cash movement but do distort the reader's understanding of *why* cash moved.
AI proposes the categorisation; the professional confirms the substance before the statement is finalised.

### 1.2.4 The Statement of Changes in Equity

**Definition and purpose.** The **Statement of Changes in Equity** reconciles the opening and closing
balance of each component of equity — share capital, each reserve, retained earnings — for the period. IAS
1 requires it to show, for each component: total comprehensive income for the period (profit or loss and
each item of OCI); the effects of any retrospective correction of a prior-period error or change in
accounting policy (IAS 8 — awareness level); and transactions with owners *in their capacity as owners* —
capital contributions, distributions (dividends), and any other movement between components (e.g. a
transfer from a revaluation reserve to retained earnings as a revalued asset is used).

**The underlying principle.** Equity is not a single number to be plugged as a balancing figure; it is a
set of distinct components, each with its own history, and every movement in every component must be
individually traceable. Total comprehensive income (§1.2.2) is the single bridge connecting the Statement
of Profit or Loss and OCI to this statement — nothing enters equity through profit or loss without first
appearing there, and nothing an owner contributes or receives as owner passes through profit or loss at
all.

**Formula.**

`Closing equity (component) = Opening equity (component) + Total comprehensive income attributable to
that component ± Owner contributions − Distributions to owners ± Transfers between components`

**Example 1.2.4 — Meridian's Statement of Changes in Equity for the month ended 31 March 2026.**

*Scenario.* Meridian was newly formed on 1 March 2026 (opening equity nil). During the month it issued
share capital of $500,000 (Example 1.1.1) and earned profit for the period of $42,500 (Example 1.2.2), with
no OCI and no distributions.

*Required.* Present the Statement of Changes in Equity.

*Working/Answer.*

| | Share capital (USD) | Retained earnings (USD) | Total equity (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance at 1 March 2026 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Issue of share capital | 500,000 | – | 500,000 |
| Profit for the period | – | 42,500 | 42,500 |
| **Balance at 31 March 2026** | **500,000** | **42,500** | **542,500** |

*Interpretation.* Closing total equity of $542,500 ties exactly to total equity on the Statement of
Financial Position (Example 1.2.1) — again, a required articulation, not a coincidence (§1.2.6).

**Example 1.2.4b — a second period, with a distribution.** Suppose that in April 2026 Meridian earns
further profit of $30,000 and its owners resolve to distribute a cash dividend of $10,000.

| | Share capital (USD) | Retained earnings (USD) | Total equity (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance at 31 March 2026 | 500,000 | 42,500 | 542,500 |
| Profit for the period | – | 30,000 | 30,000 |
| Dividends declared and paid | – | (10,000) | (10,000) |
| **Balance at 30 April 2026** | **500,000** | **62,500** | **562,500** |

Check: 42,500 + 30,000 − 10,000 = **$62,500** retained earnings; total equity = 500,000 + 62,500 =
**$562,500**.

*Interpretation.* A cash dividend reduces equity directly and never passes through the Statement of Profit
or Loss — Meridian's April profit of $30,000 is unaffected by the separate decision to distribute $10,000
of it. The dividend also appears as a financing cash outflow on the Statement of Cash Flows in the period
it is actually paid, a link the professional should always check (§1.2.3).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Presenting only the total-equity movement and losing the required by-component
detail (share capital vs each reserve vs retained earnings), which hides, for example, a share issue inside
what looks like retained profit growth. (2) Treating a dividend as *declared* the same as a dividend *paid*
— a declared but unpaid dividend reduces equity and creates a liability (cross-ref KA 1.4) but does not
reduce cash until settlement. (3) Running new share issues or buybacks through profit or loss — transactions
with owners in their capacity as owners never appear there.

**AI in this topic.** Automated reconciliation tools can roll forward each equity component period to
period and flag any movement that does not map to a recognised category (profit, OCI, contribution,
distribution, transfer) for investigation — a genuinely useful control. Classifying a new or unusual equity
transaction correctly in the first place (for example, a complex share-based payment arrangement, or a
capital contribution structured as a loan waiver) remains a professional judgement the tool cannot make
unaided. AI flags the unexplained movement; the professional determines its correct classification.

**Cross-references.** §1.1.1 (equity within the accounting equation); §1.2.2 (profit and OCI feeding this
statement); KA 1.4 (provisions and accruals, including a declared-but-unpaid dividend); §1.2.6.

### 1.2.5 Notes to the financial statements

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 1 requires **notes** presenting: the basis of preparation and the
significant accounting policies applied; information required by IFRS Accounting Standards that is not
presented elsewhere on the face of the primary statements; and additional information relevant to
understanding the statements, including the significant judgements management has made in applying
accounting policies and the key sources of estimation uncertainty that carry a significant risk of a
material adjustment to carrying amounts within the next financial year. Notes are not an optional appendix
— professionally and legally they are as much a part of "the financial statements" as the four primary
statements, and disclosures such as commitments, contingencies (cross-ref IAS 37, KA 1.4) and related-party
transactions frequently carry the highest audit and professional risk in the whole set.

**The underlying principle.** A single line on the face of a statement — "Property, plant and equipment,
$147,500" — cannot convey composition, movement, or the judgement behind that number. The note's job is to
make the figure auditable and understandable without cluttering the primary statement, and every note
should be traceable back to the line it supports.

**Formula (property, plant and equipment roll-forward).**

`Closing net book value = Opening net book value + Additions − Depreciation − Disposals ± Revaluation
movements`

**Example 1.2.5 — Meridian's property, plant and equipment note at 31 March 2026.**

*Scenario/Required.* Build the PPE note supporting the $147,500 net book value on the Statement of
Financial Position (Example 1.2.1), using the equipment purchase (Example 1.1.3, transaction 3) and the
month's depreciation charge (Example 1.1.3, transaction 11).

*Working/Answer.*

| | Cost (USD) | Accumulated depreciation (USD) | Net book value (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| At 1 March 2026 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Additions | 150,000 | – | |
| Depreciation charge for the period | – | 2,500 | |
| **At 31 March 2026** | **150,000** | **2,500** | **147,500** |

*Interpretation.* The note reconciles precisely to the single SFP line: 150,000 − 2,500 = 147,500. A reader
relying on the SFP alone would not know whether $147,500 represented a single new asset (as here) or a
larger fleet net of substantial accumulated depreciation — the note supplies that composition.

**Example 1.2.5b — a significant-judgement disclosure and its sensitivity.** Management assessed the site
equipment's useful life at five years (60 months), based on expected physical wear over the rail-signalling
programme, giving the month's $2,500 depreciation charge ($150,000 ÷ 60). This assessed useful life is
disclosed as a significant judgement because a plausible alternative assessment would materially change the
reported expense: if the useful life were instead assessed at four years (48 months), the monthly charge
would be $150,000 ÷ 48 = **$3,125** — a 25% increase in the depreciation line for an unchanged asset cost.

*Interpretation.* This is exactly the kind of sensitivity a professional may be asked to prepare for an
audit or management discussion: the note does not just state the policy, it lets a reader gauge how much
the reported figures depend on a judgement rather than an observable fact.

*Chart type:* two-bar bridge (cost bar and accumulated-depreciation bar), with net book value called out.
*Underlying data:* the table in Example 1.2.5, plus the derived net book value 147,500.

- *Frame 1:* the Statement of Financial Position with the "Property, plant and equipment 147,500" line

- *Frame 2:* the line "expands" downward into the PPE note table, cost and accumulated depreciation

- *Frame 3:* the useful-life judgement callout appears beside the depreciation column. Narration: "Every

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Boilerplate accounting-policy notes copied from a template without reflecting the
entity's actual policies — a recurring audit finding. (2) Omitting a judgement or estimate disclosure where
a reasonably possible change in that judgement would materially affect the figures (Example 1.2.5b). (3)
A note that does not reconcile numerically to the primary statement it supports, breaking traceability and
undermining confidence in the whole set.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI tools can draft standard accounting-policy note text from a policy library and
run automated disclosure checklists against an IFRS taxonomy, flagging notes that appear incomplete or that
fail to reconcile to the primary statements — a genuine efficiency gain, especially for routine, boilerplate
policy wording. The governance limit: AI-drafted text reflects generic or historical patterns, not
necessarily this entity's actual facts and judgements this period, and a note is only correct if it
describes what this entity actually did and decided. AI drafts the note; the professional verifies every
number ties out and every judgement disclosed is the one actually made.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.4 (provisions and contingencies, a major note category); KA 2.1 (qualitative
characteristics informing what belongs in a note); KA 2.4 (IAS 16, IFRS 16 detail); §1.2.1 (the SFP line
this note supports).

### 1.2.6 How the statements articulate — building a full set from a trial balance

**Definition and purpose.** **Articulation** describes how the four primary statements are mathematically
linked, rather than four independent reports about the same entity. This is the capstone skill of the
Knowledge Area: a project controls professional who can build (or review) a complete, internally consistent
set of statements from a single trial balance — and prove, not merely assert, that the statements articulate
— has demonstrated the same rigour a qualified accountant applies at every period close.

**The underlying principle — four linking identities.** Because every figure in every statement in this
chapter traces back to the same trial balance (Example 1.1.5), four equalities must hold simultaneously; if
any one fails, there is an error somewhere in the underlying postings or in how a trial balance line was
mapped to a statement, even if each statement's own totals look plausible in isolation.

**Formula (the four articulation checks).**

1. `Profit for the period (P&L) = Movement in retained earnings before distributions (SOCE)`
2. `Closing total equity (SOCE) = Total equity (SFP)`
3. `Closing cash and cash equivalents (SCF) = Cash and cash equivalents (SFP)`
4. `Total assets (SFP) = Total equity + Total liabilities (SFP)`

**Example 1.2.6 — Meridian's full set of statements for March 2026, articulated.**

*Scenario/Required.* Using the statements already built in §1.2.1–§1.2.4 from the single trial balance in
Example 1.1.5, confirm all four articulation checks.

*Working/Answer.*

| Check | Statement A | Statement B | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Profit flows to equity | P&L profit for the period: $42,500 | SOCE retained earnings movement: $42,500 | ✓ |
| 2. Equity ties out | SOCE closing total equity: $542,500 | SFP total equity: $542,500 | ✓ |
| 3. Cash ties out | SCF closing cash: $605,000 | SFP cash and cash equivalents: $605,000 | ✓ |
| 4. Equation holds | SFP total assets: $842,500 | SFP total equity + liabilities: $542,500 + $300,000 = $842,500 | ✓ |

*Interpretation.* All four checks pass using the figures already derived in §1.2.1–§1.2.4 — no plug, no
balancing adjustment was used anywhere in this chapter. This is precisely what a professional reperforms,
by hand or via system reconciliation, at every period close before a set of statements is released.

**Example 1.2.6b — a second, independent build (Al-Noor Site Services, SAR).** To confirm the method
generalises, KA 1.1's second worked entity, **Al-Noor Site Services** (a wholly SAR-functional entity — no
currency conversion implied), is built into a full set from its own trial balance (Example 1.1.6b: Cash
337,000 Dr; Tools 45,000 Dr; Capital 300,000 Cr; Service revenue 120,000 Cr; Wages expense 38,000 Dr).

| Statement | Key lines | Result (SAR) |
|---|---|---|
| P&L | Service revenue 120,000 − Wages expense 38,000 | Profit for the period **82,000** |
| SOCE | Opening equity 0 + Capital injected 300,000 + Profit 82,000 | Closing total equity **382,000** |
| SFP | Non-current assets (Tools) 45,000 + Current assets (Cash) 337,000 | Total assets **382,000** |
| SFP | Equity 382,000 + Liabilities 0 | Total equity and liabilities **382,000** |
| SCF | Operating 82,000 (no non-cash items, no working-capital movements) − Investing 45,000 + Financing 300,000 | Closing cash **337,000** |

Articulation checks: (1) profit 82,000 = SOCE movement 82,000 ✓; (2) SOCE closing equity 382,000 = SFP
total equity 382,000 ✓; (3) SCF closing cash 337,000 = SFP cash 337,000 ✓; (4) SFP total assets 382,000 =
equity 382,000 + liabilities 0 = 382,000 ✓.

*Interpretation.* A four-line, single-currency, no-liability entity satisfies exactly the same four
identities as Meridian's more complex first month. Note that Al-Noor's tools carry no depreciation charge
in this first, very short trading period; that is a reasonable simplification only if the acquisition date
and materiality justify it (§1.2.5's judgement-disclosure discussion applies equally here), and it is
flagged for SME confirmation below.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Forcing a "plug" figure to make statements tie out instead of tracing and
correcting the actual posting error — this echoes the suspense-account discipline in §1.1.5 and is worse
here because it can hide the error behind an apparently clean set of statements. (2) Preparing the SFP and
P&L without cross-checking to the SCF and SOCE: an error of principle that leaves the trial balance in
balance (§1.1.5's example) can still be caught here if it breaks one of the four articulation checks — for
instance, misclassifying a capital contribution as revenue would inflate profit and understate the SOCE's
"capital contributed" line by the same amount, and a careful reviewer comparing the two would catch it. (3)
Treating comparative-period figures as optional — IAS 1 requires a comparative period for a complete set of
statements; this chapter's single-period examples are for teaching clarity and are not a template for
omitting comparatives in practice.

**AI in this topic.** Automated "smart close" tools can generate a full draft set of financial statements
directly from a trial balance or ERP extract and run articulation checks like those above automatically, at
far greater speed and volume than a manual reperformance. This is a mature and valuable use case. It does
not remove the professional's responsibility to verify that the trial balance itself was correctly derived
(KA 1.1) and that each trial-balance line was mapped to the correct statement line and classification —
particularly judgement calls such as the current/non-current split (§1.2.1) or the by-nature/by-function
presentation choice (§1.2.2) — before the statements are certified as a true and fair representation of the
entity. AI assembles and checks the arithmetic; the professional certifies the result.

**Cross-references.** §1.1.5–§1.1.6 (the trial balance this chapter builds from); KA 1.5 (chart of
accounts and cost coding, which determines how ledger accounts map to statement lines); KA 2.1 (qualitative
characteristics and comparative information); §1.2.1–§1.2.5 (each statement built in full above).

### Key terms

- **Statement of Financial Position** — reports assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time; the
  classified presentation of the accounting equation.
- **Current / non-current** — classification based on realisation or settlement within the normal
  operating cycle or twelve months of the reporting date, and (for liabilities) the entity's unconditional
  contractual right to defer settlement.
- **Working capital** — current assets minus current liabilities.
- **Current ratio** — current assets divided by current liabilities.
- **Statement of Profit or Loss and Other Comprehensive Income** — reports financial performance over a
  period; profit or loss plus items of OCI.
- **By nature / by function** — two IFRS-permitted ways to classify expenses within profit or loss; neither
  changes the bottom-line profit.
- **Other comprehensive income (OCI)** — items of income and expense that IFRS Accounting Standards
  require or permit to be recognised outside profit or loss.
- **Total comprehensive income** — profit or loss for the period plus OCI for the period.
- **Reclassification ("recycling")** — the subsequent transfer of an OCI item into profit or loss in a
  later period; not all OCI items reclassify.
- **Statement of Cash Flows** — reports cash inflows and outflows classified into operating, investing and
  financing activities (IAS 7).
- **Direct method / indirect method** — the two IAS 7-permitted presentations of operating cash flows; both
  give the same net result.
- **Statement of Changes in Equity** — reconciles the opening and closing balance of each component of
  equity for the period.
- **Distribution** — a transfer of economic benefit to owners in their capacity as owners (e.g. a dividend);
  never passes through profit or loss.
- **Notes to the financial statements** — disclosures of accounting policies, judgements, estimates and
  supporting detail; a required part of a complete set of financial statements.
- **Articulation** — the set of mathematical links connecting the four primary financial statements.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 1.2.1 [level: recall]**
Which financial statement reports an entity's assets, liabilities and equity at a single point in time?
A. Statement of Profit or Loss and OCI
B. Statement of Financial Position ✔
C. Statement of Cash Flows
D. Statement of Changes in Equity

*Rationale:* B reports financial position at a point in time (§1.2.1). A and C report flows over a period;
D reconciles equity movements over a period, not a point-in-time snapshot.

**MCQ 1.2.2 [level: application — numerical]**
Using Meridian's classified Statement of Financial Position at 31 March 2026 (current assets $695,000;
current liabilities $100,000), what is its working capital?
A. $842,500
B. $695,000
C. $595,000 ✔
D. $100,000

*Rationale:* Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities = 695,000 − 100,000 = 595,000 (Example
1.2.1). A is total assets; B is current assets alone; D is current liabilities alone.

**MCQ 1.2.3 [level: application — numerical]**
Meridian reports contract revenue of $220,000 and profit for the period of $42,500 for March 2026. What is
its net profit margin?
A. 5.0%
B. 19.3% ✔
C. 27.3%
D. 80.7%

*Rationale:* Net profit margin = 42,500 / 220,000 = 19.3% (§1.2.2). A wrongly divides profit by total
assets; C is direct project cost as a percentage of revenue; D is total expenses as a percentage of
revenue — both C and D compute a cost ratio, not a profit margin.

**MCQ 1.2.4 [level: analysis]**
Which of the following is most likely to be recognised in other comprehensive income rather than profit or
loss?
A. Contract revenue from a certified milestone
B. A revaluation surplus on owned freehold land under the IAS 16 revaluation model ✔
C. Depreciation expense on site equipment
D. Interest expense on a bank loan

*Rationale:* B is a classic OCI item under IAS 16 (Example 1.2.2b). A, C and D are all recognised in profit
or loss as ordinary items of revenue and expense.

**MCQ 1.2.5 [level: application]**
Under IAS 7, in which section of the Statement of Cash Flows would proceeds from a new bank loan be
presented?
A. Operating activities
B. Investing activities
C. Financing activities ✔
D. Other comprehensive income

*Rationale:* Borrowing is a financing activity — it changes the size and composition of the entity's
borrowings (§1.2.3, Example 1.2.3). Operating activities relate to the entity's principal revenue-producing
activities; investing activities relate to acquisition/disposal of long-term assets; OCI is not part of the
cash flow statement at all.

**MCQ 1.2.6 [level: application — numerical]**
An entity's retained earnings are $42,500 at 31 March 2026. During April it earns profit of $30,000 and
pays a dividend of $10,000. What is the closing retained earnings balance at 30 April 2026?
A. $72,500
B. $62,500 ✔
C. $52,500
D. $82,500

*Rationale:* Closing retained earnings = 42,500 + 30,000 − 10,000 = 62,500 (Example 1.2.4b). A omits the
dividend; C subtracts the dividend twice in effect (mis-sequencing); D omits the dividend and mis-adds.

### Self-check questions

1. Using Meridian's figures from this chapter, explain why the closing cash balance on the Statement of
   Cash Flows must equal the cash and cash equivalents figure on the Statement of Financial Position, and
   demonstrate the equality using the actual numbers.
2. Distinguish "profit for the period" from "total comprehensive income for the period," and explain, using
   the Atlas Plant & Equipment mini-case (Example 1.2.2b), when and why the two figures differ.
3. For Al-Noor Site Services (Example 1.2.6b), set out the roll-forward of total equity for the period and
   confirm it reconciles to total equity reported on its Statement of Financial Position.
4. Explain, in your own words, why depreciation is added back to profit in the indirect-method Statement of
   Cash Flows even though it is a genuine expense in the Statement of Profit or Loss.

**Answers**

1. Cash movements recorded through the operating, investing and financing sections of the Statement of
   Cash Flows are, by construction, the same cash movements that determine the closing cash balance carried
   onto the Statement of Financial Position — they are two views of the same underlying ledger account
   (Cash). For Meridian: opening cash $0 + operating $55,000 − investing $150,000 + financing $700,000 =
   closing cash $605,000, which is exactly the cash and cash equivalents figure on the 31 March 2026
   Statement of Financial Position (Examples 1.2.1 and 1.2.3).
2. Profit for the period captures income and expenses recognised in profit or loss; total comprehensive
   income adds items of other comprehensive income that IFRS requires or permits to bypass profit or loss
   (e.g. an IAS 16 revaluation surplus). They differ whenever OCI is non-zero: Atlas Plant & Equipment's
   profit of $180,000 differs from its total comprehensive income of $205,000 by exactly its $25,000
   revaluation surplus for the year.
3. Opening equity SAR 0 + capital injected SAR 300,000 + profit for the period SAR 82,000 = closing total
   equity SAR 382,000, which reconciles exactly to total equity of SAR 382,000 on Al-Noor's Statement of
   Financial Position (Example 1.2.6b).
4. Depreciation reduces profit as a genuine allocation of the asset's cost over its useful life, but no cash
   actually leaves the entity when the depreciation charge is recognised — the cash outflow occurred earlier,
   when the asset was purchased (and is instead reported in investing activities, in the period of
   purchase). Adding depreciation back in the indirect method removes this non-cash charge so that the
   remaining adjustments (working-capital movements) isolate the cash effect of the period's genuine
   operating activity.
