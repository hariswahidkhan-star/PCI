# Domain 1 — Foundations of Accounting for Project Controls

## KA 1.5 — Chart of accounts & cost coding for projects

KA 1.1 built the double-entry ledger from first principles; KA 1.2–1.4 showed how that ledger becomes
financial statements, accruals and provisions. None of that mechanism tells a project controls
professional *which project, which work package, or which discipline* a cost belongs to — a general
ledger account called "Subcontract cost" is enough for statutory reporting, but useless on its own for
answering "is the signalling installation over budget?" This Knowledge Area closes that gap. It covers
the **chart of accounts (CoA)** that every entity needs for external, natural-account reporting; the
**cost breakdown structure (CBS)** and **project cost coding** that let cost be captured by project,
work package and discipline as well as by natural account; how cost codes **map onto the work breakdown
structure (WBS)**; and the **control account** — the point where scope, budget and actual cost meet for
management reporting and (later, in Domain 6) earned value measurement. The chapter closes with an honest
look at where AI genuinely helps with coding at scale — automated classification of transactions and
anomaly detection in postings — and where it does not. Every coded figure in this chapter reconciles
exactly to the Meridian Rail Contracting LLC ledger built in KA 1.1, so the two chapters read as one
continuous worked example: the same $177,500 of March cost, first proved arithmetically correct in
Example 1.1.6, is coded here down to work-package and discipline level without a single dollar going
missing or being double-counted.

### 1.5.1 The chart of accounts for a project-based organisation

**Definition and purpose.** The **chart of accounts (CoA)** is the complete, structured list of the
**natural accounts** — asset, liability, equity, income and expense accounts (KA 1.1, §1.1.2) — that an
entity's ledger is built from. Every account used in KA 1.1's journal, ledger and trial balance
("Cash," "Accounts payable," "Contract revenue," "Subcontract cost") is a line in Meridian's CoA. Its
purpose is to give every transaction one, and only one, correct natural-account home so that the trial
balance and financial statements are produced consistently period after period.

**The underlying principle — separate "what" from "where."** A natural account answers *what kind* of
economic event occurred (materials were consumed; labour was paid). It does not, by itself, answer *where*
— which project, which work package, which discipline. A project-based contractor delivering many
concurrent contracts is tempted to answer "where" by creating a new natural account for every project
("Subcontract cost — Project 07," "Subcontract cost — Project 08" …), but this multiplies the CoA without
limit and breaks comparability: a report summing "all subcontract cost" now has to aggregate dozens of
differently-named accounts instead of one. The professional solution, used by every mainstream ERP, is to
keep the **natural CoA small and stable** and to add "where" as one or more separate **coding segments**
(project, WBS/work package, discipline) attached to each posting — this is the subject of §1.5.2–§1.5.3.
The CoA answers "what"; the cost coding structure answers "where."

**Structure.** A CoA is organised into numbered **blocks**, one block per account type, so that an
account's code alone signals its type and normal balance (KA 1.1, §1.1.2) before a user even reads its
name. Extending the codes already introduced in KA 1.1 (Fig 1.1.3 used 101, 201 and 301):

| Block | Account type | Normal balance | Example accounts (code) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100–199 | Assets | Debit | Cash (101), Accounts receivable (110), Materials inventory (120), Equipment — cost (150), Accumulated depreciation — contra-asset (155, credit) |
| 200–299 | Liabilities | Credit | Accounts payable (210), Loan payable (201) |
| 300–399 | Equity | Credit | Share capital (301), Retained earnings (350) |
| 400–499 | Income | Credit | Contract revenue (401) |
| 500–599 | Direct project cost (Expense) | Debit | Direct project cost — materials (510), Labour cost (520), Subcontract cost (530), Equipment/plant cost (540) |
| 600–699 | Indirect / overhead cost (Expense) | Debit | Depreciation expense (610), Site overheads (620), Administration overhead (690) |

Note the deliberate **gaps** in the numbering (e.g. 540 leaves 541–599 free) — good coding practice
reserves unused numbers within a block so a new account can be inserted later without renumbering
existing accounts and breaking every historical report that cites them. Note also the split between the
500-block (direct field cost, traceable to a work package) and the 600-block (indirect/overhead cost,
not traceable to one work package) — a distinction Domain 5 (KA 5.1) develops fully; §1.5.2 uses it
immediately to separate direct from indirect cost coding.

*Interpretation.* Attaching a code is a pure relabelling exercise with no effect on the double-entry

**Example 1.5.1b — why not one account per project (a mini-case).** Meridian's finance director proposes
creating a dedicated "Subcontract cost" account for each of the firm's 40 concurrent projects, reasoning
that this would let each project's subcontract spend be read straight off the trial balance. *Analysis:*
across the 12 direct/indirect expense accounts in the CoA above, this would require 12 × 40 = **480**
expense accounts instead of 12, before even adding project-specific asset or income accounts; every
month-end report that sums "total subcontract cost across the firm" would need to aggregate 40 differently
-coded lines instead of one, and every new project would require a CoA change request routed through the
finance system. *Conclusion:* the proposal is rejected in favour of the segment-based cost code developed
in §1.5.2 — one stable "Subcontract cost" (530) account, with "which project" carried by a separate coding
segment on each posting.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Creating a new natural account to answer a "where" question (a project, a client,
a site) instead of using a coding segment — this is the single most common cause of CoA bloat
(Example 1.5.1b). (2) Reusing a retired account code for an unrelated new account, which corrupts
historical trend reports that key on the code rather than the name. (3) Numbering direct and indirect cost
accounts in the same block, which makes it impossible to separate field-traceable cost from overhead
without opening every transaction (KA 5.1 depends on this separation being clean).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted CoA design tools can benchmark a proposed chart against industry-typical
structures for contracting and engineering firms and flag likely duplication (e.g. two near-identical
account names that should be one). This is a genuinely useful design-time aid; it is not a substitute for
the professional judgement of *how many* accounts the entity's reporting actually needs — over-automating
CoA design risks reproducing exactly the bloat problem in Example 1.5.1b, just faster.

**Cross-references.** §1.1.2 (Dr/Cr rules and account types this CoA is built on); §1.5.2 (the cost
coding segments that carry "where"); KA 5.1 (direct vs indirect cost, cost drivers and allocation, built
on the 500/600 split introduced here).

### 1.5.2 The cost breakdown structure (CBS) and project cost coding

**Definition and purpose.** The **cost breakdown structure (CBS)** — the term used in the AACE
International Total Cost Management (TCM) framework — is a hierarchical breakdown of project cost into
cost elements (labour, materials, subcontract, equipment) that can be organised by project, phase and
discipline independently of the natural CoA. A **cost code** is the alphanumeric identifier assigned to
one CBS position; **cost coding** is the discipline of tagging every transaction with the correct code at
the point it is captured (a timesheet, a purchase order, a supplier invoice) so it can be reported at any
level of the CBS later.

**The underlying principle.** A single dollar of project cost genuinely has several simultaneous
descriptions — it belongs to one project, one phase of that project, one discipline, and one natural
account — and a well-designed coding structure lets all of these be recovered from one posting rather than
forcing a choice between them. This is why cost coding is built as a **segmented code**: several short
fields concatenated together, each field independently meaningful, rather than one long unstructured
number.

**Coding structure.** Meridian's project cost code takes the form:

`Cost code = P.WW.DD.NNN`

| Segment | Meaning | Width (units) | Example values |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | Project identifier | 2 digits | 07 = Rail Signalling Contract |
| WW | WBS phase / work package | 2 digits | 10 Design · 20 Procurement · 30 Installation & Testing · 40 Project management |
| DD | Discipline | 2 digits | 01 Design & engineering · 02 Civil works · 03 Signalling systems · 04 Electrical · 09 General/indirect |
| NNN | Natural account (ties to the CoA, §1.5.1) | 3 digits | 510 Direct project cost · 520 Labour · 530 Subcontract · 610 Depreciation |

For example, `07.30.03.530` reads as "Project 07, Installation & Testing, Signalling systems,
Subcontract cost" — a single fifteen-character code carries four independent facts about the dollar it is
attached to, and every one of those facts can be recovered by reading one segment.

**Example 1.5.2 — coding Meridian's March 2026 actual cost.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's cost engineer codes the same four expense-account totals already posted and
trial-balanced in KA 1.1 (Direct project cost $60,000; Labour cost $45,000; Subcontract cost $70,000;
Depreciation expense $2,500) down to work-package and discipline level.

*Required.* Produce the fully coded cost detail and confirm it reconciles to the KA 1.1 general-ledger

*Interpretation.* The same coded data can be cut three different ways with no further arithmetic:
summed by natural account it reproduces the four GL balances above; summed by WBS phase it gives
$12,000 (Design) + $38,000 (Procurement) + $125,000 (Installation & Testing) + $2,500 (Project management)
= $177,500; summed by discipline it gives $12,000 (Design & engineering) + $33,000 (Civil works) +
$108,000 (Signalling systems) + $22,000 (Electrical) + $2,500 (General/indirect) = $177,500. A well-built
coding structure is exactly this: one capture, many equally valid reporting cuts, all reconciling.

**Example 1.5.2b — coding is currency-agnostic (mini-case).** Meridian's signalling subcontractor invoices
from its Saudi branch in Saudi Riyal. The cost code `07.30.03.530` applies unchanged regardless of the
invoice currency — the code describes *what the cost is and where it belongs*, not what currency it was
paid in. At an illustrative rate of **SAR 3.75 = USD 1** (never a live rate — spine §4), the $70,000
subcontract line equals **SAR 262,500**; the coding structure, the WBS mapping and the control-account
roll-up in §1.5.3–§1.5.4 are entirely unaffected by which currency the transaction happened to be raised
in. Currency translation is a separate accounting step (KA 2.4) performed after coding, not instead of it.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Building a single unstructured cost-code number (e.g. a sequential six-digit
code with no segment meaning) — it may be shorter, but no fact can be recovered from it without a lookup
table, and it cannot be summed by WBS or discipline without that same lookup. (2) Letting segment widths
drift (a three-digit WBS phase appearing alongside two-digit phases elsewhere) — this breaks automated
parsing and reporting. (3) Coding a transaction to a discipline that is convenient rather than correct
(e.g. coding electrical labour to "General/indirect" because the correct discipline code is not
top-of-mind) — small individually, but it silently distorts every discipline-level report (§1.5.4 develops
the consequences).

**AI in this topic.** Where the natural-account suggestion described in KA 1.1 (§1.1.2) predicts one
segment, cost-coding classification is a harder, multi-segment prediction problem — an AI classifier must
predict project, WBS phase, discipline *and* natural account together, usually from unstructured text on
an invoice or timesheet. This is exactly the "automated coding/classification" capability flagged in this
Knowledge Area's brief; §1.5.6 treats it in full once the CBS, WBS and control-account structures it
classifies against have all been introduced.

**Cross-references.** §1.5.1 (the natural-account segment, NNN, drawn from the CoA); §1.5.3 (mapping the
WW segment formally to the WBS); KA 5.1 (cost drivers, allocation and the direct/indirect distinction
this coding structure encodes); KA 5.3 (the operational cost-control cycle that consumes this coded data).

### 1.5.3 The work breakdown structure (WBS) and mapping cost codes to it

**Definition and purpose.** The **work breakdown structure (WBS)**, as defined in the PMBOK Guide, is a
deliverable-oriented hierarchical decomposition of the total scope of work to be carried out by the
project team. Each descending level represents an increasingly detailed definition of the project work,
down to a **work package** — the lowest-level WBS component, small enough to be estimated, scheduled,
costed and assigned to a single owner. The **WBS dictionary** documents, for each element, its scope,
deliverables, acceptance criteria and the resources and cost assigned to it.

**The underlying principle — the 100% rule.** PMBOK's **100% rule** states that the WBS must capture
100% of the scope defined by the project — all the work, and only that work, with no gaps and no
overlaps between sibling elements. This is the scope-side twin of the coding reconciliation principle
already met in §1.5.2 and developed formally in §1.5.5: just as the WBS must sum to 100% of scope, the
cost coded against the WBS must sum to 100% of the project's actual cost — no cost uncoded, no cost coded
twice. Meridian's WW segment (§1.5.2) is the practical device that ties every coded dollar to exactly one
WBS element, enforcing the 100% rule in the cost data.

**Structure — the WBS-to-cost-code mapping matrix.** Because the WW segment of every cost code is a WBS
phase, the coded data from §1.5.2 can be laid out directly as a matrix: WBS phases as rows, natural
accounts as columns.

**Example 1.5.3 — Meridian's WBS-by-cost-type matrix for March 2026.**

*Scenario/Required.* Aggregate Example 1.5.2's coded detail into a WBS × natural-account matrix and
confirm both the row and column totals reconcile to $177,500.

*Working.*

| WBS phase | 510 Direct project cost | 520 Labour cost | 530 Subcontract cost | 610 Depreciation | Row total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 Design | 0 | 12,000 | 0 | 0 | **12,000** |
| 20 Procurement | 38,000 | 0 | 0 | 0 | **38,000** |
| 30 Installation & Testing | 22,000 | 33,000 | 70,000 | 0 | **125,000** |
| 40 Project management | 0 | 0 | 0 | 2,500 | **2,500** |
| **Column total** | **60,000** | **45,000** | **70,000** | **2,500** | **177,500** |

*Answer.* Row totals sum to $12,000 + $38,000 + $125,000 + $2,500 = **$177,500**; column totals sum to
$60,000 + $45,000 + $70,000 + $2,500 = **$177,500**. Both directions reconcile to the same figure, and to
the KA 1.1 general-ledger total.

*Interpretation.* This matrix is the single most useful report the coding structure produces: read across
a row, a phase manager sees their total cost by type; read down a column, the finance function sees a
natural account's total split by phase; the grand total in the bottom-right corner is the one number both
views must always agree on — a built-in cross-check every period.

**Example 1.5.3b — a WBS element with no work package below it (pitfall mini-case).** Suppose Meridian's
WBS listed "35 Testing & commissioning" as a distinct phase but no work package, cost code, or budget was
ever assigned beneath it because the scope was assumed to be included in "30 Installation." *Analysis:*
this breaches the 100% rule twice over — either the testing scope is not really separate (in which case
"35" should not exist as a WBS element) or it is separate and currently has **zero** coded cost against
real scope of work, which will surface as a large, unexplained variance the moment testing activity
starts and gets coded somewhere ad hoc. *Conclusion:* every WBS element down to work-package level must
have at least one valid cost code before work starts against it — a check performed at WBS baseline
(cross-ref KA 8.2), not discovered mid-project.

#### Fig 1.5.3 — WBS-by-cost-type mapping matrix

*Chart type:* cross-tabulation (pivot) table, rendered as a heat-shaded grid — darker fill for larger

*Underlying data:* the five-row, six-column table in Example 1.5.3.
*Render-ready description:* a matrix grid, brand blue `#1D4ED8` shading intensity proportional to cell

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating the WBS as an *organisation* chart (who does the work) rather than a
*deliverable* decomposition (what work is delivered) — PMBOK is explicit that the WBS decomposes
deliverables, not people; the organisational dimension is the separate OBS met in §1.5.4. (2) Decomposing
some branches of the WBS to a much finer level of detail than others for no scope-driven reason, which
makes cost and schedule data noisy and hard to compare across phases. (3) Letting the matrix's grand total
silently drift because a late change added a WBS element without a corresponding cost-code request
(§1.5.5 develops the coding-governance control that prevents this).

**AI in this topic.** Natural-language tools can propose a first-draft WBS decomposition from a scope
description or contract document, and can suggest which existing cost codes a newly added WBS element
should map to based on similar past projects. Both are useful accelerants for planning (cross-ref KA 8.2);
neither can apply the 100% rule with authority, because only the professional planning team knows whether
the *scope itself* is complete — an AI tool checking coding coverage can at best confirm that whatever WBS
elements exist have codes, not that the WBS as a whole is scope-complete.

**Cross-references.** §1.5.2 (the WW segment mapped here); §1.5.4 (adding the organisational dimension —
control accounts); KA 8.2 (Planning — WBS development, the scope, cost and schedule baselines); KA 10.1
(Schedule development — activities sequenced within the same WBS elements).

### 1.5.4 Control accounts: the WBS × OBS intersection

**Definition and purpose.** A **control account**, in the PMBOK sense used throughout project controls
and earned value management, is a management control point at which scope, budget and schedule are
integrated and compared to actual cost and earned value for performance measurement. A control account
sits at a chosen level of the WBS and may contain one or more work packages; PMBOK is explicit that each
work package belongs to **exactly one** control account — mirroring the WBS's own 100% rule (§1.5.3), now
applied to management responsibility rather than to scope. A control account is defined at the
intersection of the WBS (what work) and the **organisational breakdown structure (OBS)** — who is
accountable for it — often visualised as a **responsibility assignment matrix (RAM)**.

**A necessary disambiguation.** KA 1.1 (§1.1.4) already used the term **control account** in a different,
equally correct sense: a general-ledger account (Accounts Receivable, Accounts Payable) that carries a
summarised total reconciling to a subsidiary ledger. From this point in the BoK onward, unless stated
otherwise, **"control account" means the PMBOK/EVM sense** — a WBS × OBS management point — because that
is overwhelmingly the sense project controls professionals mean day to day; where the bookkeeping sense
is intended it is written out in full ("GL control account," as in §1.1.4). Confusing the two in
conversation with a finance colleague is a common and avoidable source of miscommunication.

**The underlying principle.** A control account gives one named owner a single, complete cost and
schedule picture for the scope they are accountable for — the manager of "Installation & Testing" should
be able to see one number for their control account's actual cost, not have to hunt across every natural
account and discipline separately. Because every coded transaction already carries a WBS phase (§1.5.2),
defining control accounts at WBS-phase level lets the roll-up be read directly off the matrix already
built in Example 1.5.3.

**Example 1.5.4 — Meridian's control accounts for March 2026.**

*Scenario/Required.* Meridian defines one control account per WBS phase, each owned by a named manager,
and reports each control account's March actual cost.

*Working.*

| Control account | WBS phase | Owner (OBS) | March actual (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| CA-10 | Design | Design Manager | 12,000 |
| CA-20 | Procurement | Procurement Manager | 38,000 |
| CA-30 | Installation & Testing | Installation Manager | 125,000 |
| CA-40 | Project management | Project Manager | 2,500 |
| **Project total** | | | **177,500** |

*Answer.* Four control accounts sum to **$177,500**, the row totals already proved in Example 1.5.3 and
the general-ledger total from KA 1.1.

*Interpretation.* Each manager now has one number that is theirs to explain — this is the reporting layer
that Domain 6 (EVM) will attach planned value and earned value to, turning "$125,000 actual" into a
CPI/SPI for the Installation Manager specifically (cross-ref KA 6.1).

**Example 1.5.4b — a miscoding that distorts two control accounts (mini-case).** Suppose $15,000 of
electrical installation labour, correctly `07.30.04.520`, was mistakenly captured as `07.20.04.520` —
right discipline and natural account, wrong WBS phase (Procurement instead of Installation & Testing).
*Analysis:* CA-20 (Procurement) now reports $38,000 + $15,000 = **$53,000**; CA-30 (Installation &
Testing) now reports $125,000 − $15,000 = **$110,000**. The project total is unchanged at
$53,000 + $110,000 + $12,000 + $2,500 = **$177,500**, so the general ledger — and a GL-level reconciliation
of the kind performed in §1.5.5 — shows nothing wrong at all. *Conclusion:* the Procurement Manager's
report is overstated by $15,000 and the Installation Manager's is understated by the same amount; if
Domain 6's CPI were computed at control-account level from these figures, both managers' performance
indices would be wrong even though the project as a whole is correctly stated — exactly the "error of
principle" phenomenon KA 1.1 (§1.1.5) showed the trial balance cannot catch, now recurring one layer up
in the WBS/OBS structure instead of the natural-account structure. §1.5.6 returns to this exact scenario
to show how anomaly detection can help.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Confusing the two senses of "control account" described above in a live
discussion between project controls and finance — always state which is meant if there is any doubt.
(2) Defining control accounts at a level so granular that the reporting burden outweighs the management
value — PMBOK guidance is to set the level where meaningful management decisions are actually made, not
at every work package. (3) Assuming a control account being "on budget" in total means every work package
within it is on budget — offsetting over- and under-runs within one control account can mask a real
problem (Example 1.5.4b shows the same masking effect operating one level up).

**AI in this topic.** Dashboarding tools can automate the roll-up from coded transactions to control
account totals in real time, removing the manual aggregation step in Example 1.5.4 entirely. What such
tools cannot do unassisted is judge whether a WBS-phase miscoding like Example 1.5.4b has occurred, because
the roll-up arithmetic is correct in both the miscoded and correctly-coded versions — this is precisely
where anomaly detection, not simple aggregation, adds value (§1.5.6).

### 1.5.5 Coding governance, data integrity and reconciliation

**Definition and purpose.** **Coding governance** is the set of rules and controls that keep a cost
coding structure trustworthy over the life of a project: a **coding manual/dictionary** defining every
valid segment value and its meaning; a **change-control process** for requesting a new code (or retiring
one); and a periodic **reconciliation** between the coded project cost ledger and the general ledger
control totals it must always agree with.

**The underlying principle.** Coding a transaction happens once, at or near the point the cost is
captured (a timesheet entry, a purchase order, a supplier invoice); every downstream report — the WBS
matrix, the control-account roll-up, an eventual earned-value calculation — inherits whatever was decided
at that single point. Governance exists because coding errors are cheap to prevent at the point of entry
and expensive to unpick later, once dozens of reports have already been built on top of the wrong code.

**Structure — the reconciliation check.** Exactly as the trial balance proves `ΣDr = ΣCr` (KA 1.1, §1.1.5),
the coding reconciliation proves:

`Σ (Coded project cost detail) = Σ (General ledger control-account balances for the same accounts)`

Like the trial balance, this is a **necessary but not sufficient** check (KA 1.1, §1.1.5): it will catch
an *omission* (cost posted to the GL but never coded, or coded but never posted) because the two totals
will disagree; it will **not** catch a *misclassification* between two valid codes of the same total value
(Example 1.5.4b), because both totals still agree. The two checks are complementary, not substitutes for
one another.

**Example 1.5.5 — reconciliation catches an omission.**

*Scenario.* At March close, the general ledger again shows Labour cost of $45,000 (KA 1.1). Due to an
incomplete timesheet upload, only $7,000 of the $12,000 Design-phase labour was coded to `07.10.01.520`;
the remaining $5,000 was never coded at all.

*Required.* Reconcile the coded cost detail to the general ledger and quantify the break.

*Working.*

| | Direct project cost (510) | Labour cost (520) | Subcontract cost (530) | Depreciation (610) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| General ledger (KA 1.1) | 60,000 | 45,000 | 70,000 | 2,500 | **177,500** |
| Coded detail (as submitted) | 60,000 | 40,000 | 70,000 | 2,500 | **172,500** |
| **Break** | 0 | **5,000** | 0 | 0 | **5,000** |

*Answer.* A **$5,000 break**, entirely within the Labour cost (520) column, traced to the incomplete
`07.10.01.520` coding.

*Interpretation.* The reconciliation does its job precisely: it cannot say *which* work package the
missing $5,000 belongs to without further investigation, but it correctly proves that $172,500 of coded
detail cannot explain a $177,500 general ledger balance, and it narrows the search to one natural account
— a large efficiency gain over checking all thirteen accounts individually.

**Example 1.5.5b — a code-freeze mini-case.** Meridian's coding manual specifies that cost codes for a
closed reporting period are **frozen** once that period's management report has issued — no new
transaction may be coded against February once the February report is out, even if the underlying invoice
is dated in February; a late invoice is coded to the current open period (March) with a note explaining
the timing difference. *Analysis:* without this rule, a manager could keep "improving" a closed period's
reported cost weeks after stakeholders have already acted on the original figures, and two different
reports for the same period could legitimately show different totals. *Conclusion:* period freezing is a
governance control, not an accounting control — it does not change *how much* cost exists, only *which
period's report* it is allowed to affect, and it is the coding-structure analogue of the accounting cut-off
procedures met in KA 1.3.

#### Fig 1.5.5 — Reconciliation bridge, coded detail to general ledger

*Chart type:* two-bar comparison chart with a labelled bridge segment for the break, in the same visual
grammar as Fig 1.1.5/1.1.6.
*Underlying data:* the three-row table in Example 1.5.5 (General ledger, Coded detail, Break).
*Render-ready description:* Bar 1 "General ledger total" at $177,500 (solid brand blue `#1D4ED8`); Bar 2
"Coded detail total" at $172,500, with a hatched $5,000 segment stacked on top labelled "unreconciled
break — Labour cost (520)" to bring the two bars to equal height, making the gap visually explicit rather
than implied.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating a clean reconciliation (Example 1.5.2, no break) as proof that every
individual code used was the *correct* one — as Example 1.5.4b shows, a reconciliation that balances is
blind to misclassification between codes, exactly as a trial balance is blind to an error of principle.
(2) Allowing ad-hoc, undocumented cost codes to be created outside the change-control process "just this
once" — these are the codes least likely to appear correctly in the coding manual and most likely to be
miscoded downstream. (3) Reconciling only at project close rather than every period — by project close,
the corrective action a timely reconciliation would have triggered is no longer possible.

**AI in this topic.** Reconciliation itself — matching two totals and isolating a break to a specific
account or code — is a mechanical, rules-based task that tools have automated reliably for years; this is
not a frontier AI capability, and claiming otherwise would overstate what is actually happening. What AI
adds on top is pattern-based **anomaly detection** across the coded detail itself, which can surface a
misclassification (Example 1.5.4b) that a reconciliation alone would miss — the subject of §1.5.6.

**Cross-references.** §1.1.5 (the trial balance's own "necessary but not sufficient" limitation, echoed
here); §1.1.4 (subsidiary-ledger-to-control-account reconciliation, the bookkeeping ancestor of this
check); KA 1.3 (accrual cut-off, the accounting analogue of a code freeze); KA 5.2 (the cost control
cycle — commitment, accrual, actual — that this reconciliation feeds every period).

### 1.5.6 AI in chart of accounts and cost coding: automated classification and anomaly detection

**Definition and purpose.** Two AI capabilities are genuinely established in this domain today.
**Automated coding/classification** uses a machine-learning or large-language-model classifier, trained
on historical coded transactions, to suggest the cost code (or the natural account, or both) for a new,
unclassified transaction — a supplier invoice line, a timesheet entry, a purchase requisition — usually
from its free-text description. **Anomaly detection** uses statistical or machine-learning models over
the coded transaction history to flag postings that are unusual relative to that history — a code
combination that has never occurred before, or a value far outside a code's normal range — for human
review, rather than to code anything itself.

**The underlying principle — "AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable."** Neither capability decides a
final code. A classifier proposes a code with a confidence score; a professional reviewer accepts,
corrects or rejects it before it posts (or reviews it promptly after, under a materiality-based sampling
policy). An anomaly-detection model raises a flag; a professional investigates and determines whether the
flagged posting is a genuine error, a legitimate but rare event, or a sign of something more serious (a
control weakness, or worse). This is the same governance boundary already established for natural-account
coding suggestions in KA 1.1, §1.1.2 and §1.1.3 — applied here to the harder, multi-segment coding problem
introduced in §1.5.2.

**Example 1.5.6 — an automated coding suggestion.**

*Scenario.* A supplier invoice line reads: "Supply and install cable trays, containment and conduit —
signalling systems, Rail Signalling Contract, Zone 3 (installation phase)." Meridian's AP-automation tool,
trained on a year of Meridian's own historical coded invoices, proposes three candidate codes ranked by
confidence:

| Rank | Suggested code | Confidence | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `07.30.03.530` | 92% | Installation & Testing · Signalling systems · Subcontract cost |
| 2 | `07.30.03.510` | 6% | Installation & Testing · Signalling systems · Direct project cost (materials) |
| 3 | `07.20.03.510` | 2% | Procurement · Signalling systems · Direct project cost (materials) |

*Required.* State the professional's review step and outcome.

*Working/Answer.* The reviewer checks the invoice: it is a **supply-and-install** line from the signalling
subcontractor, i.e. subcontract cost, not a standalone materials purchase — the top suggestion,
`07.30.03.530`, is confirmed and posted. Note this is the identical code already used for the $70,000
subcontract line in Example 1.5.2 — the tool has correctly generalised from Meridian's own coding history.

*Interpretation.* A 92% top-ranked confidence with two plausible, correctly-adjacent alternatives is a
realistic, useful output: it accelerates the reviewer's decision without removing it. A tool that instead
returned a single code with no confidence score and no alternatives would give the reviewer nothing to
exercise judgement against — which is itself a governance red flag for how the tool has been configured.

**Example 1.5.6b — anomaly detection catching the miscoding mini-case.** Returning to Example 1.5.4b: an
anomaly-detection model, scoring each new posting against Meridian's coded history, flags the
`07.20.04.520` posting (Procurement · Electrical · **Labour**) because WBS phase 20 (Procurement) has
never previously received a Labour-cost (520) posting in Meridian's history — Procurement has
historically only ever received Direct project cost (510) postings, consistent with its nature as a
buying, not an installing, phase. The flag is queued for review, not auto-corrected; the reviewer traces
it to the timesheet source, confirms it should have been `07.30.04.520` (Installation & Testing ·
Electrical · Labour), and corrects it before the control-account report in Example 1.5.4 is issued —
recovering exactly the $15,000 misclassification that a reconciliation alone (§1.5.5) would not have
caught, because both codes summed to the same $177,500 project total.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Training a classifier on historically *miscoded* data and having it confidently
reproduce the same mistakes at greater scale — exactly the risk already flagged for natural-account
coding in KA 1.1, §1.1.2, and just as real here because the model has no independent way to know
Meridian's own history contains errors. (2) Auto-posting high-confidence suggestions above a materiality
threshold with no sampling review at all — confidence is a statement about pattern-similarity to history,
not a guarantee of correctness. (3) Treating an anomaly flag as proof of an error — some flagged postings
are simply the *first* legitimate instance of a genuinely new but valid cost pattern (a phase doing a type
of work it has not done before); the flag means "look," not "reject."

**AI in this topic — honest capabilities and limits.** These capabilities are real and in active use
today, most visibly inside large ERP platforms (SAP, Oracle and similar) and in dedicated AP-automation /
intelligent-document-processing tools that read invoices and suggest coding, alongside anomaly-detection
modules built into analytics and continuous-monitoring platforms. Their genuine strength is speed and
consistency at high transaction volumes. Their real limits are: they generalise from **historical** coding
patterns, including historical mistakes; they have no independent knowledge of a project's actual scope
or WBS beyond what the coded data already tells them; and a clean, unflagged result is not proof of
correctness — it only means the posting resembled history closely enough not to stand out, which is a
different thing from being right (exactly the same qualification KA 1.1, §1.1.5 made about a balanced
trial balance). Feature capability in this space is evolving quickly and specific product claims should
always be checked against the current vendor documentation rather than assumed from this text.

**Cross-references.** §1.1.2 and §1.1.3 (natural-account coding suggestions and RPA-drafted journal

### Key terms

- **Chart of accounts (CoA)** — the structured list of an entity's natural accounts (asset, liability,
  equity, income, expense).
- **Natural account** — an account describing *what kind* of economic event occurred, independent of
  which project it relates to.
- **Cost breakdown structure (CBS)** — a hierarchical breakdown of project cost by element (labour,
  materials, subcontract, equipment), organisable by project, phase and discipline (AACE TCM usage).
- **Cost code / cost coding** — the segmented identifier (and the discipline of assigning it) that tags a
  transaction with project, WBS phase, discipline and natural account.
- **Work breakdown structure (WBS)** — a deliverable-oriented hierarchical decomposition of total project
  scope (PMBOK); its lowest component is the **work package**.
- **100% rule** — the WBS must capture 100% of project scope, with no gaps and no overlaps.
- **WBS dictionary** — the document recording each WBS element's scope, deliverables and acceptance
  criteria.
- **Control account (PMBOK/EVM sense)** — a WBS × OBS management point where scope, budget, schedule and
  actual cost are integrated for performance measurement; contains one or more work packages.
- **Control account (bookkeeping sense)** — a general-ledger account summarising a subsidiary ledger
  (KA 1.1, §1.1.4); disambiguated from the PMBOK sense in §1.5.4.
- **Organisational breakdown structure (OBS)** — the structure showing who in the organisation is
  accountable for which scope.
- **Responsibility assignment matrix (RAM)** — the WBS × OBS matrix assigning accountability.
- **Coding manual / dictionary** — the governing document defining every valid coding segment value.
- **Code freeze** — a governance control preventing new postings against a closed reporting period.
- **Reconciliation break** — the unexplained difference between two totals that should agree (here,
  coded detail vs general ledger); necessary but not sufficient proof of correctness.
- **Anomaly detection** — statistical/ML flagging of transactions unusual relative to historical pattern.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 1.5.1 [level: recall]**
What is the principal reason a project-based contractor should avoid creating a separate natural-account
code for every project, rather than using a cost-coding segment?
A. Natural accounts cannot legally be shared between projects
B. It multiplies the chart of accounts without limit and breaks cross-project comparability ✅
C. Auditors require exactly one natural account per project
D. Cost codes are less accurate than natural accounts

*Rationale:* Example 1.5.1b shows 12 accounts × 40 projects = 480 accounts if project is encoded as a
natural account, versus 12 accounts plus a coding segment; B is the CoA-design principle in §1.5.1. A, C
and D are not real constraints or requirements.

**MCQ 1.5.2 [level: application — numerical]**
Using the coded detail in Example 1.5.2, what is the total March actual cost for WBS phase "30
Installation & Testing"?
A. $60,000
B. $70,000
C. $125,000 ✅
D. $177,500

*Rationale:* Direct project cost (15,000+7,000=22,000) + Labour cost (18,000+15,000=33,000) + Subcontract
cost (70,000) = 22,000+33,000+70,000 = $125,000 (Example 1.5.3). A is the total 510 column; B is only the
530 sub-total; D is the whole project.

**MCQ 1.5.3 [level: application]**
Under PMBOK, which statement about a control account is correct?
A. A control account may contain work packages belonging to more than one control account simultaneously
B. A control account sits at the intersection of the WBS and the OBS, and each work package belongs to
   exactly one control account ✅
C. A control account is defined only by natural account code, independent of WBS or OBS
D. A control account is a synonym for the general-ledger accounts-payable control account in every
   context

*Rationale:* B is the PMBOK definition applied in §1.5.4. A contradicts the WBS/control-account 100% rule
analogue; C ignores the OBS dimension entirely; D conflates the two distinct senses of "control account"
that §1.5.4 explicitly disambiguates.

**MCQ 1.5.4 [level: analysis]**
In the mini-case at Example 1.5.4b, $15,000 of labour was coded to the wrong WBS phase (Procurement
instead of Installation & Testing) but the correct discipline and natural account. Why does a
reconciliation between coded detail and the general ledger (§1.5.5) fail to detect this error?
A. Reconciliations only check natural accounts, never WBS phases
B. The $15,000 moved between two control accounts but the project total remained $177,500, so both
   totals being reconciled still agree ✅
C. The error was too small in value to be material
D. Reconciliations are not designed to detect any coding errors

*Rationale:* B restates the core limitation in §1.5.5 — the check proves coded-total equals GL-total, and
misclassification between codes of the same natural account leaves both totals unchanged, exactly as an
error of principle leaves a trial balance unchanged (KA 1.1, §1.1.5). A misstates what is checked; C is
false — materiality is irrelevant to whether the totals still agree; D overstates the limitation
(omissions are caught).

**MCQ 1.5.5 [level: application — numerical]**
Reconciling coded cost detail totalling $172,500 against a general ledger total of $177,500 for the same
accounts, what does the $5,000 break most directly indicate, per §1.5.5's reconciliation logic?
A. $5,000 of cost was definitely double-counted
B. $5,000 of cost exists in the general ledger but has not been captured in the coded detail (an
   omission) ✅
C. The general ledger itself must be wrong
D. The reconciliation is inconclusive and provides no information

*Rationale:* Example 1.5.5 shows coded detail below the GL total, consistent with an omission (uncoded
labour), not a duplication (which would show coded detail *above* the GL total, A). C wrongly assumes the
GL, not the coded detail, is the source of error without further evidence; D understates what the check
does establish (it narrows the break to a specific natural account).

**MCQ 1.5.6 [level: recall]**
Which of the following best distinguishes automated coding/classification from anomaly detection, as
applied to project cost postings (§1.5.6)?
A. Classification always outperforms anomaly detection in accuracy
B. Classification proposes a code for a new transaction; anomaly detection flags an already-coded
   transaction as statistically unusual for review ✅
C. Anomaly detection assigns the final code; classification only flags issues
D. The two techniques are interchangeable and always used together

*Rationale:* B correctly separates the two capabilities defined in §1.5.6. A is an unsupported and
unnecessary accuracy comparison; C reverses the two techniques' actual roles; D is false — either can be,
and often is, deployed independently.

### Self-check questions

1. Explain the difference between a chart of accounts and a cost breakdown structure/cost coding
   structure, and why a project-based organisation needs both rather than either alone.
2. Decode the cost code `07.10.01.520` segment by segment, and state which of Meridian's control accounts
   (Example 1.5.4) it rolls up into.
3. Using the coded detail in Example 1.5.2, reconstruct the discipline-level roll-up (Design & engineering,
   Civil works, Signalling systems, Electrical, General/indirect) and confirm it totals $177,500.
4. Explain why the miscoding in Example 1.5.4b is invisible to the reconciliation check in §1.5.5 but
   could be surfaced by the anomaly-detection example in §1.5.6b — what specifically does the anomaly
   model detect that the reconciliation does not?

**Answers**

1. A chart of accounts answers "what kind of cost" (natural account: materials, labour, subcontract,
   depreciation) and is kept deliberately small and stable; a CBS/cost coding structure answers "which
   project, phase and discipline" via separate coding segments. Both are needed because a project-based
   organisation must report cost both by nature (for statutory financial statements, KA 1.2) and by
   project/work package (for project management) from the same underlying transaction, without creating a
   new natural account for every project (Example 1.5.1b).
2. `07` = Project 07 (Rail Signalling Contract); `10` = WBS phase 10, Design; `01` = Discipline 01, Design
   & engineering; `520` = natural account 520, Labour cost. It rolls up into control account **CA-10**
   (Design), owned by the Design Manager (Example 1.5.4).
3. Design & engineering: 12,000 (07.10.01.520 only). Civil works: 15,000 (07.30.02.510) + 18,000
   (07.30.02.520) = 33,000. Signalling systems: 38,000 (07.20.03.510) + 70,000 (07.30.03.530) = 108,000.
   Electrical: 7,000 (07.30.04.510) + 15,000 (07.30.04.520) = 22,000. General/indirect: 2,500
   (07.40.09.610). Total = 12,000 + 33,000 + 108,000 + 22,000 + 2,500 = **$177,500**, matching the project
   total.
4. The reconciliation in §1.5.5 only compares two grand totals (coded detail vs general ledger); because
   the $15,000 moved between two WBS phases without changing the natural account or the project total,
   both totals still agree and no break appears. The anomaly-detection model in §1.5.6b instead examines
   the *pattern* of which natural accounts have historically posted against each WBS phase, and flags the
   posting because Procurement has never before received a Labour-cost (520) posting — it detects an
   unusual *combination* of segments, which a total-to-total reconciliation is not designed to see.
