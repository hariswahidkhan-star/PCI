# Domain 1 — Foundations of Accounting for Project Controls

## KA 1.1 — The accounting model

A project controls professional does not need to be a qualified accountant, but every cost report,
earned-value curve, invoice application and forecast the professional produces is, ultimately, a view
into the same underlying record: the double-entry ledger. This Knowledge Area builds that record from
first principles — the accounting equation, the rules for debiting and crediting each type of account,
the mechanics of journals and ledgers, and the trial balance that proves (mechanically, not absolutely)
that the books are in balance. A single worked transaction cycle for a fictitious project contractor runs
through the whole chapter so that the journal, the ledger and the trial balance are seen as one connected
system rather than three separate techniques. Everything in Domains 2–7 — revenue recognition, provisions,
cost coding, earned value, invoicing — is built on the mechanics taught here.

### 1.1.1 The accounting equation and the business entity concept

**Definition and purpose.** The accounting equation states that:

`Assets = Liabilities + Equity`

This is the foundational identity underlying the Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting that IFRS
Accounting Standards are built on, and it holds for every reporting entity regardless of legal form —
a limited company, a joint venture, or a special-purpose vehicle set up to deliver a single project. It
holds because of the **business entity concept**: the entity is accounted for separately from its owners,
its parent company, and any other project vehicle it sits alongside. A project's accounts record what
*the project entity* controls and owes, not what its shareholders or partners personally own.

**The underlying principle — double aspect.** Every economic event recorded in the accounts has two
effects, and those two effects always leave the equation in balance. If a company borrows cash, its
assets rise (more cash) and its liabilities rise by the same amount (the loan owed). If it buys equipment
for cash, one asset (cash) falls and another asset (equipment) rises by the same amount — total assets are
unchanged. This "double aspect" is the reason bookkeeping is called **double-entry**: every transaction
is recorded twice, once for each effect, and the two recordings must be numerically equal (sections 1.1.2–1.1.3).

**Formula.** Expanding the equity term:

`Assets = Liabilities + (Contributed capital + Retained earnings)`

`Retained earnings (closing) = Retained earnings (opening) + Net profit for the period − Distributions to owners`

Where: **Assets** = economic resources the entity controls as a result of past events, from which future
economic benefit is expected (cash, receivables, inventory, property, plant & equipment); **Liabilities**
= present obligations to transfer economic resources as a result of past events (loans, trade payables,
provisions — KA 1.4); **Equity** = the residual interest in the assets after deducting liabilities;
**Contributed capital** = amounts owners have paid into the entity; **Retained earnings** = accumulated
net profit not yet distributed; **Distributions** = dividends or owner drawings.

**Example 1.1.1 — the equation stays in balance.**

*Scenario.* A new project company, **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**, is formed on 1 March 2026 to
deliver a rail-signalling contract. In its first three days it records:

| # | Transaction | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Owners subscribe for share capital, paid in cash | 500,000 |
| 2 | Bank loan drawn down, received in cash | 200,000 |
| 3 | Site equipment purchased for cash | 150,000 |

*Required.* Show the effect of each transaction on the accounting equation.

*Working.*

| After transaction | Assets (Cash + Equipment) | = | Liabilities (Loan) | + | Equity (Share capital) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | 0 | = | 0 | + | 0 |
| 1 | 500,000 (cash) | = | 0 | + | 500,000 |
| 2 | 700,000 (cash) | = | 200,000 | + | 500,000 |
| 3 | 700,000 (550,000 cash + 150,000 equipment) | = | 200,000 | + | 500,000 |

*Answer.* **Assets = $700,000 = Liabilities $200,000 + Equity $500,000** after all three transactions.

*Interpretation.* Transaction 3 is an asset-for-asset exchange (cash converts into equipment) — it changes
the *composition* of assets but not the total, and neither liabilities nor equity move. This is the single
most common source of confusion for learners: not every transaction touches the profit or loss.

**Example 1.1.1b — a transaction that does move equity.** If Meridian later invoices a client $220,000
for certified work (Example 1.1.3), assets rise (a new receivable) and equity rises (revenue increases
retained earnings) — liabilities are untouched. The equation still balances: assets and equity both rise
by $220,000.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Assuming every transaction changes profit — many change only the *asset mix* or
swap an asset for a liability reduction. (2) Confusing the entity with its owners — an owner's personal
spending is never a transaction of the project entity. (3) Treating "the equation balances" as proof the
figures are *correct* — it only proves the double aspect was applied consistently (section 1.1.5 develops this
limitation fully).

**AI in this topic.** ERP and accounting platforms increasingly auto-classify transactions and flag when
an entry would break the equation before it can post — a useful, real-time control. Where AI is weaker is
judgement about the *entity boundary* itself: whether a joint-venture interest should be equity-accounted
or consolidated, or whether a special-purpose project vehicle is genuinely a separate entity for reporting
purposes, remains a professional accounting judgement that AI tools can flag but not resolve. AI proposes
a classification; the qualified accountant decides the entity question.

**Cross-references.** section 1.1.2 (how the two effects are recorded as debits and credits); KA 1.2 (how the
equation's three elements become the Statement of Financial Position); KA 1.5 (mapping accounts to a chart
of accounts and project WBS).

### 1.1.2 Debit and credit rules by account type

**Definition and purpose.** Double-entry bookkeeping records each transaction's two effects using the
conventions **debit** (Dr, conventionally the left side of an account) and **credit** (Cr, the right
side). The convention itself carries no moral or directional meaning ("debit" does not mean "bad") — it
is simply a consistent labelling system, in use since at least the 15th century in European commercial
practice, that lets every account type be increased or decreased predictably.

**The underlying principle.** Every account belongs to one of five types, and each type has a **normal
balance** — the side on which increases are recorded:

| Account type | Increases with | Decreases with | Normal balance | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asset | Debit | Credit | Debit | Cash, receivables, inventory, equipment |
| Expense | Debit | Credit | Debit | Labour cost, materials cost, depreciation |
| Liability | Credit | Debit | Credit | Loans, trade payables, provisions |
| Equity | Credit | Debit | Credit | Share capital, retained earnings |
| Income/Revenue | Credit | Debit | Credit | Contract revenue, interest income |

A useful way to hold this: **Assets and Expenses** sit on the same side of the accounting equation as
"uses" of resources and normally carry **debit** balances; **Liabilities, Equity and Income** sit on the
"sources of funding / performance" side and normally carry **credit** balances. A **contra-account** — an
account that offsets another within the same broad class, such as accumulated depreciation offsetting
equipment cost, or an allowance for doubtful debts offsetting receivables — takes the *opposite* normal
balance to the class it sits within: accumulated depreciation is an asset-related account but carries a
**credit** normal balance.

**Formula.** For every journal entry, by construction: `Σ Debits = Σ Credits`. This is not a separate
formula to solve — it is the *constraint* every entry must satisfy, and it is what makes the trial balance
(section 1.1.5) possible.

**Example 1.1.2 — classifying and recording four transactions.**

*Scenario.* Continuing Meridian Rail Contracting's first month (March 2026):

| # | Transaction | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Construction materials purchased on credit from a supplier | 80,000 |
| 5 | Materials consumed on site during the month | 60,000 |
| 6 | Site labour wages paid in cash | 45,000 |
| 7 | Subcontractor invoice received, unpaid at month-end | 70,000 |

*Required.* Identify the account type and Dr/Cr treatment for each.

*Working.*

| # | Debit (account, type) | Credit (account, type) | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | Materials inventory (Asset) ↑ | Accounts payable — supplier (Liability) ↑ | 80,000 |
| 5 | Direct project cost (Expense) ↑ | Materials inventory (Asset) ↓ | 60,000 |
| 6 | Labour cost (Expense) ↑ | Cash (Asset) ↓ | 45,000 |
| 7 | Subcontract cost (Expense) ↑ | Accounts payable — subcontractor (Liability) ↑ | 70,000 |

*Answer.* Four entries, each with equal Dr and Cr totals — $80,000, $60,000, $45,000 and $70,000
respectively.

*Interpretation.* Note transaction 5: it moves cost from the Statement of Financial Position (inventory,
an asset) into the Statement of Profit or Loss (direct project cost, an expense) as materials are
physically consumed — the accounting recognition follows the economic event, not the cash movement.

**Example 1.1.2b — a classification pitfall.** Suppose the site clerk records the materials purchase
(transaction 4) as a debit to "Materials expense" instead of "Materials inventory," on the (mistaken)
assumption that anything bought for the site is immediately a cost. Because the materials had not yet been
consumed, this overstates expense and understates the asset in the same period — both sides of the entry
still balance (Dr Materials expense 80,000 / Cr Accounts payable 80,000), so nothing about the entry
*looks* wrong in isolation. This is why the account **type**, not just the Dr/Cr mechanics, must be checked
against the chart of accounts and cost coding structure (KA 1.5) before posting.

*Chart type:* a 5-row reference table/grid (as above), colour-coded brand blue for "debit increases"
cells and a neutral grey for "credit increases" cells.
*Underlying data:* the five-row table above.
*Render-ready description:* a single-page quick-reference grid, sans-serif labels, suitable as a

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Reversing the rule for **contra-assets** — accumulated depreciation and
allowances for doubtful debts *increase* with a credit even though they relate to assets. (2) Recording
owner **drawings or dividends** as an expense — they reduce equity directly and never pass through the
Statement of Profit or Loss. (3) Assuming "debit" always means "more" — it means more only for
debit-normal account types; for a liability or income account, a debit *reduces* the balance.

**AI in this topic.** Modern ERP and invoice-capture tools use machine learning to suggest the debit/credit
account for a scanned invoice or bank-feed line, based on historical coding patterns. This materially
speeds up transaction capture, but the governance limit is real: the model learns *past* coding habits,
including past mistakes (Example 1.1.2b), and has no inherent concept of capital-versus-revenue
expenditure judgement or of a genuinely novel transaction type. Suggested coding should route through a
reviewer for anything above a materiality threshold or outside the training pattern, with the human
accountant retaining sign-off — AI proposes the code; the professional decides.

**Cross-references.** section 1.1.1 (why the two sides must be equal); section 1.1.3 (recording the entry in the
journal); KA 1.5 (chart of accounts and cost coding, including AI-assisted auto-coding at scale).

### 1.1.3 Recording transactions — the general journal

**Definition and purpose.** The **journal** is the book of original (chronological) entry: every
transaction is recorded there, in date order, before it is classified into individual accounts. A journal
entry states the date, the account(s) debited, the account(s) credited, the amounts, and a short narration
explaining the transaction's business purpose — the narration is what lets a reviewer or auditor
understand the entry months later without re-tracing the source document.

**The underlying principle.** Journalising separates *capture* (getting the transaction into the system
promptly and completely) from *classification and summarisation* (posting to the ledger, section 1.1.4). A
**simple entry** has one debit and one credit line; a **compound entry** has more than two lines but the
total debits must still equal the total credits.

**Format.** No formula beyond the balancing constraint `ΣDr = ΣCr` per entry; the standard layout is:

| Date | Account | Ref | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---|---|

**Example 1.1.3 — the full March journal for Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's complete set of transactions for March 2026 (its first month), including the
transactions introduced in sections 1.1.1–1.1.2 plus the remainder of the month:

| # | Transaction | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Share capital subscribed, received in cash | 500,000 |
| 2 | Bank loan drawn down, received in cash | 200,000 |
| 3 | Site equipment purchased for cash | 150,000 |
| 4 | Materials purchased on credit | 80,000 |
| 5 | Materials consumed on site | 60,000 |
| 6 | Site labour wages paid in cash | 45,000 |
| 7 | Subcontractor invoice received, unpaid | 70,000 |
| 8 | Client invoiced for work certified (interim application) | 220,000 |
| 9 | Cash received from client against invoice | 150,000 |
| 10 | Payment made to the materials supplier on account | 50,000 |
| 11 | Depreciation for the month on site equipment (straight-line, 60-month useful life: $150,000 ÷ 60) | 2,500 |

*Required.* Journalise all eleven transactions.

*Working.*

| # | Debit | Credit | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cash | Share capital | 500,000 |
| 2 | Cash | Loan payable | 200,000 |
| 3 | Equipment (cost) | Cash | 150,000 |
| 4 | Materials inventory | Accounts payable | 80,000 |
| 5 | Direct project cost | Materials inventory | 60,000 |
| 6 | Labour cost | Cash | 45,000 |
| 7 | Subcontract cost | Accounts payable | 70,000 |
| 8 | Accounts receivable | Contract revenue | 220,000 |
| 9 | Cash | Accounts receivable | 150,000 |
| 10 | Accounts payable | Cash | 50,000 |
| 11 | Depreciation expense | Accumulated depreciation | 2,500 |

*Answer.* Eleven balanced entries; every line's debit total equals its credit total.

*Interpretation.* Transaction 8 illustrates that revenue can be recognised (credited) before cash is
received — the debit is to a receivable, not to cash. The professional-level treatment of *when* and *how
much* revenue to recognise on a construction contract is the subject of IFRS 15 (KA 2.2); section 1.1.3 teaches
only the mechanical posting once the amount to recognise has been determined.

**Example 1.1.3b — a compound entry (illustrative variant, not part of the Meridian ledger).** Suppose
instead that Meridian had purchased its site equipment (transaction 3) by paying $100,000 cash immediately
and taking 30-day credit terms from the equipment supplier for the $50,000 balance. The single economic
event still produces one balanced entry, but now with three lines:

| Debit | Credit | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment (cost) | | 150,000 |
| | Cash | 100,000 |
| | Accounts payable — equipment supplier | 50,000 |

Total debits $150,000 = total credits $150,000. Compound entries are common wherever a transaction is
part-settled and part-financed — retention withheld on an invoice (KA 7.4) is a further example met later.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) An entry where debits and credits are unequal — must never be posted; most
systems block it, but manual adjustments and spreadsheet-based project accruals are where this slips
through. (2) Missing or uninformative narration — "misc adjustment" gives an auditor nothing to test.
(3) Posting a transaction to the wrong accounting period (a March invoice booked in April) — this breaks
the matching principle (KA 1.3) even though the entry itself balances.

**AI in this topic.** Robotic process automation (RPA) and AI document-extraction tools now read
invoices, timesheets and bank-feed data and auto-generate draft journal entries at scale — a major
efficiency gain in high-volume project environments. The governance limit: extraction errors (a
misread amount, a duplicated invoice, a misidentified supplier) propagate directly into the ledger if
entries post without review, and an AI system has no independent way to know a document is fraudulent
or duplicated. Practice should route AI-drafted entries through a reconciliation and approval control
before posting, especially for unusual, judgement-based, or high-value entries — AI drafts the entry;
the professional approves it.

### 1.1.4 The ledger — posting from journal to T-accounts

**Definition and purpose.** The **general ledger** is the complete set of accounts to which journal
entries are posted, classified and summarised. Each account is commonly visualised as a **T-account** —
debits on the left, credits on the right, with a running balance. Where transaction volume for a category
is high (e.g. many individual customers or suppliers), a **subsidiary ledger** keeps the detail (one
account per customer or supplier) while a single **control account** in the general ledger (Accounts
Receivable, Accounts Payable) carries the total — the two must always reconcile.

**The underlying principle.** Posting is purely mechanical *if* the journal entry was correctly classified
(section 1.1.2): each debit and credit line is transferred to its named account, and a posting reference links
the ledger entry back to its journal source — this cross-reference is what preserves the audit trail.

**Example 1.1.4 — posting Meridian's March journal to the ledger.**

*Scenario/Required.* Post all eleven entries from Example 1.1.3 and state each account's closing balance
at 31 March 2026.

*Working — the Cash T-account, in full (all amounts USD):*

| Debit | | Credit | |
|---|---|---|---|
| (1) Share capital | 500,000 | (3) Equipment | 150,000 |
| (2) Loan payable | 200,000 | (6) Labour cost | 45,000 |
| (9) Accounts receivable | 150,000 | (10) Accounts payable | 50,000 |
| **Total Dr** | **850,000** | **Total Cr** | **245,000** |

Closing balance = 850,000 − 245,000 = **$605,000 (debit)**.

*Working — closing balances for every account, posted the same way:*

| Account | Type | Closing balance | Side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | Asset | 605,000 | Dr |
| Accounts receivable | Asset | 70,000 | Dr |
| Materials inventory | Asset | 20,000 | Dr |
| Equipment (cost) | Asset | 150,000 | Dr |
| Accumulated depreciation | Contra-asset | 2,500 | Cr |
| Accounts payable | Liability | 100,000 | Cr |
| Loan payable | Liability | 200,000 | Cr |
| Share capital | Equity | 500,000 | Cr |
| Contract revenue | Income | 220,000 | Cr |
| Direct project cost | Expense | 60,000 | Dr |
| Labour cost | Expense | 45,000 | Dr |
| Subcontract cost | Expense | 70,000 | Dr |
| Depreciation expense | Expense | 2,500 | Dr |

*Answer.* Thirteen posted account balances, as above (full reconciliation to the trial balance in
section 1.1.5).

*Interpretation.* Accounts receivable nets the $220,000 billed against the $150,000 collected, leaving
$70,000 still owed by the client — this single balance is exactly what an interim payment-application
review (KA 7.4) would need to reconcile against the client's own records.

**Example 1.1.4b — subsidiary ledger reconciliation.** Meridian's Accounts Payable control account shows
a closing credit balance of $100,000 (above). The subsidiary (supplier-level) ledger records:

| Supplier | Invoiced | Paid | Closing balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supplier A — materials | 80,000 | 50,000 | 30,000 |
| Supplier B — subcontractor | 70,000 | 0 | 70,000 |
| **Total** | **150,000** | **50,000** | **100,000** |

The subsidiary ledger total ($100,000) reconciles exactly to the control account ($100,000) — a routine
but essential check performed every period; a mismatch signals a posting error, a duplicate, or a missing
invoice.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Posting to the wrong account (e.g. a subcontract cost posted to labour cost) —
balances but misclassifies; only detected by account-level review, not by the trial balance. (2)
Transposition errors when copying figures (e.g. $45,000 posted as $54,000) — these do cause an
imbalance and are usually divisible by 9, a classic diagnostic. (3) Omitting the posting reference,
which severs the audit trail back to the journal and source document — a control weakness even when the
numbers are right.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted reconciliation tools can automatically match subsidiary-ledger detail
to control-account totals across hundreds of accounts and flag breaks for review, and anomaly-detection
models can highlight postings that are statistically unusual (an account receiving an entry type it
never has before). These tools are strong at *surfacing* candidates for review; they do not, on their
own, establish *why* a break exists — that root-cause investigation remains the accountant's task (see
also KA 1.5's treatment of anomaly detection in project cost coding).

**Cross-references.** section 1.1.3 (the journal entries posted here); section 1.1.5 (compiling the trial balance from
these balances); KA 1.5 (control accounts within the cost breakdown structure).

### 1.1.5 The trial balance — preparation, purpose and limits

**Definition and purpose.** The **trial balance** is a listing, at a point in time, of every ledger
account's closing balance, split into a debit column and a credit column. Its purpose is narrow but
important: it proves that total debits equal total credits across the whole ledger — a check on the
*mechanical accuracy* of double-entry, and the working document from which the financial statements are
first drafted (KA 1.2).

**The underlying principle.** Because every journal entry was individually balanced (section 1.1.3) and posting
is a like-for-like transfer (section 1.1.4), the sum of all debit balances must equal the sum of all credit
balances *if, and only if,* every entry was posted completely and to the correct side. The trial balance
is therefore a **necessary but not sufficient** check: it will catch a one-sided posting or a
transposition that breaks the Dr = Cr identity, but it cannot catch an error that preserves that identity.

**Formula.** `Σ (Debit balances) = Σ (Credit balances)`. If unequal, the difference is temporarily parked
in a **suspense account** until the discrepancy is traced and corrected — the suspense account must never
appear in finalised financial statements.

**Example 1.1.5 — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC, trial balance at 31 March 2026.**

*Scenario/Required.* Using the closing balances from Example 1.1.4, prepare the trial balance.

*Working/Answer.*

| Account | Debit (USD) | Credit (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | 605,000 | |
| Accounts receivable | 70,000 | |
| Materials inventory | 20,000 | |
| Equipment (cost) | 150,000 | |
| Accumulated depreciation | | 2,500 |
| Accounts payable | | 100,000 |
| Loan payable | | 200,000 |
| Share capital | | 500,000 |
| Contract revenue | | 220,000 |
| Direct project cost | 60,000 | |
| Labour cost | 45,000 | |
| Subcontract cost | 70,000 | |
| Depreciation expense | 2,500 | |
| **Total** | **1,022,500** | **1,022,500** |

*Interpretation.* Debits and credits agree at **$1,022,500** — the ledger is mechanically in balance and
ready to be used as the basis for a trial set of financial statements (KA 1.2).

**Example 1.1.5b — an error the trial balance cannot catch.** Suppose the materials-consumption entry
(transaction 5, Example 1.1.2) had instead been debited in full to **Labour cost** rather than **Direct
project cost** — an **error of principle** (both are debit-normal expense accounts, so the entry still
balances). The trial balance would show Direct project cost at $0 and Labour cost at $105,000 instead of
$60,000 and $45,000 — but the total debit column would still sum to exactly $1,022,500, unchanged. The
trial balance offers no signal that anything is wrong; the error can only be found by reviewing individual
account activity against source documents (or, in a project-controls context, against the cost breakdown
structure in KA 1.5, where a $105,000 labour cost with no matching timesheet hours would stand out).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating "the trial balance balances" as proof the accounts are *correct* — it
is not; errors of **principle**, of **omission** (a transaction never recorded at all), of **commission**
(posted to the wrong account of the same type), and **compensating errors** (two unrelated mistakes that
happen to offset) all leave the trial balance in balance. (2) Leaving a suspense account balance in a
"final" trial balance used for reporting. (3) Confusing a trial balance with a Statement of Financial
Position — the trial balance is a working paper, not a financial statement, and expense/income accounts
have not yet been closed to retained earnings.

**AI in this topic.** Continuous-close and exception-monitoring tools can run trial-balance checks in
real time as transactions post, and can flag balances that move outside a historical or expected range
(a classic anomaly-detection use case). This shortens the detection window for genuine posting breaks
considerably. It does not close the fundamental gap described above: an AI model checking "does Dr equal
Cr" is checking exactly the same identity a human trial balance checks, and a compensating or
principle-based error that preserves that identity is, in general, just as invisible to the model unless
it is separately trained to reconcile balances against an independent source (e.g. timesheets, a physical
asset register) — which is a different, valuable control, not a substitute for professional review.

statements from this trial balance); KA 1.5 (chart of accounts, cost coding, and control-account
structures that make errors of principle easier to detect); KA 1.4 (provisions and accruals, tested in a

### 1.1.6 Closing the cycle: from trial balance to the accounting equation

**Definition and purpose.** This topic closes the loop opened in section 1.1.1: it reconciles the trial balance
back to `Assets = Liabilities + Equity`, and previews (without yet building — that is KA 1.2) how the
trial balance becomes a set of financial statements. It also demonstrates, on a second, independent
worked example, that the full cycle — journal → ledger → trial balance → equation — is the same
mechanism regardless of the size or currency of the entity.

**The underlying principle.** The trial balance's income and expense accounts summarise into **net
profit for the period**, which becomes part of closing equity; its asset and liability accounts (net of
accumulated depreciation) are the closing Statement of Financial Position elements. The accounting
equation must hold using these closing, "rolled-up" figures exactly as it held at each individual step.

**Example 1.1.6 — reconciling Meridian's trial balance to the equation.**

*Scenario/Required.* Using Example 1.1.5, compute Meridian's net profit for March 2026 and confirm that
Assets = Liabilities + Equity at 31 March 2026.

*Working.*

Net profit = Contract revenue − (Direct project cost + Labour cost + Subcontract cost + Depreciation
expense)
Net profit = 220,000 − (60,000 + 45,000 + 70,000 + 2,500) = 220,000 − 177,500 = **42,500**

Closing equity = Share capital + Net profit for the period (no distributions in month 1)
Closing equity = 500,000 + 42,500 = **542,500**

Closing assets = Cash + Accounts receivable + Inventory + Equipment (net of accumulated depreciation)
Closing assets = 605,000 + 70,000 + 20,000 + (150,000 − 2,500) = 605,000 + 70,000 + 20,000 + 147,500
= **842,500**

Closing liabilities = Accounts payable + Loan payable = 100,000 + 200,000 = **300,000**

Check: Liabilities + Equity = 300,000 + 542,500 = **842,500**

*Answer.* **Assets $842,500 = Liabilities $300,000 + Equity $542,500.** The equation holds exactly.

*Interpretation.* This is the professional's real-world check every period: if this equality does not
hold once income and expense accounts are rolled into equity, there is a posting or classification error
somewhere in the cycle, even though the raw trial balance in Example 1.1.5 appeared to balance. In
practice the roll-up and check are performed by the accounting system, but a project controls
professional should be able to reperform it by hand on a summarised trial balance — this is precisely the
bridge into KA 1.2, where these three totals become the formal Statement of Financial Position and
Statement of Profit or Loss.

**Example 1.1.6b — a second, independent full cycle (SAR).** To confirm the mechanism generalises, a
small Saudi site-services subcontractor, **Al-Noor Site Services**, records its first month wholly in
Saudi Riyal (no currency conversion implied — this is a SAR-functional entity):

| # | Transaction | Amount (SAR) |
|---|---|---|
| a | Owner injects capital, in cash | 300,000 |
| b | Hand tools purchased for cash | 45,000 |
| c | Site services delivered and invoiced; client pays immediately in cash | 120,000 |
| d | Site wages paid in cash | 38,000 |

*Journal (all four entries balanced):*

| # | Debit | Credit | Amount (SAR) |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | Cash | Capital | 300,000 |
| b | Tools (asset) | Cash | 45,000 |
| c | Cash | Service revenue | 120,000 |
| d | Wages expense | Cash | 38,000 |

*Ledger closing balances:* Cash = 300,000 − 45,000 + 120,000 − 38,000 = **337,000**; Tools = **45,000**;
Capital = **300,000**; Service revenue = **120,000**; Wages expense = **38,000**.

*Trial balance:* Debit side: 337,000 + 45,000 + 38,000 = **420,000**. Credit side: 300,000 + 120,000 =
**420,000**. Balances.

*Equation check:* Net profit = Service revenue 120,000 − Wages expense 38,000 = **82,000**. Closing
equity = Capital 300,000 + Net profit 82,000 = **382,000**. Closing assets = Cash 337,000 + Tools 45,000
= **382,000**. Closing liabilities = **0**.
Check: Liabilities + Equity = 0 + 382,000 = **382,000** = Assets **382,000**. ✓

*Interpretation.* Even a four-line, single-currency, no-liability entity must satisfy the same equation.
The professional's habit of independently recomputing net profit from the revenue and expense accounts,
then re-deriving equity and re-checking it against assets, is itself a control — it is exactly the kind
of arithmetic slip (for example, mis-subtracting the expense lines) that this final check is designed to
catch before a figure reaches a report.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Rolling income/expense accounts into equity incorrectly (e.g. omitting a cost
line from the net-profit calculation) — always recompute net profit independently, from the revenue and
expense account balances, as a cross-check before closing equity is derived. (2) Assuming the equation
check is redundant once the trial balance already balances — it is a *different*
check (roll-up arithmetic), not a repeat of the Dr = Cr check. (3) Applying an FX rate to a
single-currency entity's own accounts — Al-Noor's SAR figures are not converted from anything; illustrative
FX only applies where the spine's convention (section 4) is explicitly invoked for a cross-currency example.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted "close" dashboards can auto-generate the roll-up from trial balance to
summarised Statement of Financial Position/Statement of Profit or Loss figures, and can draft explanatory
commentary on period-on-period movements. As with every other step in this chapter, the output is a
strong first draft: the professional must independently verify the net-profit roll-up and the equation
check, precisely because — as Example 1.1.6b demonstrates — even a simple arithmetic step can be wrong
while every individual entry was correctly posted. AI proposes the summarised figures; the professional
decides by re-performing the check before it is reported externally.

### Key terms

- **Accounting equation** — Assets = Liabilities + Equity; the identity every posting must preserve.
- **Business entity concept** — the entity is accounted for separately from its owners or parent.
- **Double-entry** — every transaction recorded with equal, offsetting debit and credit effects.
- **Debit (Dr) / Credit (Cr)** — the left/right recording convention; effect depends on account type.
- **Normal balance** — the side (Dr or Cr) on which a given account type ordinarily carries a balance.
- **Contra-account** — an account offsetting another of the same broad class (e.g. accumulated
  depreciation against equipment cost); carries the opposite normal balance to its class.
- **Journal** — the book of original entry, recording transactions chronologically.
- **Compound entry** — a journal entry with more than one debit or credit line.
- **Posting** — transferring a journal entry's amounts to the relevant ledger accounts.
- **General ledger** — the complete set of accounts to which entries are posted.
- **Subsidiary ledger / Control account** — supplier/customer-level detail; the control account carries
  the summarised total in the general ledger and must reconcile to the subsidiary ledger.
- **Trial balance** — a listing of all ledger balances proving total debits equal total credits.
- **Suspense account** — a temporary holding account for an unreconciled trial-balance difference.
- **Error of principle / commission / omission / compensating error** — categories of error that a
  balanced trial balance will not, by itself, reveal.
- **Retained earnings** — accumulated net profit not yet distributed to owners.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 1.1.1 [level: recall]**
Under the accounting equation, which of the following correctly states the relationship for a limited
company?
A. Assets + Liabilities = Equity
B. Assets = Liabilities + Equity ✅
C. Assets = Liabilities − Equity
D. Liabilities = Assets + Equity

*Rationale:* B is the defining identity (section 1.1.1). A and D transpose terms incorrectly; C reverses the sign
of equity, which would make equity negative whenever assets exceed liabilities in a solvent entity.

**MCQ 1.1.2 [level: application — numerical]**
A project entity purchases equipment for $90,000, paying $60,000 in cash immediately and taking 30-day
credit terms for the balance. What is the effect on total assets at the moment of purchase?
A. Total assets increase by $90,000
B. Total assets increase by $30,000 ✅
C. Total assets are unchanged
D. Total assets decrease by $60,000

*Rationale:* Cash falls by $60,000, equipment rises by $90,000, net effect +$30,000, matched by a new
$30,000 accounts payable liability (Example 1.1.3b). Option A ignores the cash outflow; C wrongly assumes
a pure asset swap; D ignores the equipment recognised.

**MCQ 1.1.3 [level: application]**
Which of the following statements about accumulated depreciation is correct?
A. It is an expense account with a normal debit balance
B. It is a contra-asset account with a normal credit balance ✅
C. It is a liability account with a normal credit balance
D. It reduces equity directly without affecting any asset account

*Rationale:* Accumulated depreciation offsets the related asset's cost and carries a credit normal
balance (section 1.1.2), even though it relates to an asset, not a liability (C) or an expense (A) — the expense
is "depreciation expense," a separate account. D is incorrect because it is presented against the asset
on the Statement of Financial Position, not as a direct equity reduction.

**MCQ 1.1.4 [level: analysis]**
A trial balance balances, but a $15,000 subcontract cost was mistakenly posted in full to "Labour cost."
What best describes why the error was not detected by the trial balance?
A. The trial balance only checks liabilities, not expenses
B. Both accounts are debit-normal expense accounts, so total debits were unaffected ✅
C. The error caused an equal and opposite understatement in accounts payable
D. Trial balances cannot be prepared when errors of this type exist

*Rationale:* An error of principle/commission that stays within the same normal-balance side leaves
Σ Debits = Σ Credits unchanged (Example 1.1.5b) — the trial balance's Dr = Cr check is blind to it. A
misstates what the trial balance checks; C describes an unrelated, non-existent effect; D is false — the
trial balance would still be prepared and would still balance.

**MCQ 1.1.5 [level: application — numerical]**
Using Meridian Rail Contracting's March results (contract revenue $220,000; direct project cost $60,000;
labour cost $45,000; subcontract cost $70,000; depreciation expense $2,500), what is net profit for the
month?
A. $220,000
B. $177,500
C. $42,500 ✅
D. $45,000

*Rationale:* Net profit = 220,000 − (60,000+45,000+70,000+2,500) = 220,000 − 177,500 = 42,500 (Example
1.1.6). A is revenue before any costs; B is total costs, not the net result; D is only the labour cost
line.

### Self-check questions

1. Explain, using the accounting equation, why purchasing site equipment for cash does not change total
   assets, and contrast this with what happens when the equipment is instead purchased partly on credit.
2. A $60,000 materials cost was posted in full to the wrong expense account (a debit-normal account, same
   as the correct one). What type of error is this, and why does a balanced trial balance fail to reveal
   it? How would it likely be found in practice?
3. From Meridian Rail Contracting's ledger (Example 1.1.4b), reconcile the Accounts Payable subsidiary
   ledger (by supplier) to the Accounts Payable control account balance.
4. For Al-Noor Site Services (Example 1.1.6b), show the net-profit calculation and confirm that Assets =
   Liabilities + Equity at the end of the month.

**Answers**

1. A cash purchase is an asset-for-asset exchange: cash falls and equipment rises by the same amount, so
   total assets are unchanged and neither liabilities nor equity move. A part-credit purchase instead
   increases total assets (equipment rises by more than cash falls) and increases liabilities by the
   credit portion taken — the equation still balances, but at a higher total (Example 1.1.2 / 1.1.3b).
2. This is an error of principle/commission — an entry with equal, correctly-sized debit and credit
   amounts, posted to the wrong account of the same normal-balance side. Because total debits still equal
   total credits, the Dr = Cr trial-balance check cannot detect it (Example 1.1.5b); in practice it is
   found by account-level review against source documents, or by a cost-coding/WBS reconciliation flagging
   an implausible balance (KA 1.5).
3. Supplier A (materials): invoiced $80,000, paid $50,000, closing balance $30,000. Supplier B
   (subcontractor): invoiced $70,000, paid $0, closing balance $70,000. Total subsidiary ledger = $100,000,
   which reconciles exactly to the Accounts Payable control account balance of $100,000 (Example 1.1.5).
4. Net profit = Service revenue 120,000 − Wages expense 38,000 = SAR 82,000. Closing equity = Capital
   300,000 + Net profit 82,000 = SAR 382,000. Closing assets = Cash 337,000 + Tools 45,000 = SAR 382,000.
   Liabilities = 0. Check: 382,000 = 0 + 382,000 ✓.
