# Domain 1 — Foundations of Accounting for Project Controls

## KA 1.3 — Accrual accounting & the matching concept

Knowing how to post a balanced journal entry (KA 1.1) only answers *how* a transaction is recorded — it
says nothing about *which period* it belongs in. That question is the subject of this Knowledge Area, and
it is one of the most consequential a project controls professional faces: a cost report, an earned-value
curve or a monthly management account is only meaningful if revenue and cost are recognised in the period
they actually relate to, not merely the period in which cash happened to move. This KA develops the accrual
basis of accounting required by IFRS, the matching principle that underpins how project costs are aligned
with the revenue they help generate, and the four everyday adjusting entries — accrued expenses, accrued
income, prepayments and deferred income — that a controls team must identify at every period-end close.
The chapter continues Meridian Rail Contracting LLC's ledger (KA 1.1) into its second month of trading, so
the same entity and notation carry forward. Everything here is a direct prerequisite for provisions
(KA 1.4), the full financial statements (KA 1.2), IFRS 15 revenue recognition (KA 2.2) and the
over-/under-billing analysis performed in the commercial cycle (KA 7.5).

### 1.3.1 Cash basis versus accrual basis accounting

**Definition and purpose.** Under the **cash basis**, a transaction is recorded only when cash is received
or paid. Under the **accrual basis**, a transaction is recorded when the underlying economic event occurs —
revenue when it is earned, expense when it is incurred — regardless of when the associated cash moves.
IAS 1 *Presentation of Financial Statements* requires an entity to prepare its financial statements (other
than the Statement of Cash Flows itself, which is explicitly cash-based by design) on the accrual basis.
The Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting reinforces this: income and expenses are recognised when
they meet the definitions of, and recognition criteria for, changes in assets and liabilities — not simply
when cash changes hands.

**The underlying principle.** Cash timing is administratively convenient but economically arbitrary — a
client may take 60 days to pay a certified invoice, and a subcontractor's invoice may arrive weeks after
the work was performed. If a project's performance were judged on cash movements alone, a genuinely
profitable month could appear loss-making purely because collections lagged and payments fell due early
(and vice versa). Accrual accounting removes this timing noise so that the reported result reflects
economic performance for the period.

**Formula.**

`Cash-basis result (period) = Cash receipts − Cash payments`

`Accrual-basis result (period) = Revenue earned − Expenses incurred` (regardless of cash timing)

Both are expressed in the reporting currency (USD) for the period stated.

**Example 1.3.1 — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC, April 2026: cash basis vs accrual basis.**

*Scenario.* Meridian completed its first month of trading in March 2026 (KA 1.1). In April 2026 it
records:

| # | Transaction | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cash received from client, settling the March receivable (revenue already recognised in March) | 70,000 |
| 2 | New work certified and invoiced to the client in April (no cash received in April) | 180,000 |
| 3 | Subcontractor cost incurred on site in April; only part paid in cash in April ($60,000 paid, $50,000 remains unpaid at 30 April) | 110,000 |
| 4 | Site labour wages for April work, paid in cash in April | 45,000 |

*Required.* Compute Meridian's April result under (a) the cash basis and (b) the accrual basis.

*Working.*

(a) Cash basis: Cash in = 70,000 (txn 1) + 0 (txn 2) = **70,000**. Cash out = 60,000 (txn 3, part-paid) +
45,000 (txn 4) = **105,000**. Cash-basis result = 70,000 − 105,000 = **(35,000)**.

(b) Accrual basis: Revenue earned in April = 180,000 (txn 2 only — txn 1's cash relates to revenue already
earned and recognised in March). Expenses incurred in April = 110,000 (txn 3, the full cost incurred,
irrespective of the $50,000 still unpaid) + 45,000 (txn 4) = **155,000**. Accrual-basis result =
180,000 − 155,000 = **25,000**.

*Answer.* **Cash basis: a net outflow of $(35,000). Accrual basis: a profit of $25,000** for the same
month, the same underlying transactions.

*Interpretation.* The two bases disagree by $60,000 in the same direction for the wrong reason: the cash
figure is dragged down by paying for costs incurred (subcontract, labour) while genuinely new revenue
earned in April sits, uncollected, as a receivable. A project controls professional reading only a cash
report in April would see a $35,000 outflow and might wrongly conclude the project deteriorated; the
accrual result — a $25,000 profit — is the figure that reflects April's actual operating performance.

**Mini-case — why the distinction matters for a funding decision.** Meridian's parent group reviews cash
and accrual results monthly before releasing further working-capital funding to project entities. If the
April cash result alone were used as the funding trigger, Meridian would be flagged as underperforming and
funding could be delayed — even though the business earned a genuine $25,000 profit in the month and its
$50,000 unpaid subcontractor balance and $180,000 receivable are both short-term timing items, not signs of
distress. The correct control response is to review **both** bases together: cash basis for liquidity
management (can the entity pay its bills?), accrual basis for performance measurement (is the entity
profitable?) — neither basis alone answers both questions.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating a healthy cash balance as proof of profitability, or a cash shortfall as
proof of loss — liquidity and profitability are related but distinct questions (see the mini-case above).
(2) Recognising revenue at the point of invoicing without checking that the underlying work was genuinely
performed in that period — invoicing timing and earning timing usually coincide but are not the same test.
(3) Applying cash-basis thinking to internal project reports "because it's simpler," which silently
reintroduces the very distortion accrual accounting exists to remove.

**AI in this topic.** Cash-flow forecasting and dashboarding tools can automatically reconcile a project's
cash-basis and accrual-basis positions side by side and flag when the two diverge sharply — a genuinely
useful early-warning signal (a large, growing gap can indicate a collections problem or a billing backlog).
The governance limit is interpretive: an AI dashboard can compute the $60,000 gap in Example 1.3.1, but
deciding *why* it exists and whether it is benign timing or an emerging risk is a professional judgement
informed by contract terms, client payment history and site knowledge that the tool does not have. AI
proposes the reconciliation; the professional decides what it means.

### 1.3.2 The matching principle: aligning cost with the revenue it earns

**Definition and purpose.** The **matching principle** (historically "the matching concept") holds that
expenses should be recognised in the same period as the revenue they helped generate, rather than in the
period cash was paid or an invoice was received. It is why, on a construction contract, cost of work
incurred is recognised against revenue as the work progresses, not dumped into the period an invoice
happens to land in. A technical nuance worth stating honestly: the IFRS Conceptual Framework no longer
treats "matching" as a free-standing override — expenses are formally recognised when they meet the
definition of a decrease in assets or increase in liabilities that can be measured reliably (an
asset/liability-driven approach), not simply "because there was matching revenue." In practice, however,
the *outcome* looks identical for project costs: IAS 2 *Inventories* requires cost of inventories to be
expensed in the period the related revenue is recognised, and IFRS 15's input-method percentage of
completion (KA 2.2) is, in substance, cost-to-revenue matching applied to a long-term contract. This KA
therefore keeps "matching" as the practical working description project controls professionals use daily,
while flagging that the formal IFRS recognition test sits one level underneath it.

**The underlying principle.** Matching prevents two distortions: recognising cost too early (before the
related revenue, understating current-period profit and overstating a later period) and recognising cost
too late (overstating current-period profit). On a multi-period contract, the practical mechanism is the
input-method percentage of completion introduced in the spine's canonical formula set and developed fully
in KA 2.2 and KA 3.2.

**Formula.**

`% complete = costs incurred to date / total estimated costs`

`Revenue recognised to date = % complete × total contract price`

`Matched (recognised) gross profit to date = Revenue recognised to date − costs incurred to date`

**Example 1.3.2 — the Trackbed Drainage subcontract package.**

*Scenario.* Meridian holds a fixed-price subcontract package, "Trackbed Drainage," with a contract price of
$500,000 and a total estimated cost to complete of $400,000 (a budgeted margin of $100,000, i.e. 20% of
price). By 30 April 2026, cumulative costs incurred on the package total $100,000.

*Required.* Determine the revenue and gross profit Meridian should recognise to date under the matching
(input-method percentage-of-completion) approach.

*Working.*

% complete = 100,000 / 400,000 = **25.0%**

Revenue recognised to date = 25.0% × 500,000 = **$125,000**

Matched gross profit to date = 125,000 − 100,000 = **$25,000**

*Answer.* **Revenue $125,000; matched cost $100,000; gross profit $25,000** (a 20.0% margin on revenue
recognised, consistent with the 20% margin budgeted into the package as a whole).

*Interpretation.* Cost and revenue move together in lockstep because both are driven by the same 25%
progress figure — this is the matching principle in action on a project, and it is exactly the mechanism
KA 2.2 develops in full for IFRS 15 long-term contracts.

**Mini-case — matching versus the amount actually invoiced.** Suppose the client's April certificate,
based on measured quantities under the BoQ (KA 7.3), values Meridian's progress at only $90,000 for
billing purposes — a different figure from the $125,000 matching calculation above, because billing
measurement and cost-based progress rarely move at identical rates. If Meridian's management report used
the *invoiced* amount as "revenue," it would understate April's true performance by $35,000 and would be
reporting on a billing basis, not an accrual/matching basis. The correct accrual treatment recognises the
full $125,000 as revenue and carries the $35,000 gap (`revenue recognised − amounts billed`, the spine's
canonical contract asset/liability formula) as a **contract asset** — unbilled revenue — on the statement
of financial position, pending formal certification. This is developed fully in KA 2.2 (IFRS 15) and
KA 7.5 (over-/under-billing); here the point is narrower: *matching drives revenue recognition, billing
does not.*

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Recognising revenue on the amount invoiced rather than the amount earned — a
billing-basis error, not an accrual error, but one that produces the same kind of distortion. (2) Matching
cost incurred against the *contract price* directly instead of against the *proportion of price earned* —
the correct comparison is always cost-to-date versus revenue-to-date, not cost-to-date versus the full
contract value. (3) Assuming the formal IFRS recognition test and the everyday "matching" description are
different rules that could give different answers — for project costs they are, in substance, the same
outcome reached by different reasoning paths (see the technical nuance above).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted cost-to-complete tools can compute percentage-of-completion automatically
once cost-to-date and an estimated-total-cost figure are supplied, and can flag packages where the
cost-based % complete and an independently reported physical-progress % complete diverge — a useful
consistency check. The governance limit is that the **estimated total cost** (the denominator) is itself a
forecast requiring professional judgement (cross-refer KA 3.2 estimate classes, KA 6.3 EAC methods); an AI
tool computing a precise-looking 25.0% from an unreliable estimate produces false precision, not accuracy.
The professional remains responsible for the estimate-to-complete input, not just for accepting the tool's
arithmetic.

### 1.3.3 Accruals: recognising costs and revenue before the invoice arrives

**Definition and purpose.** An **accrual** is a period-end adjusting entry that recognises an expense
incurred, or income earned, before the supporting invoice has been received or issued. An **accrued
expense** records a cost the entity has genuinely consumed but not yet been billed for; **accrued income**
(also called unbilled revenue) records revenue genuinely earned but not yet invoiced to the client. Both
exist because the accrual basis (§1.3.1) requires recognition when the economic event occurs, and invoices
frequently lag the event itself by days or weeks.

**The underlying principle.** An accrual is an *estimate*, made from the best available evidence at
period-end (site records, measured quantities, timesheets, progress reports), reversed once the actual
invoice arrives so the true amount can be recorded without double-counting. This reversing mechanism is
developed fully in §1.3.5.

**Formula.**

Accrued expense entry: `Dr Expense / Cr Accrued expenses (liability)` — amount = best estimate of cost
incurred but not yet invoiced.

Accrued income entry: `Dr Accrued income (asset) / Cr Revenue` — amount = best estimate of revenue earned
but not yet invoiced.

**Example 1.3.3 — accrued subcontract cost.**

*Scenario.* At 30 April 2026, Meridian's site team estimates that a subcontractor performed $18,000 of
additional drainage work in the final week of April. The subcontractor's invoice is not expected until
10 May.

*Required.* Record the 30 April adjusting entry and the actual invoice when it is received.

*Working.*

30 April (accrual): `Dr Subcontract cost 18,000 / Cr Accrued expenses 18,000`

1 May (reversal — see §1.3.5): `Dr Accrued expenses 18,000 / Cr Subcontract cost 18,000`

10 May (actual invoice received, for a slightly higher amount reflecting overtime hours): `Dr Subcontract
cost 18,500 / Cr Accounts payable 18,500`

*Answer.* April's accounts correctly carry **$18,000** of subcontract cost for work genuinely performed in
April. May's accounts, after the reversal nets against the new invoice, carry the **$500** difference
(18,500 − 18,000) as the true incremental May cost.

*Interpretation.* Across the two months, total subcontract cost recognised is 18,000 + 500 = $18,500 —
exactly the actual invoice amount — with each month bearing only the cost that belongs to it. This is the
matching principle (§1.3.2) applied through time.

**Example 1.3.3b — accrued income (unbilled revenue).** Meridian's site team also identifies that
client-approved additional works worth $27,000 were completed in the last days of April but will only be
formally certified and invoiced in May.

30 April (accrual): `Dr Accrued income 27,000 / Cr Contract revenue 27,000`

May (certified and invoiced at exactly the accrued amount): `Dr Accounts receivable 27,000 / Cr Accrued
income 27,000` — a reclassification, not new May revenue, because the $27,000 was already recognised in
April.

*Interpretation.* Had this accrual been omitted, April revenue would have been understated by $27,000 and
May revenue overstated by the same amount when the invoice was eventually raised — the total earned across
the two months is correct either way, but the *period* in which the performance is reported would be
wrong. For a project controls professional reporting monthly progress against a baseline, that period
error is exactly the kind of distortion accrual accounting is designed to prevent.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Forgetting to reverse an accrual, then posting the actual invoice on top of it —
this double-counts the cost or revenue in the following period. (2) Accruing on unreliable or unsupported
evidence (a verbal estimate with no measured basis) — an accrual must still be the best available estimate
from credible evidence, not a guess. (3) Confusing an accrual with a provision (KA 1.4): an accrual is for
a cost or income that is *known to have occurred* and is *reliably measurable* from near-certain evidence
(e.g. work physically completed); a provision covers a liability of *uncertain timing or amount* (e.g. a
possible warranty claim) — the two are recognised on different evidential thresholds.

**AI in this topic.** Machine-learning tools can compare site-progress data, timesheets and purchase-order
commitments against invoices actually received, flagging work that appears to have been performed but not
yet billed by either party — a genuinely useful accrual-completeness check at scale, especially across
hundreds of cost lines. The governance limit: such tools detect *candidates* for accrual from structured
data trails; they cannot verify that informally instructed or undocumented work occurred, and a model that
is not conservative can propose accruals with unwarranted confidence. Every AI-suggested accrual should be
corroborated against a human-verifiable source (a measured quantity, a signed instruction, a timesheet)
before posting — AI proposes the candidate list; the professional confirms the evidence and the amount.

**Cross-references.** §1.3.1 (why timing distortion matters); §1.3.2 (matching as the reason accruals

(cost coding, which an accrual must still be coded correctly against).

### 1.3.4 Deferrals: prepayments and deferred (unearned) income

**Definition and purpose.** A **deferral** is the mirror image of an accrual: cash moves *first*, and
recognition follows as the related benefit is consumed or the related performance is delivered. A
**prepayment** (prepaid expense) is a cost paid in advance of the period it relates to — it sits on the
statement of financial position as an **asset** until consumed, at which point it is expensed. **Deferred
income** (unearned revenue) is cash received in advance of performance — it sits as a **liability** until
the entity has actually earned it by delivering the promised goods or services, matching the contract
liability concept IFRS 15 uses for advance payments and mobilisation fees (KA 2.2).

**The underlying principle.** Recognising the whole of a prepayment as an expense (or the whole of an
advance receipt as revenue) in the period cash moves would overstate that period's result and understate
later periods — exactly the distortion accrual accounting exists to prevent, approached from the opposite
direction to §1.3.3.

**Formula.**

`Prepaid asset remaining = Amount paid − Amount consumed/expensed to date`

`Deferred income liability remaining = Amount received − Amount earned to date`

Where the benefit or performance is delivered evenly over time, the systematic (straight-line) basis is:

`Amount recognised per period = Total prepayment or advance / Number of periods covered`

**Example 1.3.4 — prepaid insurance.**

*Scenario.* On 1 April 2026, Meridian pays $24,000 for a 12-month site insurance policy covering 1 April
2026 to 31 March 2027.

*Required.* Record the initial payment and the 30 April adjusting entry, and state the prepaid asset
remaining.

*Working.*

1 April (payment): `Dr Prepaid insurance 24,000 / Cr Cash 24,000`

Monthly expense = 24,000 / 12 = **$2,000**

30 April (adjustment): `Dr Insurance expense 2,000 / Cr Prepaid insurance 2,000`

Prepaid asset remaining at 30 April = 24,000 − 2,000 = **$22,000**

*Answer.* **April insurance expense $2,000; prepaid asset carried forward $22,000.**

*Interpretation.* Only one month's benefit has been consumed by 30 April, so only one month's cost belongs
in April's result; the remaining eleven months' worth stays on the statement of financial position as an
asset and will be released at $2,000 a month as each month's cover is used up.

**Example 1.3.4b — deferred income on a mobilisation advance.** On 5 April 2026, Meridian's client pays a
$100,000 mobilisation advance for a new work package to be delivered evenly over the following five months
(April to August 2026 inclusive).

5 April (receipt): `Dr Cash 100,000 / Cr Deferred income 100,000`

Monthly revenue recognised = 100,000 / 5 = **$20,000**

30 April (adjustment): `Dr Deferred income 20,000 / Cr Contract revenue 20,000`

Deferred income liability remaining at 30 April = 100,000 − 20,000 = **$80,000**

Continuing the same pattern, the liability runs down as follows:

| Month-end | Revenue recognised in month (USD) | Deferred income liability remaining (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| 30 Apr | 20,000 | 80,000 |
| 31 May | 20,000 | 60,000 |
| 30 Jun | 20,000 | 40,000 |
| 31 Jul | 20,000 | 20,000 |
| 31 Aug | 20,000 | 0 |

*Interpretation.* The advance is a **contract liability** — cash Meridian holds but has not yet earned —
until the corresponding work is delivered; it is never revenue at the point of receipt. KA 2.2 develops the
IFRS 15 judgement on *how* the release pattern should be determined (straight-line here, for teaching
simplicity, is only appropriate if performance genuinely occurs evenly; IFRS 15 requires the pattern to
reflect the actual transfer of goods or services, which may not be straight-line on a real project).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Recognising an advance receipt as revenue on receipt — this is a cash-basis error
that directly contradicts §1.3.1. (2) Expensing a prepayment in full at the point of payment "to keep it
simple" — this overstates the current period's cost and understates future periods'. (3) Assuming a
straight-line release is always correct — it is only correct where the underlying benefit or performance is
genuinely even across the period; an uneven delivery profile (e.g. most mobilisation activity happening in
month one) requires a release pattern that reflects that profile, not a mechanical 1/n split.

**AI in this topic.** Contract- and subscription-tracking assistants can automatically schedule prepayment
and deferred-income amortisation once a total amount and a term are entered, generating the month-by-month
release table shown above without manual recalculation — a real efficiency gain for a controls team
managing many small prepayments and advances. The governance limit is judgement, not arithmetic: deciding
*whether* straight-line genuinely reflects the pattern of benefit or performance (as opposed to being the
tool's default assumption) is a professional call, particularly where IFRS 15's output-method guidance
(KA 2.2) points to an uneven pattern. An AI schedule is a strong first draft of the mechanics, not a
substitute for that judgement.

### 1.3.5 The period-end adjustment cycle: bringing it together

**Definition and purpose.** Before a period's management accounts or financial statements are finalised, a
controls or finance team runs a **period-end close**: reviewing the unadjusted ledger for accruals,
deferrals and other adjustments (depreciation, KA 1.1 §1.1.3; provisions, KA 1.4) still required to reflect
the period correctly. For accruals specifically, it is common practice — though not a universal
requirement — to post a **reversing entry** at the start of the next period, so the following period's
actual invoice can be recorded normally without the bookkeeper having to remember and net off a prior
estimate. Deferrals (prepayments and deferred income) are **not** reversed in this way: they are simply
amortised down, period by period, until the balance reaches zero (§1.3.4).

**The underlying principle.** Every adjustment in §1.3.3 and §1.3.4 changes reported profit relative to the
unadjusted, cash-driven figures a system produces automatically from invoices and payments alone. A
disciplined close therefore reconciles "profit before period-end review" to "profit after period-end
review" explicitly, so nothing is missed and every adjustment is traceable to its supporting evidence.

**Formula.**

`Adjusted profit = Unadjusted profit + Accrued income + Deferred income earned − Accrued expenses −
Prepayments expensed`

(each term restricted to the amount relating to the period under review).

**Example 1.3.5 — Meridian's April 2026 period-end adjustment bridge.**

*Scenario.* Before period-end review, Meridian's April ledger shows the unadjusted position from Example
1.3.1: revenue $180,000, expenses $155,000 (subcontract cost $110,000 + labour $45,000), giving an
unadjusted profit of $25,000. The insurance payment and mobilisation advance (Example 1.3.4/1.3.4b) sit
correctly on the statement of financial position as a prepaid asset and a deferred-income liability, not
yet touching profit. At period-end, the controls team identifies the four adjustments developed in §1.3.3
and §1.3.4.

*Required.* Build the adjustment bridge from unadjusted to adjusted April profit.

*Working.*

| Item | Effect | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| Unadjusted profit (Example 1.3.1) | | 25,000 |
| + Accrued income — unbilled work earned in April (Example 1.3.3b) | Revenue ↑ | 27,000 |
| + Deferred income earned in April (Example 1.3.4b) | Revenue ↑ | 20,000 |
| − Accrued expense — unbilled subcontract cost (Example 1.3.3) | Expense ↑ | (18,000) |
| − Prepaid insurance expensed for April (Example 1.3.4) | Expense ↑ | (2,000) |
| **Adjusted profit for April** | | **52,000** |

Check by re-deriving directly: adjusted revenue = 180,000 + 27,000 + 20,000 = 227,000; adjusted expenses =
155,000 + 18,000 + 2,000 = 175,000; adjusted profit = 227,000 − 175,000 = **52,000** — consistent with the
bridge.

*Answer.* **Adjusted April profit is $52,000**, more than double the $25,000 unadjusted figure.

*Interpretation.* Three of the four adjustments increase reported profit (accrued income, deferred income
earned) and two increase reported cost (accrued expense, prepaid expensed) — the net effect here happens to
be a $27,000 increase, but there is nothing systematic about the direction: a different period could easily
see accruals reduce profit. The value of the bridge is that every adjustment is visible and individually
traceable to the evidence behind it, rather than the $52,000 appearing as a single unexplained number.

**Mini-case — the reversing entries on 1 May.** Following the convention stated above, Meridian reverses
the two *accrual* entries on 1 May, but does not reverse the two *deferral* entries:

`Dr Accrued expenses 18,000 / Cr Subcontract cost 18,000` (reverses the estimate; the actual $18,500
invoice, when it arrives on 10 May, posts normally per Example 1.3.3)

`Dr Contract revenue 27,000 / Cr Accrued income 27,000` (reverses the estimate; the actual May certificate,
when raised, posts normally per Example 1.3.3b)

The prepaid insurance ($22,000 remaining) and deferred income ($80,000 remaining) are left exactly as they
stand at 30 April — they are not "wrong" balances needing correction, they are asset/liability balances
that will continue to run down through May, June and beyond exactly as scheduled in Example 1.3.4b. A
controls professional who reversed *every* period-end adjustment indiscriminately would incorrectly wipe
out genuine prepaid and deferred-income balances that still belong on the statement of financial position.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Reversing a deferral by mistake, alongside the genuine accrual reversals — this
incorrectly erases a real asset or liability balance still on the books. (2) Running the period-end review
so late that the adjustments miss the reporting deadline, forcing a restatement. (3) Treating the bridge as
a one-off exercise rather than a recurring monthly discipline — every period needs its own review, because
last period's accruals reverse and new ones must be identified afresh.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted close-management tools can compile a draft adjustment pack automatically
from source data — outstanding purchase orders and goods-received notes not yet invoiced, measured-progress
records not yet certified, prepayment and advance schedules due for release — cutting the time a controls
team spends assembling the bridge shown above. This is squarely the domain's governing principle in
practice: the tool proposes a complete, evidence-linked draft adjustment pack; the qualified professional
reviews each line, confirms the evidence, and signs off the pack before it enters the management accounts
or financial statements. AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.

### Key terms

- **Cash basis** — recognising a transaction only when cash is received or paid.
- **Accrual basis** — recognising revenue when earned and expense when incurred, regardless of cash timing;
  required by IAS 1 for financial statements other than the Statement of Cash Flows.
- **Matching principle** — aligning the recognition of cost with the revenue it helped generate; in
  practice reached today via the Conceptual Framework's asset/liability recognition criteria.
- **Accrued expense** — a cost incurred but not yet invoiced or paid at period-end; recorded as an expense
  and a liability.
- **Accrued income (unbilled revenue)** — revenue earned but not yet invoiced at period-end; recorded as
  revenue and an asset.
- **Prepayment (prepaid expense)** — a cost paid in advance of the period it relates to; carried as an
  asset until consumed.
- **Deferred income (unearned revenue / contract liability)** — cash received in advance of performance;
  carried as a liability until earned.
- **Adjusting entry** — a period-end entry (accrual, deferral, depreciation, provision) that corrects the
  unadjusted ledger to the correct period.
- **Reversing entry** — an entry, conventionally applied to accruals only, that reverses a prior-period
  estimate on the first day of the new period so the actual invoice can post without double-counting.
- **Period-end close** — the recurring discipline of identifying and posting all required adjustments
  before a period's results are finalised.
- **Contract asset / contract liability** — the IFRS 15 terms (developed in KA 2.2) for, respectively,
  unbilled revenue earned and cash received before performance.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 1.3.1 [level: recall]**
Under IFRS, which basis of accounting is required for the financial statements, other than the Statement
of Cash Flows itself?
A. Cash basis
B. Accrual basis ✔
C. Modified cash basis
D. Fair value basis

*Rationale:* IAS 1 requires the accrual basis for financial statements other than cash flow information
(§1.3.1). A describes the basis that is explicitly excepted only for the cash flow statement itself; C and
D are not the IFRS requirement for general-purpose financial statements.

**MCQ 1.3.2 [level: application — numerical]**
Using Meridian's April 2026 data (Example 1.3.1): $70,000 cash received (relating to March revenue),
$180,000 of new work invoiced in April, $110,000 of subcontract cost incurred in April ($60,000 paid),
and $45,000 of labour paid in cash. What is the accrual-basis profit for April?
A. $(35,000)
B. $25,000 ✔
C. $70,000
D. $180,000

*Rationale:* Accrual profit = revenue earned (180,000) − expenses incurred (110,000 + 45,000 = 155,000) =
25,000. A is the cash-basis result; C is cash received alone; D is revenue alone with no costs deducted.

**MCQ 1.3.3 [level: application — numerical]**
A contractor pays $24,000 on 1 April for a 12-month insurance policy covering 1 April to the following
31 March. Under accrual accounting, what insurance expense should be recognised for April alone?
A. $24,000
B. $2,000 ✔
C. $0
D. $22,000

*Rationale:* 24,000 ÷ 12 months = $2,000 per month (Example 1.3.4). A expenses the whole prepayment
immediately, a cash-basis error; D is the prepaid asset remaining after April, not the April expense; C
wrongly ignores that one month of cover has already elapsed.

**MCQ 1.3.4 [level: application — numerical]**
A client pays a $100,000 mobilisation advance on 5 April, to be released evenly as revenue over five
months (April–August). What deferred income liability remains at 30 June, after three months' revenue has
been recognised?
A. $100,000
B. $80,000
C. $40,000 ✔
D. $0

*Rationale:* Monthly release = 100,000 ÷ 5 = 20,000; after three months (April, May, June), 60,000 has been
earned, leaving 100,000 − 60,000 = $40,000 (Example 1.3.4b). A is the balance before any release; B is the
balance after only one month; D assumes all five months have elapsed.

**MCQ 1.3.5 [level: analysis]**
Which statement correctly distinguishes an accrued expense from a prepayment?
A. An accrued expense is cash paid in advance of consumption; a prepayment is a cost incurred but not yet
invoiced.
B. An accrued expense is a cost incurred but not yet paid or invoiced; a prepayment is cash paid in advance
of the cost being incurred. ✔
C. Both are liabilities on the statement of financial position.
D. Both are conventionally reversed at the start of the following period.

*Rationale:* B correctly states the mirror-image relationship between §1.3.3 and §1.3.4. A swaps the two
definitions; C is wrong because a prepayment is an asset, not a liability; D is wrong because reversing
entries are conventionally applied to accruals, not to prepayments, which simply amortise down (§1.3.5).

**MCQ 1.3.6 [level: application — numerical]**
The Trackbed Drainage package has a contract price of $500,000, total estimated cost of $400,000, and
$140,000 of costs incurred to date. Applying the input-method percentage of completion, what revenue
should be recognised to date?
A. $140,000
B. $175,000 ✔
C. $400,000
D. $500,000

*Rationale:* % complete = 140,000 ÷ 400,000 = 35.0%; revenue recognised = 35.0% × 500,000 = $175,000
(§1.3.2). A is only the cost incurred, not revenue; C and D apply the wrong base value without the
% complete step.

### Self-check questions

1. Using Meridian's April 2026 data (Example 1.3.1), explain why the cash-basis result showed a net
   outflow while the accrual-basis result showed a profit, and state which figure better reflects the
   underlying performance of the business for project-controls purposes.
2. A subcontractor completes $18,000 of work in the last week of April that is not invoiced until May.
   Explain the adjusting entry required at 30 April and the reversing entry required on 1 May, and state
   why the reversal is needed.
3. Using the Trackbed Drainage package (Example 1.3.2), explain why the amount invoiced to the client in a
   given period can differ from the revenue recognised in that period, and name the statement-of-financial-
   position item that captures the difference.
4. A client's $100,000 mobilisation advance is released evenly as revenue over five months. Calculate the
   deferred income balance remaining at the end of month four.

**Answers**

1. The cash-basis result reflects only the timing of cash movements (collecting March's receivable, paying
   subcontractors and labour) and ignores that $180,000 of new work was earned and invoiced in April; the
   accrual-basis result matches revenue earned against costs incurred in the same period regardless of cash
   timing and is the figure that reflects April's true operating performance (Example 1.3.1).
2. At 30 April: `Dr Subcontract cost 18,000 / Cr Accrued expenses 18,000`, recognising the cost in the
   period it was incurred. On 1 May: `Dr Accrued expenses 18,000 / Cr Subcontract cost 18,000`, reversing
   the estimate so the actual invoice — whatever its final amount — can be recorded normally in May without
   double-counting the cost already recognised in April (§1.3.3, §1.3.5).
3. Revenue is recognised by matching costs incurred to date against total estimated cost and price (the
   input-method percentage of completion); the amount invoiced follows the contract's separate billing and
   measurement terms, which may lag or lead progress. The difference (here, $125,000 recognised − $90,000
   invoiced = $35,000) is captured as a **contract asset** (unbilled revenue), formally developed under
   IFRS 15 in KA 2.2 and KA 7.5.
4. Monthly release = 100,000 ÷ 5 = $20,000. After four months, 4 × 20,000 = $80,000 has been earned, so
   the deferred income balance remaining is 100,000 − 80,000 = **$20,000**.
