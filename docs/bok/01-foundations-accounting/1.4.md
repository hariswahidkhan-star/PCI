# Domain 1 — Foundations of Accounting for Project Controls

## KA 1.4 — Cost provisions & cost accruals

Every project eventually meets an obligation whose *existence* is clear but whose *amount* or *timing* is
not: a warranty claim that has not yet arrived, a subcontractor dispute that has not yet settled, a
contract that has quietly turned loss-making before the final account is agreed. Getting these onto the
books correctly — not too early, not too late, not too large, not too small — is the discipline this
Knowledge Area teaches. It builds directly on the double-entry mechanics of KA 1.1 and the timing logic of
KA 1.3 (accrual accounting and the matching concept), and it applies them to the single standard that
governs this territory: **IAS 37 *Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets***. The chapter
distinguishes a provision from a simple accrual and from a trade payable; sets out IAS 37's three-part
recognition test; measures a provision using the expected-value method and, where material, discounting;
and works through the two cases a project controls professional meets most often — a **warranty provision**
and a **contract-loss (onerous contract) provision** — using the continuing worked entity, **Meridian Rail
Contracting LLC**, introduced in KA 1.1. The onerous-contract treatment in particular sits exactly where
project controls work meets financial reporting: a forecast cost overrun surfaced by an EAC review (KA 3.4,
KA 6.3) can, past a threshold, become a legal recognition event under IAS 37 — a fact every professional
producing a cost forecast needs to understand, even though the accounting entry itself is prepared by
finance.

### 1.4.1 The recognition boundary: accruals, trade payables and provisions

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 37 defines a **provision** as *a liability of uncertain timing or amount*.
This single phrase is the boundary that separates a provision from three neighbouring items that a project
cost report also carries:

- A **trade payable** is a liability for goods or services that have been received and invoiced — the
  amount and the timing of settlement are both known with reasonable certainty (subject only to normal
  payment terms).
- An **accrued expense** (an "accrual" in the everyday project-controls sense used in KA 1.3) is a
  liability for goods or services that have been *received* but not yet *invoiced* — the amount can
  normally be estimated with a high degree of reliability from a delivery note, a timesheet or a meter
  reading, and the timing of settlement is not genuinely in doubt.
- A **provision** sits a step further along the same spectrum: both the *amount* and/or the *timing* carry
  genuine uncertainty, and measuring it requires a judgement-based estimate rather than a straightforward
  calculation from a source document.

**The underlying principle.** All three are liabilities and all three satisfy the accounting equation in
exactly the way KA 1.1 describes — a recognised obligation matched by a same-period expense. What changes
along the spectrum is the *degree of estimation uncertainty*, and that has direct consequences: a provision
must clear IAS 37's specific three-part recognition test (section 1.4.2) before it is recognised at all, must be
remeasured every reporting period (section 1.4.6), and — where the difference matters — may need to be discounted
to present value (section 1.4.3), none of which routinely applies to a trade payable or a simple accrual.

**Formula.** No numerical formula attaches to classification itself; the qualifying test is logical:

`A liability is a provision if, and only if: [amount uncertain OR timing uncertain] AND [it is a present
obligation from a past event] AND [settlement will probably require an outflow of resources]`

The last two conditions are developed fully as IAS 37's recognition test in section 1.4.2.

**Example 1.4.1 — classifying five items at Meridian's December 2026 month-end review.**

*Scenario.* Meridian Rail Contracting LLC is nine months into its 24-month, $10,000,000 fixed-price
rail-signalling contract (commenced 1 April 2026). At the 31 December 2026 month-end close, five items are
on the review list:

| # | Item | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | December electricity for the site compound; invoice not yet received, but usage is known from the meter reading | 8,400 |
| 2 | The contractual two-year workmanship warranty that will attach to the signalling installation on handover | to be estimated (section 1.4.3) |
| 3 | A supplier's invoice for materials delivered in November, received and agreed, unpaid at month-end | 54,000 |
| 4 | A former subcontractor's claim alleging breach of contract; Meridian's lawyers assess the chance of losing as *possible*, not *probable* | 250,000 claimed |
| 5 | An insurance claim lodged for storm damage to site cabins; the insurer has confirmed settlement in writing and recovery is virtually certain | 75,000 |

*Required.* Classify each item and state whether, and how, it is recognised.

*Working.*

| # | Classification | Recognised? | On the books as |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Accrued expense — amount and timing reliably known from the meter reading | Yes | Dr Utilities expense / Cr Accrued expenses, $8,400 |
| 2 | Provision — timing and amount both genuinely uncertain, governed by IAS 37 | Yes, once measured | Warranty provision (sections 1.4.3–1.4.4) |
| 3 | Trade payable — invoice already received and agreed | Yes | Dr Materials inventory or cost / Cr Trade payables, $54,000 |
| 4 | Contingent liability — outflow assessed as *possible*, not *probable*; recognition test fails | No — disclosure only | Note disclosure of a possible $250,000 exposure |
| 5 | Not a contingent asset — recovery is virtually certain, so it ceases to be "contingent" and is recognised as a receivable | Yes | Dr Insurance receivable / Cr Other income, $75,000 |

*Answer.* Recognised on the Statement of Financial Position at 31 December 2026 (excluding the warranty,
measured in section 1.4.3): accrued expense $8,400 + trade payable $54,000 = $62,400 in liabilities; insurance
receivable $75,000 in assets. The $250,000 lawsuit is disclosed in the notes only — no liability is booked.

*Interpretation.* Items 1 and 3 never reach IAS 37's test at all — they are ordinary liabilities with
reliable amounts. Item 4 shows the test *failing* on the "probable" criterion (section 1.4.2). Item 5 shows the
mirror-image rule for assets: IAS 37 does not let an entity recognise a contingent asset merely because
recovery is probable, but once recovery becomes *virtually certain* the asset is, by definition, no longer
contingent and is recognised in full.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Over-providing "to be safe" for an item that does not clear the probable-outflow
test — this manufactures a hidden reserve and breaches IAS 37 as much as under-providing does; provisions
must reflect the *current best estimate*, not the most cautious one. (2) Confusing a provision with a
project **management reserve or contingency** (KA 3.1) — a management reserve is a *budgeting* tool held
against unidentified future scope or risk and is not a recognised liability at all; a provision is a
*recognised, measured* obligation for a specific, identified past event. (3) Applying probability-weighting
machinery to a routine, near-certain accrual (item 1) — proportionate effort matters; not every liability
needs the full IAS 37 apparatus.

**AI in this topic.** Document-intelligence tools can scan invoices, meter data, contracts and legal
correspondence at month-end and pre-classify items into "accrual," "payable," "possible provision" or
"possible contingent liability" buckets, materially speeding up the close. The governance limit is squarely
on the *judgement* calls: whether a pattern of past practice (not a signed clause) creates a constructive
obligation, and where exactly "possible" tips into "probable," are professional — often legal — judgements
that a classification model can flag as worth reviewing but should not be trusted to resolve unsupervised,
particularly near the recognition threshold where the accounting outcome (recognise vs disclose vs ignore)
changes materially.

### 1.4.2 Recognition criteria for a provision under IAS 37

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 37 recognises a provision only when **all three** of the following hold:

1. The entity has a **present obligation** — legal or constructive — **as a result of a past event**.
2. It is **probable** that settling the obligation will require an **outflow of economic resources**.
3. A **reliable estimate** can be made of the amount of the obligation.

A **legal obligation** arises from a contract, legislation or other operation of law. A **constructive
obligation** arises where an entity's own past conduct — a published policy, or an established pattern of
practice — has created a valid expectation in others that it will discharge certain responsibilities, even
without a strict legal duty to do so (for example, a contractor who has always made good minor defects on
past projects, without being contractually bound to, may have created a constructive obligation to do so
again). IAS 37 explicitly does *not* permit a provision for a general risk of future operating losses,
because a future loss has no obligating *past event* — it fails criterion 1 outright, however probable it
seems.

**The underlying principle — a decision test, not a formula.** Each of the three criteria is a gate: fail
any one, and no provision is recognised. Where criterion 2 fails because the outflow is *possible* but not
*probable*, the item becomes a **contingent liability**, disclosed in the notes (unless the possibility of
outflow is remote, in which case even disclosure is unnecessary). The mirror-image item for a possible
economic benefit is a **contingent asset**: never recognised, disclosed only where an inflow is probable,
and — as Example 1.4.1 showed — recognised in full only once inflow is virtually certain, at which point it
is, by definition, no longer "contingent."

**Formula.** Expressed as a logical AND:

`Recognise a provision ⇔ (present obligation, legal or constructive) AND (probable outflow) AND (reliable estimate possible)`

`Fails only criterion 2 (possible, not probable) ⇒ contingent liability (disclose, unless remote)`
`Fails criterion 1 (no obligating past event) ⇒ no provision and, ordinarily, no disclosure`

**Example 1.4.2 — applying the three-part test to three items.**

*Scenario.* Continuing Meridian's 31 December 2026 review:

| Item | Present obligation? | Probable outflow? | Reliable estimate? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Contractual workmanship warranty on the signalling system, in the signed contract | Yes — legal, from the signed contract | Yes — historical defect data shows claims are routine | Yes — engineering can model a probability-weighted range (section 1.4.3) |
| B. Former subcontractor's breach-of-contract claim (Example 1.4.1, item 4) | Contested — Meridian denies any obligation | No — lawyers assess the chance of losing as possible, not probable | Not reliably, pending legal process |
| C. A general expectation that site remediation costs may rise across the industry next year | No — no specific past event obliges Meridian | N/A | N/A |

*Required.* State the accounting outcome for each.

*Working/Answer.*

| Item | Outcome |
|---|---|
| A | Recognise a provision — all three criteria met |
| B | Contingent liability — disclose the $250,000 exposure in the notes; do not recognise |
| C | No provision and no disclosure — fails at the first gate; there is no obligating past event, only a general future expectation |

*Interpretation.* Item C is the pitfall IAS 37 is most often invoked to prevent: providing for a *general
risk of future cost increases* looks prudent but is not permitted, because prudence in IAS 37 operates
*within* the three-part test, not as a licence to override it.

**Example 1.4.2b — a constructive obligation without a signed clause.** Suppose Meridian's rail-signalling
contract is silent on defects after handover, but Meridian has, without exception, made good minor defects
free of charge on its previous four projects, and has told this client informally that it will do the same
here. Criterion 1 is still met — not through a legal clause, but through a **constructive obligation**
created by established practice and a valid expectation created in the client's mind. The remaining tests
(probable outflow, reliable estimate) are assessed exactly as for a legal obligation.

#### Fig 1.4.2 — The IAS 37 recognition decision tree

*Chart type:* a three-gate decision-tree/flowchart.
*Series shown:* Gate 1 "Present obligation (legal or constructive) from a past event?" → No: stop, no
provision, generally no disclosure. Yes → Gate 2 "Probable outflow of resources?" → No: contingent
liability, disclose unless remote. Yes → Gate 3 "Reliable estimate possible?" → No (rare): treat as a
contingent liability and disclose. Yes → **Recognise a provision**.
*Underlying data:* the three items A/B/C from Example 1.4.2, plotted as three paths through the tree.
*Render-ready description:* brand blue `#1D4ED8` flowchart, diamond decision nodes, rounded outcome boxes;
each of Meridian's three items shown as a labelled token travelling its own path to its outcome box.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Recognising a provision for a merely *possible* obligation (item B) — this
overstates liabilities and understates profit without basis; the correct treatment is disclosure only.
(2) Providing for general future operating losses or a general risk climate (item C) — always fails
criterion 1; IAS 37 requires a specific past event, not a trend. (3) Missing a constructive obligation
because there is no contract clause to point to (Example 1.4.2b) — the professional must look at conduct
and public statements, not only signed documents.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-analytics and legal-document-review tools can extract candidate obligating
clauses from long-form contracts and correspondence, and can surface historical litigation-outcome
statistics to help assess whether an outflow is "probable" versus "possible." This materially speeds up
evidence-gathering for item B-type assessments. It does not, and should not, replace the legal and
accounting judgement of where the probable/possible line falls in a specific dispute — case volumes are
typically small, precedent is fact-specific, and a model's probability estimate from a handful of
comparable cases can be unreliable; AI assembles the evidence base, the professional (with legal counsel)
makes the recognition call.

**Cross-references.** section 1.4.1 (the boundary this test formalises); section 1.4.3 (measuring an item that passes the
test); KA 7.2 (contract obligations, claims and disputes in the commercial-management context); KA 12.2
(risk identification and the risk register, which often first flags an item that later becomes a
provision).

### 1.4.3 Measuring a provision — best estimate, expected value and discounting

**Definition and purpose.** Once an item clears the recognition test, IAS 37 requires it to be measured at
the **best estimate** of the expenditure required to settle the obligation at the reporting date. Where the
obligation involves a **large population of similar items** (a warranty across many units sold, for
example), the best estimate is the **expected value**: every possible outcome weighted by its probability.
Where a single obligation has a continuous range of possible outcomes with no single most-likely figure
standing out, the **midpoint of the range** is typically used instead. Where the effect of the time value of
money is **material** — typically for provisions expected to settle more than approximately a year from the
reporting date — the estimate is **discounted** to present value.

**The underlying principle.** Expected value converts a spread of uncertain outcomes into a single number
suitable for the accounts, without pretending that uncertainty has disappeared — the figure booked is a
probability-weighted average, not a prediction of what will actually happen. Discounting then recognises
that a dollar payable in two years is a smaller present obligation than a dollar payable today, because
money has a time value; **not discounting** a genuinely long-dated provision would overstate the liability.

**Formula.**

`Expected value (undiscounted best estimate) = Σ (probability_i × outcome_i), for all possible outcomes i`

`Present value, PV = FV / (1 + r)^n`

Where: **FV** = the undiscounted expected value at the future settlement date; **r** = the discount rate per
period (a rate reflecting the time value of money and risks specific to the liability, stated as an
illustrative assumption in every example — this is a judgement input, never a market-quoted "risk-free"
constant); **n** = the number of periods to expected settlement.

**Example 1.4.3 — expected value and discounting of Meridian's workmanship warranty provision.**

*Scenario.* On 1 April 2028, Meridian hands over the completed signalling installation, triggering a
two-year contractual workmanship warranty to 31 March 2030 (Example 1.4.2, item A). Engineering and
commercial teams assess three possible outcomes for total warranty costs over the two-year term, drawing on
Meridian's defect history on comparable projects:

| Outcome | Probability | Estimated cost (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| No material defects | 60% | 0 |
| Minor defects, isolated repairs | 30% | 150,000 |
| Major defect requiring batch replacement | 10% | 600,000 |

For discounting, claims are, on average, expected around the end of the two-year term (a simplifying
teaching assumption, flagged for SME review), and Meridian applies an illustrative discount rate of 5% per
annum reflecting the time value of money and the risk profile of this liability.

*Required.* (a) The undiscounted expected value; (b) the present value at initial recognition.

*Working.*

(a) EV = (0.60 × 0) + (0.30 × 150,000) + (0.10 × 600,000) = 0 + 45,000 + 60,000 = **105,000**

(b) PV = 105,000 / (1.05)² = 105,000 / 1.1025 = **95,238** (nearest USD)

*Answer.* Undiscounted best estimate **$105,000**; present value at initial recognition **$95,238**.

*Interpretation.* The $9,762 gap between the two figures ($105,000 − $95,238) is not a reduction in what
Meridian ultimately expects to pay — it is the time-value-of-money adjustment, which reverses itself back
into the carrying amount over the two years as the "unwinding of the discount" (section 1.4.4), a financing cost,
not a change in the underlying claims estimate.

**Example 1.4.3b — using the midpoint instead of expected value.** If, instead of three discrete scenarios,
Meridian's engineers could only say that total warranty cost would fall "somewhere between $60,000 and
$140,000, with no point in the range more likely than any other," IAS 37's midpoint convention would apply:
best estimate = (60,000 + 140,000) / 2 = **$100,000**. The two methods — expected value for a modelled
population of discrete outcomes, midpoint for a genuinely uniform continuous range — are not
interchangeable; the choice depends on the shape of the information actually available, not on which gives
a more convenient number.

#### Fig 1.4.3 — Expected value and the effect of discounting

*Chart type:* two linked charts — (1) a stacked probability-weighted bar chart showing the three outcome
contributions (0, 45,000, 60,000) building to the $105,000 total; (2) a two-bar comparison, "Undiscounted
FV $105,000" vs "Discounted PV $95,238."
*Underlying data:* the outcome table above, plus the FV/PV pair.
*Render-ready description:* brand blue `#1D4ED8` stacked bar for chart 1 with each segment labelled by its
probability and dollar contribution; chart 2 a simple two-bar comparison with a bracket and arrow showing
the ~9% reduction from discounting.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Using only the single most-likely outcome (here, "no material defects," $0) rather
than the full probability-weighted expected value across a population of similar risks — this
systematically understates provisions for anything with a "low-probability, high-impact" tail like item
three above. (2) Skipping discounting for a genuinely long-dated provision on materiality grounds without
actually testing materiality — a rule of thumb, not a licence to ignore the test. (3) **Double-counting
risk** — adjusting the cash-flow estimate upward for risk *and* using a risk-adjusted (rather than
risk-free-plus-liability-specific) discount rate; risk should be reflected once, not twice.

**AI in this topic.** Machine-learning models trained on historical warranty-claim and defect-rate data can
generate a full probability distribution of outcomes — sharper and more granular than three hand-picked
scenarios — feeding directly into the expected-value calculation. The governance limit is a direct
application of "garbage in, garbage out" (KA 13.2): the model is only as good as the historical data's
relevance to the *current* installation, materials and workmanship; a genuinely new technology or a
first-of-type installation has no comparable claims history for the model to learn from, and extrapolating
confidently from thin or irrelevant data can produce a precise-looking but poorly grounded estimate. The
professional should treat a model-generated distribution as a stronger starting point than three
hand-picked scenarios, not as a substitute for engineering sign-off on its inputs.

**Cross-references.** section 1.4.2 (the recognition test this measurement follows); section 1.4.4 (journalising this
example); section 1.4.6 (reviewing and updating the estimate each period); KA 6.3 (discounting is not used in the
EAC family, but the present-value logic recurs in KA 3.5 cash-flow forecasting).

### 1.4.4 Worked example: recognising and unwinding a warranty provision

**Definition and purpose.** This topic completes Example 1.4.3 with the full double-entry treatment: initial
recognition of a discounted provision, and the **unwinding of the discount** in each subsequent period —
the periodic increase in the provision's carrying amount that reflects the passage of time, recorded as a
finance cost rather than as an operating or project cost.

**The underlying principle.** A discounted provision's carrying amount must grow, period by period, from
its initial present value back up to the full undiscounted amount by the expected settlement date — this
growth is not a re-estimate of the claims themselves (that is a separate step, covered in section 1.4.6); it is
purely the mechanical reversal of the discounting applied at initial recognition. The unwind is charged to
profit or loss as a finance cost precisely because it is compensation for the passage of time, not for
project execution.

**Formula.** `Unwind of discount (period t) = Opening carrying amount × r`, added to the provision each
period, where `r` is the same discount rate used at initial recognition (reassessed each period per
section 1.4.6).

**Example 1.4.4 — journal entries for initial recognition and the discount unwind.**

*Scenario.* Continuing Example 1.4.3: initial recognition of the warranty provision on 1 April 2028 at its
present value of $95,238; Meridian's financial year ends 31 March.

*Required.* Journalise (i) initial recognition; (ii) the discount unwind for the year to 31 March 2029;
(iii) the discount unwind for the year to 31 March 2030 (warranty expiry).

*Working.*

| Date | Entry | Debit (USD) | Credit (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Apr 2028 | Dr Warranty expense (P&L) / Cr Warranty provision | 95,238 | 95,238 |
| 31 Mar 2029 | Dr Finance cost — unwind of discount / Cr Warranty provision | 4,762 | 4,762 |
| 31 Mar 2030 | Dr Finance cost — unwind of discount / Cr Warranty provision | 5,000 | 5,000 |

Carrying amount roll-forward: 0 → **95,238** (initial recognition) → **100,000** (95,238 + 4,762, after
year 1 unwind) → **105,000** (100,000 + 5,000, after year 2 unwind).

*Answer.* Provision carrying amount at 31 March 2030 (warranty expiry) = **$105,000**, exactly matching the
undiscounted expected value computed in Example 1.4.3 — as it must, since all the discount has now
unwound.

*Interpretation.* The $4,762 and $5,000 unwind charges are finance costs, not project operating costs, and
must **not** be charged to the project's direct cost report used for cost-performance measurement (KA
6.1–6.4) — including them there would distort CPI with a cost that has nothing to do with project execution
efficiency. For information only: if this provision were reported to a Saudi joint-venture partner in SAR,
at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1, the initial $95,238 recognition would be presented as SAR 357,143
(95,238 × 3.75) — the functional-currency books remain in USD; this is a translation for information, not a
live FX rate.

**Example 1.4.4b — balance-sheet classification.** At 1 April 2028, given the simplifying assumption that
claims are expected around the two-year mark, the full $95,238 is classified as **non-current** (settlement
expected beyond 12 months). By 31 March 2029, with only one year of the warranty remaining, the $100,000
carrying amount is reclassified in full to **current** — the current/non-current split is reassessed at
every reporting date as the expected settlement date approaches, not fixed once at initial recognition.

- *Frame 2:* the clock hand advances one full turn (one year); the bar grows smoothly to $100,000.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Charging the discount unwind to project operating cost, inflating apparent cost
overrun on the EVM/cost report (Interpretation, above). (2) Leaving the current/non-current split unchanged
across reporting dates instead of reassessing it each period (Example 1.4.4b). (3) Forgetting to reassess
the discount rate itself at each reporting date — IAS 37 requires the rate used to reflect *current* market
assessments, not the rate locked in at initial recognition, though in practice the effect on a short-dated
provision like this one is usually immaterial.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted period-close tools can auto-generate the discount-unwind journal from a
provision register and flag the current/non-current reclassification automatically as settlement dates
approach — a genuine efficiency gain in an environment with many provisions running on different clocks.
The discount-rate judgement itself, and confirmation that the unwind has been correctly excluded from
project cost-performance reporting, remain professional review points: an automation error here is easy to
miss because the entry balances and looks routine (the same class of risk flagged for journal automation in
KA 1.1 section 1.1.3).

### 1.4.5 Onerous contracts and contract-loss provisions in project controls

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 37 defines an **onerous contract** as one in which the **unavoidable costs**
of meeting the contractual obligations **exceed the economic benefits expected to be received under it**.
For a project controls professional this is the single most consequential application of provisioning in
the whole chapter: it is the accounting recognition event triggered when a forecast cost overrun, tracked
through the cost report and the EAC (KA 3.4, KA 6.3), turns a fixed-price contract from profitable to
loss-making. Where a contract becomes onerous, the present obligation for the *unavoidable future loss* is
recognised as a provision as soon as the loss is foreseeable — not deferred until the loss is actually
incurred.

**The underlying principle.** Under the percentage-of-completion (input) method used for revenue
recognition on a long-term contract (IFRS 15, KA 2.2), revenue and cost are already recognised proportionately
as work progresses (`% complete = costs incurred to date / total estimated costs`, per the spine's canonical
POC formula). If the contract's total forecast margin is negative, this mechanism *already* reflects a
proportionate share of the loss on the work done **to date** — revenue recognised at the percentage
complete will be less than the cost incurred, because both are measured against a total that no longer
supports a profit. What the normal POC mechanism does **not** capture is the loss still to come on the
**remaining** work. That remaining loss is what the onerous-contract provision is for. Recognising the
*entire* forecast loss as a fresh provision on top of the loss already reflected through POC would
double-count it — a common and important error to avoid.

**Formula.**

`Total forecast contract margin = Total contract revenue − Forecast cost at completion (EAC)`

`Margin recognised to date (via POC) = Revenue recognised to date − Costs incurred to date`
`   where Revenue recognised to date = % complete × Total contract revenue`

`Onerous contract provision required = MAX(0, − [Total forecast contract margin − Margin recognised to date])`
`   i.e. the unavoidable loss still to come on the REMAINING work only`

**Example 1.4.5 — Meridian's rail-signalling contract turns onerous at month 18.**

*Scenario.* At 30 September 2027 (month 18 of the 24-month contract), Meridian's cost-to-complete review
reforecasts total contract cost upward:

| Item | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|
| Total contract revenue (fixed price) | 10,000,000 |
| Original budgeted cost | 8,500,000 |
| Forecast cost at completion, EAC (reforecast) | 10,800,000 |
| Costs incurred to date | 8,100,000 |

*Required.* Determine whether the contract is onerous and, if so, the provision required at 30 September
2027.

*Working.*

Total forecast contract margin = 10,000,000 − 10,800,000 = **(800,000)** — a total forecast *loss* of
$800,000; the contract is onerous.

% complete (cost-to-cost input method) = 8,100,000 / 10,800,000 = **75.0%**

Revenue recognised to date = 75.0% × 10,000,000 = **7,500,000**

Margin recognised to date = 7,500,000 − 8,100,000 = **(600,000)** — already reflected through the normal
POC revenue/cost entries for the first 18 months.

Remaining revenue = 10,000,000 − 7,500,000 = **2,500,000**
Remaining forecast cost = 10,800,000 − 8,100,000 = **2,700,000**
Remaining margin = 2,500,000 − 2,700,000 = **(200,000)**

Check: total margin = margin to date + remaining margin = (600,000) + (200,000) = **(800,000)** ✓ — agrees
with the total forecast contract margin above.

*Answer.* An **onerous contract provision of $200,000** is required at 30 September 2027 — the unavoidable
loss still to come on the remaining six months of work. The $600,000 loss on work already performed needs
no separate provision; it is already captured through the ordinary POC revenue and cost entries for the
period.

*Interpretation.* The natural instinct is to provide for the whole $800,000 forecast loss the moment it is
identified. That double-counts the $600,000 already recognised through normal contract accounting. The
provision exists specifically to bring forward recognition of the loss on work **not yet done**, so that the
full $800,000 loss is reflected across the contract's life at the point it first becomes foreseeable, rather
than trickling out at a further loss-making margin as the remaining work is executed.

**Example 1.4.5b — a contrast: cost growth without an onerous contract.** Suppose the same reforecast had
instead produced EAC = $9,800,000 (cost growth of $1,300,000 above the original $8,500,000 budget, but
still below the $10,000,000 contract revenue). Total forecast margin = 10,000,000 − 9,800,000 = **+200,000**
— still a profit, just a much smaller one than originally planned. **No onerous contract provision is
required.** This is a critical distinction for the professional: significant cost overrun and profit erosion
are urgent cost-control and reporting matters (KA 4.2, KA 6.3) long before they become an IAS 37 recognition
event — the two are related but are not the same trigger.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Recognising the *entire* forecast loss ($800,000) as a new provision, double-
counting the loss already reflected through POC (the single most common error in this topic). (2) Waiting
until costs have actually overtaken the budget before acting, rather than acting on the **forward-looking
EAC** — IAS 37 requires provisioning as soon as the loss is *foreseeable*, which is a forecasting judgement,
not a backward-looking actual-cost trigger. (3) Treating the provision as a cost-control action in itself —
it is not; it is the accounting *recognition* of an already-adverse forecast, and should trigger urgent
commercial and operational response (claims, scope recovery, cost mitigation — KA 5.4, KA 7.2) alongside the
bookkeeping entry, not instead of it.

**AI in this topic.** Predictive analytics scanning cost-report trends and EAC movements over time can raise
an early-warning flag when a contract's trajectory is heading toward an onerous position, often well before
a formal month-end reforecast would surface it (this is developed further as predictive EAC and
early-warning modelling in KA 6.3–6.4 and KA 13.5). Given the direct financial-statement consequence of a
false positive or false negative here — booking or missing an $800,000-class loss — this is a high-scrutiny
use case: any AI-flagged early warning should be treated as a prompt for a professional cost-to-complete
review, never as a basis for booking or not booking a provision on its own.

### 1.4.6 Subsequent measurement: review, utilisation, reversal and the cost report

**Definition and purpose.** IAS 37 requires every provision to be **reviewed at each reporting date** and
adjusted to reflect the current best estimate. Two distinct events follow from that review, and they are
**not** the same thing: **utilisation** is the use of the provision to absorb actual expenditure that has
now been incurred against it; **reversal** is a reduction of the provision because the current best
estimate has genuinely fallen, crediting the amount no longer required back to profit or loss.

**The underlying principle.** Utilisation does **not** pass through profit or loss a second time — the
expense was already recognised when the provision was first raised (sections 1.4.3–1.4.4); paying the claim simply
converts a liability into a cash outflow, exactly as settling any other liability does. Reversal **does**
pass through profit or loss, because it corrects an estimate that is now known to have been too high — IAS
37 does not allow a deliberately conservative provision to sit unreviewed on the balance sheet; it must
track the entity's *current* best estimate, which can move down as well as up.

**Formula.** No new formula; the roll-forward identity that must hold each period is:

`Closing provision = Opening provision + New/increased provisions − Utilisation (cash paid against the provision) − Reversals (excess no longer required, credited to P&L)`

**Example 1.4.6 — a short-term provision: utilisation and reversal on a defective relay batch.**

*Scenario.* On 30 June 2027, a quality inspection identifies a batch of defective signal relays already
installed on the Meridian contract. Engineers estimate total remedial cost at $180,000, expected to be
fully resolved within nine months (by the 31 March 2028 year-end) — short enough that discounting is not
applied on materiality grounds. Meridian's year-end is 31 March.

*Required.* Journalise (i) initial recognition; (ii) utilisation of $95,000 actual remedial spend by
31 December 2027; (iii) the 31 March 2028 year-end review, at which the remaining defects are confirmed
fully rectified at a final cost of only $60,000, and the final utilisation.

*Working.*

| Date | Event | Debit (USD) | Credit (USD) | Provision balance after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 Jun 2027 | (i) Dr Remedial works expense / Cr Provision | 180,000 | 180,000 | 180,000 |
| 31 Dec 2027 | (ii) Utilisation: Dr Provision / Cr Cash | 95,000 | 95,000 | 85,000 |
| 31 Mar 2028 | (iii)(a) Reversal of excess: Dr Provision / Cr Remedial works expense | 25,000 | 25,000 | 60,000 |
| 31 Mar 2028 | (iii)(b) Final utilisation: Dr Provision / Cr Cash | 60,000 | 60,000 | 0 |

*Answer.* The provision closes at **$0**. Total cash paid = 95,000 + 60,000 = **$155,000**; total reversed
to profit or loss = **$25,000**. Check: 155,000 + 25,000 = **180,000**, exactly the original provision — the
roll-forward reconciles fully.

*Interpretation.* The $95,000 and $60,000 utilisations never touch the Statement of Profit or Loss a second
time — they are cash settlements of a liability already expensed. The $25,000 reversal **does** touch profit
or loss, as a credit, in the period the improved estimate is confirmed — it is not "found money," it is the
correction of an estimate that IAS 37 required to be reviewed, and the credit belongs in the same expense
line the original charge used, so the net effect over the life of the provision is transparent: $180,000
charged, $25,000 reversed, $155,000 net cost — matching the cash actually paid.

**Example 1.4.6b — linking the provision register to the cost report.** A project cost report built from
the general ledger (KA 1.5 chart of accounts; KA 5.2 cost control cycle) should show the $180,000 remedial
provision as a distinct line from ordinary actual cost, precisely because it moves for reasons (estimate
review, reversal) that have nothing to do with physical progress on site. A cost report that blends
provision movements into "actual cost incurred" without separate visibility would show a $25,000 favourable
cost movement in March 2028 that a reader might mistake for productivity improvement, when it is in fact an
accounting estimate correction — the two must be labelled and explained separately in management reporting
(KA 4.3).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Routing a utilisation payment through the Statement of Profit or Loss again —
double-expensing an amount already charged when the provision was first raised. (2) Failing to reverse an
excess provision, leaving a stale, over-cautious balance on the books — this understates profit in the
period the improved information becomes available and is not a permitted "smoothing" device under IAS 37.
(3) Leaving a provision unreviewed for several periods because "nothing has happened yet" — the requirement
to reassess at *each* reporting date applies even where no cash has moved.

**AI in this topic.** Anomaly-detection and variance-analytics tools can compare actual utilisation against
original provision assumptions across a portfolio of provisions and flag patterns — for example, a
particular estimator or project consistently over- or under-providing — feeding back into better estimation
practice over time (a bias-detection use case, related to the anomaly detection discussed for cost postings
in KA 1.1 section 1.1.4 and cost extraction in KA 5.4). This is genuinely useful for *portfolio-level* learning; it
needs meaningful data volume to be reliable, and it cannot substitute for the specific engineering or legal
judgement needed to assess any single claim, which is why the professional review step in Example 1.4.6
remains essential regardless of how good the pattern-detection becomes.

### Key terms

- **Provision** — a liability of uncertain timing or amount, recognised under IAS 37's three-part test.
- **Accrued expense** — a liability for goods/services received but not yet invoiced, with reliably known
  amount and timing.
- **Trade payable** — a liability for an invoice already received and agreed.
- **Legal obligation / constructive obligation** — an obligation arising from a contract or law, or from an
  entity's own past conduct creating a valid expectation in others.
- **Contingent liability** — a possible obligation, or a present obligation not recognised because outflow
  is not probable or cannot be reliably estimated; disclosed unless remote.
- **Contingent asset** — a possible asset from a past event; disclosed if inflow is probable, recognised in
  full (and ceases to be "contingent") only when inflow is virtually certain.
- **Best estimate** — the amount an entity would rationally pay to settle or transfer the obligation at the
  reporting date.
- **Expected value method** — probability-weighted average of possible outcomes, used for a population of
  similar items.
- **Discounting / present value** — adjusting a future cash outflow to reflect the time value of money,
  where the effect is material.
- **Unwinding of the discount** — the periodic increase in a discounted provision's carrying amount,
  recognised as a finance cost.
- **Onerous contract** — a contract where unavoidable costs of meeting its obligations exceed the economic
  benefits expected under it.
- **Onerous contract provision** — the provision for the unavoidable loss on the *remaining* (not yet
  performed) work under an onerous contract.
- **Utilisation** — settling actual expenditure against a provision already recognised; no further P&L
  charge.
- **Reversal** — reducing a provision because the current best estimate has fallen; credited to profit or
  loss.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 1.4.1 [level: recall]**
Which of the following best distinguishes a provision from an ordinary accrued expense under IAS 37?
A. Provisions relate only to liabilities owed to employees
B. A provision is a liability of uncertain timing or amount; an accrual's amount and timing are reliably
   known from a source document ✔
C. Accruals are always classified as non-current; provisions are always current
D. Provisions do not appear on the Statement of Financial Position, but accruals do

*Rationale:* B states IAS 37's own defining distinction (section 1.4.1). A wrongly restricts provisions to
employee liabilities; C invents a classification rule that does not exist (either can be current or
non-current); D is wrong — both are recognised liabilities on the Statement of Financial Position.

**MCQ 1.4.2 [level: application]**
A former subcontractor sues Meridian for $250,000 for alleged breach of contract; legal counsel assesses
the likelihood of Meridian losing as "possible but not probable." What is the correct treatment?
A. Recognise a provision of $250,000
B. Recognise a provision equal to a probability-weighted expected value of the claim
C. Disclose the exposure as a contingent liability; recognise no provision ✔
D. No disclosure is required because the outcome is uncertain

*Rationale:* The claim fails the second recognition gate (probable outflow) — "possible" sits below the
"probable" threshold, so IAS 37 requires disclosure, not recognition (section 1.4.2, Example 1.4.2). A and B both
wrongly recognise a liability that has not cleared the test; D is wrong because "possible" (not remote)
still requires disclosure.

**MCQ 1.4.3 [level: application — numerical]**
A warranty provision has three possible outcomes: no claims (55% probability, $0 cost), minor claims (35%,
$80,000), major claims (10%, $300,000). What is the undiscounted expected value of the provision?
A. $80,000
B. $58,000 ✔
C. $110,000
D. $300,000

*Rationale:* EV = (0.55 × 0) + (0.35 × 80,000) + (0.10 × 300,000) = 0 + 28,000 + 30,000 = $58,000 (section 1.4.3
method). A picks only the minor-claims figure; C and D do not correspond to any correct weighting.

**MCQ 1.4.4 [level: analysis — numerical]**
A fixed-price contract has total revenue of $6,000,000. At the latest reforecast, EAC (forecast cost at
completion) is $6,600,000; costs incurred to date are $4,950,000 and the input-method percentage complete
is 75.0%. What onerous-contract provision, if any, should be recognised for the loss on the *remaining*
work?
A. $600,000
B. $450,000
C. $150,000 ✔
D. $0 — losses are only recognised when actually incurred

*Rationale:* Total forecast loss = 6,000,000 − 6,600,000 = (600,000). Revenue to date = 75% × 6,000,000 =
4,500,000; margin to date = 4,500,000 − 4,950,000 = (450,000), already reflected through POC. Remaining
margin = (600,000) − (450,000) = (150,000) — this is the only amount requiring a fresh provision (section 1.4.5).
A double-counts the loss already recognised; B is only the loss to date, not the remaining loss; D
contradicts IAS 37's requirement to provide for a foreseeable loss as soon as it is foreseeable, not when
incurred.

**MCQ 1.4.5 [level: analysis]**
Which statement about discounting provisions under IAS 37 is correct?
A. All provisions must be discounted to present value, regardless of the time to settlement
B. Provisions are discounted only where the effect of the time value of money is material, typically for
   longer-dated obligations ✔
C. The periodic unwinding of the discount is presented as an increase to project operating cost
D. Discounting is prohibited under IAS 37 so that provisions remain comparable across entities

*Rationale:* B is the correct materiality-based test (section 1.4.3). A overstates the requirement — short-dated
provisions such as Example 1.4.6 are not discounted; C is wrong, the unwind is a finance cost, not an
operating/project cost (section 1.4.4); D is factually incorrect — discounting is required, not prohibited, where
material.

### Self-check questions

1. Using Example 1.4.1, explain why the December electricity accrual and the supplier's materials invoice
   are not provisions, even though both are liabilities of Meridian at the month-end.
2. A reforecast shows a fixed-price contract's EAC now exceeds total contract revenue. Explain, with
   reference to the percentage-of-completion method, why only part of the total forecast loss requires a
   newly recognised onerous-contract provision.
3. Using the relay-defect mini-case (Example 1.4.6), explain the accounting difference between
   "utilising" a provision and "reversing" a provision, and state which one affects the Statement of Profit
   or Loss.
4. Why does IAS 37 require provisions to be reviewed at every reporting date, and what does the "unwinding
   of the discount" represent economically, as distinct from a change in the underlying claims estimate?

**Answers**

1. Both items' amount and timing are reliably known from a source document (a meter reading; an agreed
   supplier invoice) — neither carries the genuine uncertainty in amount or timing that defines a
   provision under IAS 37. They are recognised as an accrued expense and a trade payable respectively,
   without needing to pass the three-part provision recognition test (section 1.4.1).
2. Under the cost-to-cost input method, revenue and cost are already recognised proportionately as work
   progresses; if the contract's total forecast margin is negative, this mechanism already reflects a
   proportionate loss on the work done to date. The onerous-contract provision exists only for the
   unavoidable loss still to come on the *remaining* work — recognising the full forecast loss again would
   double-count the loss already reflected through the normal POC entries (Example 1.4.5: total loss
   $800,000 = $600,000 already recognised through POC + $200,000 newly provided).
3. Utilisation is the cash settlement of expenditure against a provision already expensed — it does not
   pass through the Statement of Profit or Loss again ($95,000 and $60,000 in Example 1.4.6). Reversal is
   a reduction of the provision because the current best estimate has fallen, and it is credited to the
   Statement of Profit or Loss in the period the improved estimate is confirmed ($25,000 in Example 1.4.6).
4. IAS 37 requires review at each reporting date because a provision must reflect the entity's *current*
   best estimate, which can rise or fall as more information becomes available — an unreviewed, stale
   provision would misstate the liability. The unwinding of the discount is purely the mechanical reversal
   of the time-value-of-money adjustment applied at initial recognition — it represents the passage of time
   bringing the settlement date closer, not a re-estimate of how much will actually be claimed (Example
   1.4.4: the $4,762 and $5,000 unwind charges are finance costs, separate from any change in the
   underlying $105,000 expected claims total).
