# Domain 8 — Project Management Lifecycle

## KA 8.6 — Development approaches — predictive, iterative, incremental & adaptive

KA 8.1–8.5 walk a single project through the five process groups — Initiating, Planning, Executing,
Monitoring & Controlling, Closing — largely as a predictive, once-through sequence, because that is the
simplest way to teach what each process group does. This closing KA of Domain 8 makes explicit something
those chapters left implicit: the process groups themselves are **agnostic to development approach**. They
describe *what kind of work* a project must do (define scope, sequence it, execute it, control it, close it)
irrespective of *how the product is actually developed* — once, in a single pass, against a fully defined
scope, or repeatedly, in short cycles, against a scope that is deliberately left open to discovery. That
second question — the **development approach** — is what this KA addresses: the spectrum running from
predictive (waterfall) through iterative and incremental to adaptive (agile), the criteria that indicate which
point on that spectrum fits a given piece of work, how approaches combine in hybrid delivery, and — critically
for a project controls professional — how measurement and forecasting must change to match the approach
chosen. This KA introduces the spectrum and its project-controls implications only; the full mechanics of
Scrum, agile metrics and AgileEVM are Domain 9's job, and every topic below hands off to the relevant KA there
rather than pre-empting it. Continuity runs on two illustrations: **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**'s
**Pipeline Segment 4B** (KA 1.1, KA 6.1; BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**)
for the predictive end of the spectrum, and a smaller, Meridian-adjacent software scope — the **Field
Progress & Punch-list App**, commissioned by Meridian's PMO to help site engineers log progress digitally
across Pipeline Segment 4B and its sister packages — for the adaptive end, introduced in full in 8.6.4.

### 8.6.1 The development-approach spectrum: predictive, iterative, incremental and adaptive

**Definition & purpose.** A **development approach** — the term used by PMI's *PMBOK Guide*, 7th edition, its
companion *Process Groups: A Practice Guide*, and PMI/Agile Alliance's *Agile Practice Guide* — is the method
a project uses to create and evolve its product, service or result: specifically, how completely requirements
are defined before work begins, and how frequently a usable result is delivered to a customer or user. These
sources describe a continuum with four commonly named reference points, not four unrelated categories:

- **Predictive** (plan-driven; informally "waterfall") — scope, schedule and cost are defined, and so far as
  practicable fixed, early, with the process groups applied essentially once, in sequence, to a single final
  delivery.
- **Iterative** — the product is built through repeated passes, each pass **revisiting and refining the same
  piece of work** in light of feedback, rather than adding new scope.
- **Incremental** — the product is delivered through successive, **separately usable slices**, each adding
  new scope to what came before, without necessarily revisiting what was already delivered.
- **Adaptive** (agile) — combines iterative and incremental delivery with an empirical, feedback-driven
  planning cycle (short planning horizons, frequent re-planning), so that scope is deliberately left variable
  and is discovered progressively as work proceeds.

**The underlying principle.** Iterative and incremental are frequently, and wrongly, used as synonyms — the
distinction matters enough to the exam blueprint that it is worth stating precisely. Incremental delivery
answers "what new capability can we usefully hand over next?"; iterative delivery answers "how can we make
what we already delivered better, now that we have feedback on it?" Most agile frameworks, pre-eminently
Scrum (KA 9.2), are **both at once**: across sprints, the product accumulates new increments of functionality
(incremental); within and across those same sprints, earlier increments are revisited and refined in response
to Sprint Review feedback (iterative). This is why "agile" is not simply a fifth, separate point on the
spectrum — it is the region where iterative and incremental combine with adaptive, empirical planning.

**No numerical formula attaches to this topic.** The distinction is checked operationally rather than
computed: under incremental delivery, `Σ(increments delivered)` is a strictly growing scope baseline — each
pass adds new "done" capability; under iterative delivery, the scope baseline for the item in question stays
constant while its quality or fit to need improves pass over pass.

**Example 8.6.1 — Distinguishing iterative from incremental on the Field Progress & Punch-list App.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's three-person in-house development team builds the app in three two-week sprints.
Sprint 1 delivers basic photo-capture at a site location. Sprint 2 delivers punch-list item assignment (a new
capability). Sprint 3 delivers offline data sync for poor-connectivity sites (another new capability). In
parallel, based on site-engineer feedback after each sprint's demo, the photo-capture screen from Sprint 1 is
itself revised twice — once in Sprint 2 to add tagging by work package, and again in Sprint 3 to simplify the
tagging workflow after users found it slow.

*Required.* Classify each stream of work as iterative or incremental.

*Working.* Photo-capture → punch-list assignment → offline sync is three separate, independently usable
capabilities stacking on top of one another: `Σ(increments) = ` photo-capture, then `+` assignment, then `+`
sync — a growing scope baseline, i.e. **incremental**. The photo-capture screen's own two revisions did not
add new capability; they refined the same delivered slice against user feedback — the scope baseline for that
one screen was constant across the three passes, i.e. **iterative**.

*Answer.* The app's overall delivery pattern is **incremental** (new capability each sprint); the individual
screen's refinement pattern is **iterative** (same capability, repeatedly improved) — both are present
simultaneously, which is exactly what "Scrum is iterative and incremental" (KA 9.2) means in practice.

*Interpretation.* A project is rarely purely one or the other. What makes an approach genuinely *adaptive*
is not simply that it is iterative-and-incremental (a phased predictive rollout can also deliver in stages),
but that the plan for *which* increments come next, and how each is refined, is itself left open and
re-decided empirically at short intervals — covered next, in 8.6.2.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating "iterative" and "incremental" as interchangeable — they describe different mechanics (refine vs.
  add) and mixing them up is a common source of confused exam answers and confused project charters alike.
- Treating "Agile" as a single point on the spectrum rather than the region combining iterative, incremental
  and adaptive planning — this leads to the "agile-in-name-only" pitfall developed fully in 8.6.3.
- Assuming an entire organisation or industry sits at one point on the spectrum ("construction is always
  predictive," "software is always agile") rather than assessing each piece of work on its own merits — the
  Field App and Pipeline Segment 4B sit at opposite ends despite both belonging to the same rail-signalling
  programme.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based project-classification or advisory assistants can read a charter or work-
package description and suggest a starting point on this spectrum, flagging language that signals high
requirements certainty (detailed specifications, fixed engineering standards) versus emergent requirements
(user research still needed, "we'll know it when we see it" language) (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5).
Such a suggestion is a useful first draft, not a governance decision — organisational risk appetite,
contractual terms and safety/regulatory constraints are not fully visible in a charter's text, and the
accountable choice remains the professional's, applying the fuller filter in 8.6.2.

**Cross-references.** KA 8.1 (Initiating — where the approach question is first framed); KA 8.2 (Planning —

framework — the iterative-and-incremental mechanics illustrated here, in full).

### 8.6.2 When each approach fits: requirements certainty, delivery cadence, risk and the suitability filter

**Definition & purpose.** Selecting a point on the spectrum is not a matter of preference or industry habit —
it is a structured judgement about whether a piece of work's requirements and solution are genuinely
*knowable in advance* ("plannable") or can only be *discovered through delivery* ("emergent"). This topic sets
out the criteria that judgement rests on; 8.6.5 turns them into a scored filter applied to two real,
Meridian-adjacent scopes.

**The underlying principle: the cost-of-change curve.** Traditional project management practice observes
that, for physically executed, engineering-heavy work, the cost of changing a decision rises steeply the
later it is made — a routing change on paper costs a redraw; the same change after a trench is excavated and
backfilled costs re-excavation, re-testing and schedule impact, and may sit on the critical path (KA 10.2).
For digital/software work, the equivalent cost-of-change curve is comparatively flat — a screen layout can be
changed in the next sprint at little more than the cost of the sprint itself. This difference is *why*
adaptive approaches, which depend on the cost of a late change being tolerably low, emerged first and fit best
in software/product contexts, while predictive approaches remain dominant where change is expensive and hard
to reverse. It is a strong tendency, not an absolute rule: safety-critical firmware can be run predictively;
early-stage design coordination (e.g. iterative BIM clash-detection cycles) can borrow iterative techniques
inside an overall predictive programme (8.6.3).

**Selection-criteria table.**

| Factor | Favours predictive | Favours adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| Requirements/solution certainty at the outset | High — fully specifiable in advance | Low — clarified only through use/feedback |
| Rate of change expected during delivery | Low | High |
| Practicality/value of early partial delivery | Low or none (work is physically indivisible) | High (usable slices are possible and valuable) |
| Cost and reversibility of a late change | High cost, hard to reverse | Low cost, easy to reverse |
| Regulatory/contractual/safety fixity | High (fixed design approval, formal safety case) | Low to moderate |
| Stakeholder/end-user availability for frequent feedback | Low (formal, gated reviews only) | High (embedded or readily accessible) |

**Mini-case — reading the criteria against Meridian's programme, at a glance.** Meridian Rail Contracting
LLC's 24-month, $10,000,000 rail-signalling contract (KA 10.1) contains both kinds of work side by side. The
physical trenching, pipe-laying and welding under Pipeline Segment 4B reads "High/Low/Low/High/High/Low"
against the six factors above — every column points toward predictive. The Field Progress & Punch-list App
reads the opposite pattern on every factor — "Low/High/High/Low/Low/High" — every column points toward
adaptive. 8.6.5 develops this qualitative read into a full, scored assessment.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Forcing a predictive/waterfall approach onto genuinely uncertain, emergent work** — fixing a detailed
  scope and schedule for a scope that cannot actually be fully specified up front (typically because the
  right solution depends on user feedback not yet gathered) produces a plan that looks precise and is
  systematically wrong; the professional then spends the project managing change requests instead of managing
  delivery.
- Reading the table as a single dominant factor rather than a balanced assessment — a project can have high
  requirements certainty but also a genuinely high cost of reversibility working against feasibility of
  frequent small releases, and vice versa; no one factor should decide alone (developed fully in 8.6.5).
- Assuming the criteria are fixed for the life of a project — a scope that starts emergent can mature into one
  that is well enough understood to lock down, and vice versa if a regulatory change reopens a "settled"
  design.

**AI in this topic.** An AI-based advisory tool, trained on an organisation's own historical project data, can
suggest how a new work package rates against each factor in the table and flag characteristics that match
past predictive or adaptive successes and failures (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). It cannot weigh an
organisation's specific risk appetite, contractual exposure or safety accountability — those remain judgements
only the accountable professional can make, informed by, not delegated to, an AI suggestion.

### 8.6.3 Hybrid approaches and tailoring the development approach

**Definition & purpose.** **Hybrid delivery** combines more than one development approach within a single
project or programme, applying each to the component of work for which it genuinely fits best, rather than
forcing one approach across a heterogeneous scope. This follows directly from the **tailoring** principle in
PMI's *PMBOK Guide*, 7th edition — that a project team adapts its approach, processes and level of formality
to the context of the specific project — applied here to the choice of development approach itself.

**The underlying principle.** The five process groups covered in KA 8.1–8.5 are not tied to a single
development approach; they recur at whatever cadence the chosen approach implies. A predictive work package
runs Planning once, produces a baseline, and moves through Executing and Monitoring & Controlling toward a
single Closing. An adaptive work package runs a compressed version of the same process groups inside every
sprint — a short planning event, execution, a review/control point, a form of closing (the Increment) — and
again at the release level. Hybrid delivery is what results when a programme deliberately runs both cadences
side by side, because different components of its scope sit at genuinely different points on the spectrum.

**Common hybrid patterns.**

| Pattern | Description | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Predictive spine, agile pockets | Overall programme governed by predictive stage gates; specific components (often digital/software) run short adaptive sprints inside a gate's span | Engineering/infrastructure programmes with an embedded software or controls-system deliverable |
| Agile-with-milestones | Delivery is predominantly adaptive, but wrapped in fixed external contractual or regulatory milestones | Product development under a funder's fixed reporting dates |
| Phase-based hybrid | One approach for early, exploratory phases (often iterative), a different one once the solution direction firms up | Early user-research/prototyping followed by a locked-down build phase, or the reverse |

**Mini-case — Meridian's programme as a predictive spine with an agile pocket.** Meridian's wider 24-month,
$10,000,000 rail-signalling contract is governed end to end by a predictive stage-gate structure — Design &
route engineering, Regulatory approval & way-leave, Fabrication & delivery, Trenching & earthworks, Pipe-
laying & welding, Testing & commissioning, Site reinstatement & handover (KA 10.1) — each gate reviewed and
signed off before the next begins, exactly the pattern Pipeline Segment 4B (and its EVM treatment, Domain 6)
assumes throughout. The Field Progress & Punch-list App sits *inside* that same programme as an agile pocket:
its three-person team runs two-week sprints for the duration of the Design-through-Commissioning span, and
reports a rolled-up status — not sprint-by-sprint detail — into Meridian's existing monthly programme report
(cross-reference KA 4.3), so the programme's overall stage-gate governance is undisturbed by the app team's
internal cadence.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **"Agile-in-name-only"** — holding daily stand-ups and calling two-week cycles "sprints" while imposing full
  predictive governance underneath (locked scope, no backlog re-prioritisation, a sign-off gate on every
  deliverable): the ceremonies survive but none of adaptive's actual behaviours do, and none of adaptive's
  actual benefits (early feedback, low cost of change) are realised either.
- Letting "hybrid" become an excuse for discipline in neither mode — a programme that is neither properly
  gated (predictive) nor genuinely responsive to feedback (adaptive) is not a hybrid, it is an ungoverned
  project wearing labels from both.
- Assuming hybrid always means an even split — the right proportion follows the suitability filter (8.6.2,
  8.6.5) applied component by component, not a fixed ratio applied uniformly.

**AI in this topic.** AI-based programme-reporting tools can automatically translate sprint-level adaptive
metrics (velocity, burnup — KA 9.3) into the format and cadence of a predictive stage-gate report, saving the
manual roll-up effort illustrated above (cross-reference KA 4.3, KA 13.5). They cannot decide *where* the
boundary between predictive spine and agile pocket should sit for a new programme — that architectural and
organisational-design judgement remains the professional's, informed by the 8.6.2 criteria.

### 8.6.4 Implications for project controls: measuring and forecasting across the spectrum

**Definition & purpose.** How a controls professional measures progress and forecasts an outcome must match
the logic of the development approach chosen — using the wrong measurement logic for the approach in use
produces control information that is precise-looking and wrong. A predictive approach fixes scope and asks
"what will this fixed scope now cost, and when will it finish?" — classical EVM and the EAC family (Domain 6)
answer exactly that question. An adaptive approach fixes time and cost (a sprint's length and a team's
run-rate are set in advance) and asks the *converse* question: "how much of the — deliberately variable —
scope will we get for that fixed time and cost?" — velocity and burnup-based forecasting (KA 9.3), and
AgileEVM's adaptation of EV/CPI/SPI/EAC to a variable-scope backlog (KA 9.5), answer that.

**The underlying principle: the iron triangle, inverted.** Classical EVM's foundational assumption (KA 6.1.1)
is that BAC — the total budgeted scope — is a fixed, known quantity that PV and EV are measured against; that
assumption is valid precisely because predictive requirements are fixed by design. Adaptive delivery
deliberately violates it: scope is meant to change as the backlog is learned from and re-prioritised. Rather
than abandoning measurement, adaptive delivery **inverts which corner of the traditional scope–time–cost "iron
triangle" is held fixed and which is the forecast output**: time and cost become the fixed inputs; scope
becomes the variable that is planned for, tracked and forecast (KA 9.3 develops this in full).

**Table 8.6.4 — How measurement and forecasting differ across the spectrum.**

| Dimension | Predictive approach | Adaptive approach |
|---|---|---|
| What is fixed vs. variable | Scope fixed (baselined BAC, WBS); time & cost are forecast, and can vary | Time & cost fixed (sprint length, team run-rate); scope is variable and is forecast |
| Primary progress unit | Currency-denominated Earned Value (EV), as a % of BAC | Story points/features completed (velocity) |
| Core question answered | "What will the fixed scope now cost/take?" | "How much scope fits within the fixed time and cost we have left?" |
| Core metrics | PV, EV, AC, CV, SV, CPI, SPI (KA 6.1–6.2) | Velocity, burnup/burndown, cycle time, throughput (KA 9.3) |
| Forecasting technique | The EAC family: `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)`; `EAC = BAC/CPI`; `EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)/(CPI × SPI)` (KA 6.3) | Velocity-based burnup projection; AgileEVM adapts EV/CPI/SPI/EAC to a variable-scope backlog (KA 9.5) |
| Response to a confirmed change | Formal change control; re-baseline PV (KA 5.4, KA 6.1.2) | Backlog re-prioritisation; no re-baseline needed, because scope was never fixed (KA 9.1, KA 9.3) |
| Typical contract basis | Lump-sum, remeasured/BoQ, EPC (KA 7.1) | Time & materials, capped T&M, target-cost with agile delivery (KA 7.1, KA 9.6) |

**Example 8.6.4 — The same reporting question, answered on two different measurement bases, Month/Sprint 3.**

*Scenario (predictive side — Pipeline Segment 4B, reusing KA 6.1's Month-3 data set).* BAC $600,000
(SAR 2,250,000). At the Month-3 data date: PV $270,000, EV $330,000, AC $280,000.

*Scenario (adaptive side — the Field Progress & Punch-list App).* A dedicated three-person team, funded at a
fixed cost of $15,000 (SAR 56,250) per two-week sprint regardless of how many points are delivered. The
backlog is estimated at 200 story points in total. Story points delivered: Sprint 1 = 16; Sprint 2 = 19;
Sprint 3 = 21.

*Required.* (a) The predictive-side CV, SV, CPI and SPI; (b) the adaptive-side cumulative points delivered,
average velocity, and a forecast of the number of further sprints (and total cost) needed to complete the
200-point backlog.

*Working.*
(a) `CV = EV − AC = 330,000 − 280,000 = 50,000` (favourable). `SV = EV − PV = 330,000 − 270,000 = 60,000`
(favourable). `CPI = EV/AC = 330,000/280,000 = 1.18`. `SPI = EV/PV = 330,000/270,000 = 1.22` (matching KA
6.2's established figures exactly).
(b) Cumulative points to Sprint 3 = `16 + 19 + 21 = 56`. Average velocity = `56/3 = 18.7 points/sprint`.
Remaining backlog = `200 − 56 = 144 points`. Further sprints needed = `144/18.7 = 7.7 → 8 sprints` (rounded
up — a fractional sprint cannot be delivered). Total sprints = `3 + 8 = 11`. Forecast total cost =
`11 × 15,000 = 165,000` (SAR 618,750).

*Answer.* Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 3: **CPI 1.18, SPI 1.22** — the fixed $600,000 scope is forecast to cost
*less* and finish *sooner* than planned (KA 6.3 develops the full EAC). Field App, Sprint 3: **56 of 200
points delivered, velocity 18.7/sprint** — the fixed time-and-cost basis instead forecasts **11 sprints and
$165,000 total** to deliver the full backlog.

*Interpretation.* Both calculations answer "how are we doing?" — but Pipeline Segment 4B's answer describes
what the *fixed scope* will now cost and take, while the Field App's answer describes how long the *fixed
funding* will take to deliver the *variable* scope. Equivalently, if Meridian instead fixed the App's budget
at $180,000 (12 sprints), the forecast becomes a scope statement: at 18.7 points/sprint, 12 sprints would
deliver roughly `56 + 9 × 18.7 ≈ 224 points` — comfortably covering the 200-point backlog, with roughly 24
points (about 12%) of spare capacity for growth or re-prioritisation. Predictive forecasting fixes scope and
solves for time/cost; adaptive forecasting fixes time/cost and solves for scope — the same underlying
arithmetic logic, pointed in opposite directions.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Computing classical CPI/SPI directly against an intentionally variable agile backlog without adapting the
  method — treating a re-prioritised backlog as "scope creep" against an unmodified BAC misreads a deliberate,
  healthy adaptive behaviour as an adverse variance. This is precisely why AgileEVM (KA 9.5) exists as a
  distinct, adapted technique rather than an instruction simply to reuse classical EVM unmodified.
- Treating velocity as an absolute, cross-team-comparable unit — a story point is calibrated to one team's own
  relative-estimation history (KA 9.3) and is not a currency; comparing Team A's "20 points/sprint" to Team
  B's "15 points/sprint" says nothing about relative productivity.
- Assuming "fixed time/cost" is automatically true rather than actively verified — a team's run-rate must
  still be confirmed as genuinely stable (staff not silently added or removed) before it can be relied on as
  a forecasting input, exactly as PV's integrity underpins every classical EVM calculation (KA 6.1.2).

**AI in this topic.** AI-based forecasting tools can compute rolling velocity averages, generate range
forecasts (optimistic/likely/pessimistic — developed fully in KA 9.5), and flag automatically when either a
predictive EAC or an adaptive burnup trend breaches an agreed tolerance (cross-reference KA 13.5). They cannot
decide *which* measurement framework is the right one for a given work package — that is the 8.6.2 selection
judgement, made before any forecast is computed — nor substitute for the professional's sign-off on any
forecast reported to a sponsor or client. **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

### 8.6.5 Selecting an approach: a worked suitability-assessment mini-case

**Definition & purpose.** This topic turns 8.6.2's six selection criteria into a **scored suitability
filter** — in the spirit of the suitability-assessment tools described in PMI's *Agile Practice Guide*
(awareness level; not reproduced here) — and applies it to the two Meridian-adjacent scopes carried through
this KA, producing a structured, auditable recommendation rather than an intuitive guess.

**The underlying principle.** No single factor should decide a development approach alone. A structured,
multi-factor filter — even a simple additive one, as used here — forces the assessor to consider each
dimension explicitly, and leaves a documented record of *why* an approach was chosen, which is valuable
evidence at later governance review or audit (cross-reference KA 9.6). The numeric score below is a
structuring device for a qualitative judgement, not a scientifically validated instrument — a pitfall
returned to below.

**The filter.** Each of 8.6.2's six factors is scored from **1** (strongly favours predictive) to **5**
(strongly favours adaptive). The six scores are summed and averaged. As a working guide, not a mechanical
rule: an average at or below **2.0** points toward a predictive approach; at or above **4.0** points toward
adaptive; scores in the middle band point toward the hybrid patterns of 8.6.3 rather than a single approach
applied uniformly — reinforcing that this is a continuum, not a binary switch.

**Example 8.6.5 — Scoring Pipeline Segment 4B and the Field Progress & Punch-list App.**

*Scenario.* Meridian Rail Contracting LLC's PMO must decide, and document, a development approach for two
scopes within its rail-signalling contract: (a) the remaining trenching, pipe-laying and welding work on
Pipeline Segment 4B; (b) the Field Progress & Punch-list App.

*Required.* Score both scopes against the six-factor filter; compute each average; recommend an approach,
applying the decision rule above.

*Working.*

| Factor (1 = predictive, 5 = adaptive) | Pipeline Segment 4B | Field App |
|---|---|---|
| Requirements/solution certainty | 1 | 5 |
| Practicality of early partial delivery | 1 | 5 |
| End-user/customer feedback availability | 1 | 4 |
| Cost/reversibility of a late change | 1 | 5 |
| Regulatory/contractual/safety fixity | 1 | 4 |
| Team structure (dedicated/co-located vs. dispersed multi-trade) | 2 | 5 |
| **Sum** | **7** | **28** |
| **Average** | **7/6 = 1.17** | **28/6 = 4.67** |

*Answer.* Pipeline Segment 4B scores **1.17** — well below 2.0: a **strongly predictive** approach is
confirmed, consistent with its treatment throughout Domains 1–7, 10 and 12. The Field App scores **4.67** —
well above 4.0: a **strongly adaptive** approach is confirmed, delivered as Scrum (KA 9.2), most naturally
contracted as capped time & materials (KA 7.1, KA 9.6).

*Interpretation.* The gap between 1.17 and 4.67, consistent across all six independently assessed factors, is
wide enough that no plausible re-weighting of the criteria would flip either recommendation — both are clear-
cut cases. A genuinely marginal scope — one landing an average nearer 2.5–3.5 — is exactly where 8.6.3's
hybrid pattern, not a single blanket approach, becomes the right answer, and is worth flagging explicitly when
this filter is applied to future Meridian work packages. Note, too, that choosing "adaptive" for the Field App
does not remove it from Meridian's programme-level governance: as shown in 8.6.3, its rolled-up status still
reports into the same monthly stage-gate report as Pipeline Segment 4B — hybrid programme governance and a
clear-cut approach choice for an individual work package are not in tension.

Chart type: grouped bar chart, six factors on the horizontal axis, score (1–5) on the vertical axis, two bars

**Animation 8.6.5 — "Scoring the filter" [digital-only].** Frame 1: six empty factor slots. Frame 2: the

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating the numeric average as false precision — it is a structuring device for a documented, auditable
  judgement, not a scientifically calibrated instrument; a score of 1.17 is not "more correct" than a
  qualitative "strongly predictive," it is simply easier to record and defend consistently.
- Anchoring on the first factor scored and rating the remaining factors to match it, rather than assessing
  each of the six independently — this defeats the filter's purpose, which is to surface disagreement between
  factors, not manufacture agreement.
- Scoring once and never revisiting — if a scope's nature changes materially mid-project (for example, if the
  Field App later required formal safety-case certification for a site-access feature), that component's own
  score would shift toward predictive, and the filter should be reapplied to it specifically.

**AI in this topic.** An AI assistant can pre-populate a first-draft suitability-filter score from a work
package's charter or description text, giving a consistent starting point across a large portfolio of work
packages far faster than manual review (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). Every factor score rests on
organisational, contractual and safety context an AI cannot fully see and is not accountable for; the
professional must review, and may override, every score before the filter's output is used to justify a
governance decision. **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Cross-references.** KA 8.6.2 (the six criteria, defined in full); KA 9.6 (hybrid delivery & agile
governance, in full); KA 7.1 (the contract-type follow-through for each recommendation); KA 4.3 (the rolled-up
reporting cadence referenced in the interpretation above).

### Key terms

- **Development approach** — the method a project uses to create and evolve its product: how completely
  requirements are defined before work starts, and how frequently a usable result is delivered.
- **Predictive (waterfall)** — scope, schedule and cost fixed early; the process groups applied once, in
  sequence, to a single final delivery.
- **Iterative** — the product is built through repeated passes that revisit and refine the same work.
- **Incremental** — the product is delivered through successive, separately usable slices, each adding new
  scope.
- **Adaptive (agile)** — iterative and incremental delivery combined with an empirical, feedback-driven
  planning cycle; scope is deliberately variable and discovered progressively.
- **Cost-of-change curve** — the tendency for the cost of altering a decision to rise over the project
  lifecycle; steep for physically executed work, comparatively flat for software.
- **Suitability filter** — a structured, multi-factor assessment (here, a six-factor, 1–5 scored tool) used to
  recommend a development approach for a given scope.
- **Hybrid delivery** — combining more than one development approach within a project or programme, applying
  each to the component of work it best fits.
- **Tailoring** — adapting a project's processes, formality and development approach to its specific context
  (PMBOK Guide, 7th edition).
- **Iron triangle, inverted** — the adaptive reframing in which time and cost are the fixed inputs and scope
  is the variable planned for and forecast (the converse of predictive's fixed-scope logic).
- **Velocity** — story points completed per sprint (defined in full in KA 9.3); the primary adaptive progress
  unit used in 8.6.4's worked example.
- **Agile-in-name-only** — running adaptive ceremonies (stand-ups, "sprints") over an underlying scope,
  sequence and sign-off structure that remains fully predictive and fixed.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 8.6.1 [topic 8.6.1, level: recall]**
Which best distinguishes "incremental" from "iterative" delivery?
A. Incremental repeats the same deliverable to refine it; iterative adds new, separately usable scope each
   pass
B. **Incremental adds new, separately usable scope each pass; iterative repeats and refines the same
   deliverable** ✔
C. They are two names for the same thing
D. Incremental applies only to software; iterative applies only to construction
*Rationale:* as defined in 8.6.1, incremental = new usable slices stack; iterative = the same slice is
refined pass over pass. A reverses the definitions; C denies a distinction the exam blueprint tests
explicitly; D is an unsupported over-generalisation not tied to either definition.

**MCQ 8.6.2 [topic 8.6.1, level: application]**
Meridian's Field App team ships photo-capture in Sprint 1, punch-list assignment in Sprint 2, and offline
sync in Sprint 3 — each a new, independently usable capability added to the last. This delivery pattern is
best described as:
A. Purely iterative
B. Purely predictive
C. **Incremental** ✔
D. Big-bang (single final delivery)
*Rationale:* successive, separately usable slices stacking on one another is the definition of incremental
delivery (8.6.1). It is not iterative here because no single feature is being repeatedly refined in this
description; it is neither predictive nor big-bang, both of which imply a single final delivery.

**MCQ 8.6.3 [topic 8.6.2, level: analysis]**
Which factor most strongly favours a predictive development approach?
A. A small, co-located, cross-functional team
B. Low cost of making a change once work is executed
C. **High cost and impracticality of reversing a change once work is executed** ✔
D. Ready daily access to the end user for feedback
*Rationale:* per 8.6.2's selection-criteria table, a high, hard-to-reverse cost of late change is the
clearest predictive-favouring factor. A, B and D each point toward adaptive.

**MCQ 8.6.4 [topic 8.6.4, level: application — numerical]**
The Field App has delivered a cumulative 56 story points after 3 sprints, against a 200-point backlog. What
is the average velocity, and how many additional sprints are forecast to complete the remaining backlog
(rounded up to a whole sprint)?
A. 18.7 points/sprint; 7 sprints
B. **18.7 points/sprint; 8 sprints** ✔
C. 56 points/sprint; 4 sprints
D. 200 points/sprint; 1 sprint
*Rationale:* `56/3 = 18.7` points/sprint; remaining backlog `200 − 56 = 144`; `144/18.7 = 7.7 → 8` sprints
(rounded up, since a fractional sprint cannot be delivered). A rounds down instead of up; C and D
misidentify a cumulative total as a per-sprint rate.

**MCQ 8.6.5 [topic 8.6.5, level: application — numerical]**
Using the six-factor suitability filter (1 = strongly predictive, 5 = strongly adaptive), a scope scores
1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2 across the six factors. What is its average score, and what does the filter's decision rule
indicate?
A. Average 1.3; strongly favours adaptive
B. **Average 1.3; strongly favours predictive** ✔
C. Average 8; hybrid
D. Average 1.3; inconclusive, requires a seventh factor
*Rationale:* sum `= 1+1+2+1+1+2 = 8`; average `= 8/6 = 1.33`. Per 8.6.5's decision rule, an average at or
below 2.0 points toward a strongly predictive approach. C mistakes the sum for the average; D is unsupported
— the filter is designed to be decisive from six independently assessed factors.

**MCQ 8.6.6 [topic 8.6.3, level: analysis]**
A team holds daily stand-ups and calls its two-week cycles "sprints," but the scope, sequence and acceptance
criteria for all ten cycles were fixed and signed off before Cycle 1 began, with no backlog re-prioritisation
permitted thereafter. This is best described as:
A. Genuine adaptive delivery
B. A well-executed hybrid approach
C. **Agile-in-name-only — predictive delivery wearing adaptive ceremonies** ✔
D. Incremental delivery, correctly applied
*Rationale:* genuine adaptive delivery requires scope that is actually re-prioritisable through empirical
feedback (8.6.1, 8.6.3); fixing all scope and sequence upfront while retaining only the ceremonies is exactly
the "agile-in-name-only" pitfall named in 8.6.3 — not genuine agile, not a deliberate hybrid, and not simply
incremental delivery (which does not by itself imply these ceremonies).

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. In your own words, explain the difference between iterative and incremental delivery, and describe how
   Scrum uses both at once (cross-reference KA 9.2).
2. Using the six-factor suitability filter, would you expect a national electrical safety-certification test
   campaign (fixed test procedures, regulator sign-off required, high cost of re-testing) to score nearer 1
   or nearer 5? Justify your answer against at least two of the six factors.
3. A team has completed 4 sprints with cumulative story points of 15, 33, 50 and 70. Calculate the average
   velocity and forecast how many further sprints are needed to complete a 150-point backlog.

**Answers**

1. Iterative delivery revisits and refines the *same* piece of work across repeated passes without
   necessarily adding new scope; incremental delivery adds *new*, separately usable scope with each pass.
   Scrum uses both simultaneously: across sprints, the product Increment accumulates new functionality
   (incremental), while within and across those same sprints, earlier work is revisited and refined in light
   of Sprint Review feedback (iterative) — KA 9.2 develops this in full.
2. Nearer **1** (strongly predictive). Requirements/solution certainty is high — the test procedures are
   fixed by regulation, not discovered through delivery — and the cost/reversibility of a late change is
   poor — re-testing is expensive and may re-trigger the full certification sequence. Both factors point
   firmly toward predictive; the regulatory/contractual fixity factor reinforces the same conclusion.
3. Per-sprint deltas: Sprint 1 = 15; Sprint 2 = `33 − 15 = 18`; Sprint 3 = `50 − 33 = 17`; Sprint 4 =
   `70 − 50 = 20` (sum `15+18+17+20 = 70`, matching the cumulative total). Average velocity = `70/4 = 17.5`
   points/sprint. Remaining backlog = `150 − 70 = 80`. Further sprints needed = `80/17.5 = 4.6 → 5 sprints`
   (rounded up).
