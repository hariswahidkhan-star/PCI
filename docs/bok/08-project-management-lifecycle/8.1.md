# Domain 8 — Project Management Lifecycle

## KA 8.1 — Initiating

Every project this book has examined so far — Pipeline Segment 4B's cost baseline (Domain 3), its
earned-value data set (Domain 6), its risk register (Domain 12) — already existed as an authorised,
resourced, governed undertaking by the time those domains picked it up. Domain 8 goes back to before any of
that existed and asks the more basic question: how does a piece of work become "a project" at all, with a
named accountable manager, an approved budget and an agreed scope, rather than simply an intention? The
answer, in the language most widely used across the profession, is the set of project management **process
groups** codified by PMI's PMBOK Guide — **Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling and
Closing** — which this domain treats one Knowledge Area at a time: KA 8.1 (this KA) covers **Initiating** —
the business case, the charter, stakeholder identification, and the high-level scope, estimate and
authorisation that together convert an opportunity into an authorised project. KA 8.2 covers **Planning** —
the full set of subsidiary plans and baselines (scope/WBS, schedule, cost, quality, resource, risk,
procurement, communications, integration) that this KA's high-level outputs are matured into. KA 8.3 covers
**Executing**; KA 8.4 covers **Monitoring & Controlling**, where the earned-value and variance-analysis
techniques already built in Domains 4 and 6 are formally located within the wider lifecycle; and KA 8.5
covers **Closing**, running a single project through all five process groups in a threaded mini-case. KA 8.6
closes the domain by setting the whole process-group model against the adaptive/agile spectrum developed
fully in Domain 9. This KA returns to **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC** and **Pipeline Segment 4B** — the
$600,000 (SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) control account already familiar from Domains
3, 6 and 12 — but earlier in its life than any of those domains has shown: before the Class 1 estimate,
before the baseline, before the risk register, at the point where Meridian's leadership first had to decide
whether to pursue the work at all.

### 8.1.1 The project life cycle, process groups and phase gates: where Initiating sits

**Definition and purpose.** A **project life cycle** is the series of phases a project passes through from
its start to its finish, providing the overarching structure for governing a project regardless of the
specific delivery approach used within each phase (predictive, adaptive or hybrid — the full spectrum is
KA 8.6). PMI's PMBOK Guide's five **process groups** — Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring &
Controlling, and Closing — are a related but distinct idea: they group processes by the *type of management
activity* being performed, not by calendar sequence. A **phase-gate** (or **stage-gate**) life cycle, by
contrast, is a governance structure widely used in engineering, construction and capital-projects
environments, in which a project moves through a small number of sequential phases, each ending in a formal
checkpoint — a "gate" — at which a sponsor reviews the phase's outputs and authorises (or declines to
authorise) release of resources for the next phase.

**The underlying principle.** These are two different, complementary lenses over the same project, and
conflating them is a persistent source of confusion. The *process-group* lens describes what kind of work is
happening at any given moment — and, as PMBOK is explicit about, the process groups are not sequential
phases: they overlap, recur and iterate. Monitoring & Controlling runs continuously alongside Executing, not
as a discrete later stage; Planning is progressively elaborated as more becomes known, not completed once and
frozen. The *phase-gate* lens instead describes the sequential, checkpoint-based structure that controls how
much scope, budget and risk an organisation commits at each stage, and it is this second lens that gives
Initiating its practical teeth: the process group produces a charter, but it is the gate — the sponsor's
formal decision — that actually releases the resources the charter describes. In a single-phase project the
two lenses nearly coincide; in a multi-phase programme, such as the wider rail-corridor programme Pipeline
Segment 4B belongs to, Initiating recurs — in miniature — at the start of every new phase or segment, each
with its own gate.

**Formula.** This topic is conceptual; no arithmetic formula applies, in the same way KA 3.2.1 stated that no
formula defines an AACE estimate class — the "formula" here is the sequence and governance logic set out
below.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating the five PMBOK process groups as if they were five sequential *phases* of
the project — reading "Executing" as something that only starts once "Planning" has entirely finished, when
in reality later planning iterations continue well into execution. (2) Skipping a formal, documented
Initiating step for a phase or segment because "we already know the project" (a common temptation on a later
segment of a familiar multi-segment programme) — losing the documented authority and success criteria that
later govern scope decisions (§8.1.8's mini-case shows what this costs in practice). (3) Assuming Initiating
is a single, whole-programme event: on a multi-segment programme such as Meridian's wider rail-corridor
contract, each new segment typically needs at least a lightweight re-initiation of its own.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted governance and portfolio tools can generate a first-draft phase-gate
structure for a new opportunity by pattern-matching against similar past programmes, and can flag which gate
a given document package appears to satisfy against a stated checklist — a genuine time-saver in preparing
governance packs (cross-reference KA 13.5). Such a tool cannot judge whether a gate's criteria are
*substantively* met — that a business case's assumptions are realistic, that a charter's authority is
properly delegated — which remains the sponsor's accountable judgement. **AI proposes; the professional
disposes.**

### 8.1.2 The business case and investment appraisal: payback, NPV and IRR

**Definition and purpose.** A **business case** is the documented justification for why a project should be
undertaken: the opportunity or problem it addresses, the options considered, the recommended option, and its
expected costs and benefits. PMI's PMBOK Guide treats the business case as a key input the project charter
draws on; frameworks such as the Association for Project Management's Body of Knowledge go further, treating
the business case as a *living* document — owned by the sponsor and revisited at each phase gate, not filed
away once Initiating ends — a distinction worth carrying forward explicitly, since a business case that is
never revisited cannot tell a sponsor whether a project it authorised on Day 1 still makes sense on Day 200.
Testing whether a specific proposed course of action is worth funding is the job of **investment appraisal**,
and three techniques recur throughout project controls practice: **payback period**, **net present value
(NPV)** and **internal rate of return (IRR)**.

**The underlying principle.** Money committed today has an opportunity cost — it could have been used
elsewhere — and money received in the future is worth less than the same sum received today, because of that
same opportunity cost and the effect of inflation and risk (the **time value of money**). Payback, NPV and
IRR each answer a different question about a stream of cash flows: **payback** asks *how quickly is the cash
recovered* (a liquidity and rough risk screen; it ignores what happens after payback and, in its simple form,
ignores discounting altogether); **NPV** asks *does this add value in absolute terms*, once every future cash
flow is discounted back to today's money at the organisation's cost of capital; **IRR** asks *what rate of
return does this investment actually generate*, expressed as a single percentage comparable against a hurdle
rate. NPV and IRR normally agree on a straightforward accept/reject call for a single, conventional
investment (one initial outflow followed by inflows); payback answers a genuinely different question and
should be read alongside the other two, not instead of them.

**Formula(e).**

`Payback period = full years until recovery + (unrecovered investment at start of that year / cash flow during that year)`

`NPV = Σ [CFₜ / (1 + r)ᵗ], t = 0 to n` — where *CFₜ* is the net cash flow in period *t* (USD; *CF₀*, the
initial investment, is normally negative), *r* is the discount rate/cost of capital (% per period), and *n*
is the final period of the appraisal. Decision rule: **NPV ≥ 0 → accept** (the investment is expected to add
at least as much value as the cost of the capital tied up in it); NPV < 0 → reject.

`IRR` = the discount rate at which `NPV = 0`. At awareness level, IRR is commonly estimated by **linear
interpolation** between two trial discount rates that bracket a zero NPV:
`IRR ≈ r_low + [NPV_low / (NPV_low − NPV_high)] × (r_high − r_low)`. Decision rule: **IRR ≥ hurdle rate →
accept.**

**Example 8.1.2 — the investment appraisal behind Meridian's go/no-go decision on Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* Meridian has been invited to tender for Pipeline Segment 4B, part of the wider rail-corridor
programme. Delivering WP3 (Trenching & earthworks) requires the excavator already costed in KA 3.2.4 at an
$800/day hire rate. Rather than continuing to hire, Meridian's steering committee is weighing whether to
*purchase* an excavator of its own — a decision bundled into the same go/no-go authorisation as Pipeline
Segment 4B itself, because taking the segment is what creates the need. Purchase price: $150,000 (SAR
562,500). Based on planned utilisation of roughly 75 days per year across Pipeline Segment 4B and the wider
programme's following segments, at the established $800/day hire rate, the avoided hire cost is
approximately **$60,000 per year (SAR 225,000)** for three years, after which the machine is expected to be
traded in for a **salvage value of $30,000 (SAR 112,500)**. Meridian's finance function sets a hurdle rate
(cost of capital) of **10% per year** for plant investment decisions of this kind.

*Required.* The payback period, the NPV at 10%, and the IRR (by interpolation), and a recommendation.

*Working.*

| Year | Cash flow ($) | Discount factor @10% | Present value ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | (150,000) | 1.000 | (150,000) |
| 1 | 60,000 | 0.909 | 54,540 |
| 2 | 60,000 | 0.826 | 49,560 |
| 3 | 60,000 + 30,000 salvage = 90,000 | 0.751 | 67,590 |

*Payback:* cumulative cash flow: Year 0 `(150,000)`; Year 1 `(150,000) + 60,000 = (90,000)`; Year 2
`(90,000) + 60,000 = (30,000)`; recovered during Year 3, needing `30,000 / 90,000 = 0.33` of that year's cash
flow.
`Payback period = 2 + 0.33 = 2.33 years` — approximately **2 years 4 months**.

*NPV:* `Sum of PVs (Years 1–3) = 54,540 + 49,560 + 67,590 = 171,690`
`NPV = 171,690 − 150,000 = +21,690` (SAR 81,338 at the illustrative rate).

*IRR (by interpolation):* re-running the same cash flows at two trial rates —

At 15%: `PV = (60,000 × 0.870) + (60,000 × 0.756) + (90,000 × 0.658) = 52,200 + 45,360 + 59,220 = 156,780`;
`NPV@15% = 156,780 − 150,000 = +6,780`.
At 18%: `PV = (60,000 × 0.847) + (60,000 × 0.718) + (90,000 × 0.609) = 50,820 + 43,080 + 54,810 = 148,710`;
`NPV@18% = 148,710 − 150,000 = −1,290`.

`IRR ≈ 15% + [6,780 / (6,780 + 1,290)] × (18% − 15%) = 15% + 0.840 × 3% = 15% + 2.52% ≈ 17.5%`

*Answer.* **Payback ≈ 2.33 years; NPV at 10% = +$21,690; IRR ≈ 17.5%.**

*Interpretation.* All three measures point the same way: the payback (2.33 years) recovers the investment
comfortably within the machine's 3-year appraisal horizon; the NPV is positive, meaning the purchase is
expected to add roughly $21,690 of value in today's money over simply continuing to hire; and the IRR (≈17.5%)
comfortably clears Meridian's 10% hurdle rate, confirming the NPV outcome from a different angle. This is the
figure Meridian's steering committee actually takes to Gate 0/Gate 1 (§8.1.1) alongside the qualitative case
for pursuing Pipeline Segment 4B itself — continuing the framework relationship with the Client and
positioning Meridian for the programme's following segments — neither of which is captured by the NPV figure
alone, and both of which belong in the business case narrative, not the arithmetic.

**Mini-case — how sensitive is the "go" decision to the savings assumption?** Suppose the estimated avoided
hire cost is revised down to a more conservative **$45,000 per year** (utilisation lower than first assumed):
`Sum of PVs = (45,000 × 0.909) + (45,000 × 0.826) + (75,000 × 0.751) = 40,905 + 37,170 + 56,325 = 134,400`;
`NPV = 134,400 − 150,000 = −15,600` — **negative**. At the lower utilisation assumption, the purchase no
longer clears Meridian's hurdle rate and the recommendation flips to continuing to hire. This is precisely
why the underlying utilisation assumption (75 days/year, drawn from the wider programme's forecast segment
pipeline, not from Pipeline Segment 4B alone) must be stated and defended in the business case, not buried as
an unexamined input — a single assumption, changed, moved the recommendation from accept to reject.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Relying on payback alone: it ignores every cash flow after the recovery point and,
in its simple undiscounted form, ignores the time value of money entirely — a project with a fast payback but
weak later cash flows can look better than a genuinely more valuable one. (2) Ranking mutually exclusive
projects of different scale purely by IRR without also comparing NPV — a smaller project can show a higher
percentage IRR while adding less absolute value than a larger one; NPV, not IRR, is the theoretically
preferred criterion for ranking projects of different sizes. (3) Using a discount rate not genuinely tied to
the organisation's actual cost of capital or risk-adjusted hurdle rate — an arbitrary rate makes the whole
appraisal look precise while resting on an unfounded number.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted financial-modelling tools can build and stress-test a multi-scenario
NPV/IRR model far faster than manual spreadsheet iteration, running dozens of discount-rate and cash-flow
sensitivities (such as the utilisation mini-case above) in seconds (cross-reference KA 13.5). Such tools
cannot set the organisation's actual cost of capital — a treasury/finance policy decision — nor can they
know whether an input assumption (avoided hire days, salvage value) is realistic for this specific market;
an AI-generated sensitivity table is only as trustworthy as the assumptions a professional has confirmed feed
it, and the accept/reject recommendation remains the sponsor's decision.

### 8.1.3 The project charter: contents and authority

**Definition and purpose.** The **project charter** is, in PMI's PMBOK Guide, the document issued by the
project sponsor that formally authorises a project to exist and gives a named project manager the authority
to apply organisational resources to it. It is the single output of Initiating that converts "an approved
business case" into "an authorised project with an accountable owner." A typical charter states: the
project's purpose and its link back to the business case (§8.1.2); measurable objectives and success criteria
(§8.1.6); a summary of high-level requirements and scope boundaries (§8.1.6); high-level risks (previewed
here, fully registered in Planning — cross-reference KA 12.2); a summary milestone schedule and a summary
budget; a preliminary stakeholder list (§8.1.4); the assigned project manager and their delegated authority
(what they may commit, and up to what limit, without further sponsor approval); and the sponsor's name and
approval.

**The underlying principle.** A charter is deliberately a *summary* document, not a substitute for the
detailed plans Planning (KA 8.2) will produce — its value lies in being short enough to be genuinely read and
approved at sponsor level, while still being specific enough to give the project manager real, documented
authority. Without a signed charter, a project manager has no formal standing to commit resources, accept
scope changes, or direct a team — informal authority ("everyone knows I'm running this") is not the same
thing, and it collapses the moment a decision is disputed.

reached (§8.1.1, §8.1.8).

*Interpretation.* Every figure in this table either has already been fully worked in an earlier domain (the
$644,000 estimate, §8.1.7) or is deliberately left as a cross-reference to a later, fuller treatment — the
charter's discipline is to summarise and authorise, not to duplicate content that belongs elsewhere.

**Mini-case — the verbal addition.** Under commercial pressure to keep the tender programme moving, Meridian
mobilises a small site survey team before the charter above is signed. During that visit, the Client's site
representative verbally asks the team to also investigate relocating an unmarked drainage chamber found near
the corridor — work outside Pipeline Segment 4B's original boundaries. With no signed charter yet in place
naming who holds authority to accept scope changes, and at what value, the site team agrees informally and
proceeds. Three weeks later, once the charter is signed and the survey invoiced, Meridian's commercial team
cannot recover the associated $18,000 from the Client: there is no documented instruction, no named approver,
and no audit trail connecting the informal verbal request to a properly authorised change. *Interpretation.*
This is precisely why the charter — and, specifically, its delegated-authority clause — must be in place and
understood by the delivery team *before* mobilisation, not treated as paperwork to catch up on once work is
already under way.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating the charter as a one-off formality signed and then filed away, rather than
the instrument the project manager's actual authority rests on throughout delivery. (2) Writing a charter so
detailed it duplicates Planning's outputs (a full WBS, a detailed schedule) — defeating its purpose as a
concise, sponsor-readable authorisation document. (3) Leaving the delegated-authority threshold vague or
unstated, as the mini-case shows, so the project team has no clear boundary for what it may commit to without
escalation.

**AI in this topic.** AI drafting tools can generate a first-pass charter from a business case, a contract
document or a template, populating standard fields quickly and consistently (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA
13.5) — a genuine time-saver, particularly for the summary milestone and risk-preview fields. The specific
delegated-authority threshold, the named sponsor and project manager, and the sponsor's actual approval are
governance decisions and acts that only the accountable individuals can make; an AI-drafted charter is a
draft for review and signature, never a substitute for it.

### 8.1.4 Identifying stakeholders and the stakeholder register

**Definition and purpose.** A **stakeholder**, in PMI's PMBOK Guide's definition, is an individual, group or
organisation that may affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by a decision, activity or
outcome of a project. **Identify Stakeholders** is the Initiating-stage process that finds them
systematically, and its documented output is the **stakeholder register** — a structured list capturing, for
each stakeholder, their role, whether they are internal or external to the performing organisation, their
interests and expectations, and (feeding §8.1.5) an initial power/interest assessment.

**The underlying principle.** Left to an unstructured brainstorm, a project team tends to identify the
stakeholders who are loudest, most senior, or most recently encountered — the same **availability bias**
already named in KA 12.1.7 for risk identification, applied here to people rather than uncertain events.
Systematic techniques — reviewing the contract and correspondence, walking the organisation chart of both
Meridian and the Client, and deliberately asking "who else could be affected by this work, even indirectly"
— exist specifically to counter that bias, in the same way the risk breakdown structure counters it for risk
identification.

*Interpretation.* Several entries reuse stakeholders already introduced as risk-register parties in KA 12.1
— the Client/rail operator (R-05), the signalling-equipment supplier (R-02) and the utility owners (R-01) —
which is not a coincidence: a party capable of *causing* a registered risk is, by definition, also a
stakeholder whose interests and expectations the project must actively manage, not simply a name attached to
a probability and impact score.

**Mini-case — a stakeholder missed on the first pass.** Meridian's initial identification workshop, run under
time pressure, omits the local municipal drainage authority, whose approval is required for any connection
into the existing drainage network along the corridor. The omission is caught only when the design team seeks
a routine technical clearance and discovers a stakeholder with genuine approval authority was never engaged.
*Interpretation.* This is the availability-bias pattern in practice: a workshop populated mainly by people
already known to the immediate delivery team under-represents stakeholders whose involvement is procedural
rather than visible day to day — exactly the gap a structured checklist (organisation chart review, permit
and consent register, contract review) is designed to close.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Limiting identification to stakeholders with an obviously visible, immediate
interest, missing regulatory, permitting or utility-owner parties whose involvement is procedural rather than
constant (the mini-case above). (2) Treating the register as a one-time Initiating deliverable rather than
updating it as new stakeholders emerge through delivery (cross-reference KA 12.1.3's risk-to-issue lifecycle
— a comparable "keep it current" discipline). (3) Recording only names and roles without capturing genuine
interests and expectations, leaving §8.1.5's power/interest classification with nothing substantive to work
from.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted document- and correspondence-scanning tools can trawl contracts,
organisation charts, permit registers and prior project records for candidate stakeholders far faster than a
manual review, surfacing procedural parties (permitting authorities, utility owners) that a workshop-only
approach can miss (cross-reference KA 13.5) — directly useful against the availability-bias pattern named
above. Such a tool has no independent way to judge a candidate stakeholder's actual current interest or
influence on *this* project without further input, and a plausible-looking automated list still needs a
professional to confirm it is complete and accurate before it becomes the register a charter and engagement
plan rely on.

### 8.1.5 Stakeholder analysis: the power-interest grid

**Definition and purpose.** The **power–interest grid** — commonly attributed to Mendelow, and widely used
across project management and strategy texts — is a simple analytical tool that classifies each identified
stakeholder against two dimensions: **power** (their ability to influence the project — through authority,
resource control or decision rights) and **interest** (how much attention and concern they have in the
project's outcome). Plotting stakeholders onto a 2×2 grid yields four quadrants, each associated with a
different, proportionate engagement strategy, described here in the author's own words rather than any
single source's exact wording: **Manage Closely** (high power, high interest — engage actively and
frequently); **Keep Satisfied** (high power, low interest — meet their needs without over-communicating);
**Keep Informed** (low power, high interest — provide regular information, since their interest can inform
useful feedback); and **Monitor** (low power, low interest — minimal effort, but watch for change).

**The underlying principle.** Not every stakeholder warrants the same engagement effort, and treating them
uniformly wastes scarce project-management attention on parties who need little of it while under-serving
those who need more. The grid's real value is *differentiation* — it forces an explicit judgement about each
stakeholder's power and interest rather than an implicit, unexamined assumption that "senior" automatically
means "needs managing closely" or that a quiet stakeholder can be safely ignored. Because power and interest
can both change as a project progresses — a dormant stakeholder can become highly engaged the moment an issue
affecting them materialises — the grid is a **periodically reviewed** classification, not a one-time exercise
frozen at Initiating.

**Example 8.1.5 — plotting Pipeline Segment 4B's stakeholder register onto the power–interest grid.**

*Scenario.* From the §8.1.4 register, Meridian's project controls lead rates each *external and governance*
stakeholder's power and interest (internal delivery-team roles — the Project Controls Lead, Design Engineer
and Procurement Lead — are deliberately excluded here, since they are managed directly through the project
team structure and reporting line, not through a stakeholder-engagement strategy).

*Required.* The quadrant for each stakeholder, and the resulting engagement approach.

*Working/Answer.*

| Stakeholder | Power | Interest | Quadrant | Engagement approach |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Client (rail authority / possession-granting operator) | High | High | Manage Closely | Regular joint review meetings; early escalation of any issue affecting schedule or access |
| Meridian Executive Sponsor / Board | High | Low | Keep Satisfied | Periodic capital/return reporting; no need for routine operational detail |
| Rail safety regulator | High | Low | Keep Satisfied | Formal compliance reporting on a fixed cadence; engage promptly if a compliance question arises |
| Cable/duct installation subcontractor | Low | High | Keep Informed | Clear, regular progress and instruction communication; their interest is genuine but they hold limited independent authority |
| Signalling/interlocking equipment supplier | Low | High | Keep Informed | Coordinated delivery-schedule updates, given the interface risk (KA 12.1's R-02) |

*Interpretation.* Only one stakeholder sits in Manage Closely — a realistic, not a padded, outcome; the
discipline of the grid is precisely that it resists inflating everyone to the top-attention quadrant. The two

something AI has no independent way to assess — and an under-rated stakeholder (a regulator whose power is
quietly assumed to be low) carries real consequences if the classification is wrong; the professional
confirms every quadrant placement.

### 8.1.6 High-level scope and success criteria

**Definition and purpose.** At Initiating, **high-level scope** states the broad boundary of what a project
will — and, importantly, will *not* — deliver, in enough detail to authorise the project without yet
producing the detailed Work Breakdown Structure that Planning (KA 8.2) builds. **Success criteria** are the
specific, measurable conditions that must be met for the project — and, crucially, its sponsor — to consider
it successful. These are not simply a restatement of the traditional time/cost/scope-quality constraints
(often called the "iron triangle" of project management): a project can hit every one of those targets and
still fail to deliver the strategic benefit that justified its business case (§8.1.2) in the first place, so
well-formed success criteria connect the deliverable back to that benefit, not only to the delivery
constraints.

**The underlying principle.** Scope and success criteria set at Initiating exist to bound the authorisation
being sought — without a stated "in scope / out of scope" boundary, interface disputes (who is responsible
for what, at the seam between two adjoining work packages or contracts) tend to surface expensively later,
during execution, rather than being resolved cheaply now, in a document everyone can read before work starts.
Success criteria set at Initiating exist to define, in advance and by agreement, what "done well" actually
means — criteria negotiated only after the fact, once a dispute has already arisen about whether the project
succeeded, defend nobody.

**Example 8.1.6 — Pipeline Segment 4B's high-level scope and success criteria.**

*Scenario/Required.* The scope boundary and success criteria Meridian's charter (§8.1.3) references.

*In scope:* trenching and reinstatement along the 2,000 m alignment; supply and installation of cable ducts,
trackside conduits and drainage-utility pipework (KA 3.2.4's WP1–WP5); testing and commissioning of the
installed pipework and ducting.

*Out of scope:* supply of the signalling/interlocking equipment itself (a separate contract package procured
directly by the Client — KA 12.1's R-02 supplier is a Client-appointed party, not a Meridian subcontractor);
any work beyond the defined 2,000 m alignment; permanent reinstatement of the running-line ballast (a
separate track-renewals contract).

*Success criteria:*
1. Delivered within the authorised cost baseline, once finalised at Planning (± the accuracy band applicable
   to the estimate class in force — cross-reference KA 3.2.1), with any approved variation tracked separately.
2. Substantial completion by Month 6, matching the schedule already used throughout KA 6.1.
3. Zero lost-time safety incidents across the works.
4. Formal Client acceptance at handover, with no more than a stated, small number of minor outstanding
   ("snagging") items.
5. The wider rail-corridor programme's signalling upgrade able to proceed on schedule once Segment 4B's ducts
   and conduits are handed over — an *outcome*-based criterion, not merely an output count, connecting
   directly back to the strategic benefit stated in the business case (§8.1.2).

*Interpretation.* Criterion 5 is deliberately different in kind from criteria 1–4: it is not satisfied merely
by counting metres of pipeline installed, but by confirming the delivered work actually enables the benefit
the business case was built to capture. A project can meet criteria 1–4 in full and still, if criterion 5 is
ignored, be judged a disappointment by the sponsor who approved the business case on the strength of the
wider programme benefit.

**Mini-case — the undefined interface.** Suppose the "out of scope" boundary above had been left unstated.
Partway through trenching, a dispute arises over who is responsible for temporary reinstatement of ballast
disturbed during excavation near the running line — Meridian, assuming it falls to the separate track-
renewals contractor; the Client, assuming Meridian's trenching scope implicitly includes making good any
disturbance it causes. Without an explicit boundary statement to point to, resolving the dispute consumes
meeting time, correspondence and goodwill that a single clear sentence in the charter would have avoided
entirely. *Interpretation.* An explicit "out of scope" statement is not bureaucratic caution; it is the
cheapest possible form of interface risk management, resolved once, in writing, before work starts, rather
than negotiated under pressure once a dispute is already live.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Stating success criteria so vaguely ("deliver a high-quality outcome") that they
cannot be objectively assessed at closure (cross-reference KA 8.5). (2) Confusing a high-level scope statement
with a complete WBS — the level of decomposition Planning, not Initiating, is responsible for (KA 8.2). (3)
Omitting an explicit out-of-scope statement, as the mini-case shows, leaving interface responsibility to be
argued out later rather than agreed in advance.

**AI in this topic.** AI drafting tools can propose a first-draft scope statement and a candidate set of
success criteria from a contract, request-for-tender document or similar past project, using pattern-matching
against comparable packages (cross-reference KA 13.5) — a genuine drafting accelerator, particularly for
routine in/out-of-scope boundary language. Such a tool cannot know which specific boundary or criterion is
commercially or politically sensitive for *this* Client relationship, nor can it anticipate an interface
dispute like the mini-case above without being told the site-specific context; the sponsor and project
manager must review, adjust and own the final wording before it is issued.

**Cross-references.** §8.1.2 (the strategic benefit success criterion 5 connects back to); §8.1.3 (the
charter that references this scope and these criteria); §8.1.7 (the high-level estimate this scope bounds);
KA 8.2 (the detailed WBS and scope statement this topic's boundary matures into); KA 8.5 (assessing success
criteria at Closing).

### 8.1.7 The initial high-level estimate and its accuracy class

**Definition and purpose.** At Initiating, a project's cost figure can only be as mature as the scope
definition behind it, and — as KA 3.2.1's AACE International Cost Estimate Classification System establishes
in full — that means, in practice, a **Class 5** estimate (occasionally Class 4), with a wide stated accuracy
range, because detailed design does not yet exist. This topic does not repeat that classification system; it
applies it specifically to the question Initiating must answer: is the high-level estimate, and its stated
range, close enough to any indicative budget ceiling to justify authorising the project to proceed into
Planning at all — a check commonly described as **feasibility screening**.

**The underlying principle.** An Initiating-stage estimate is never a committed budget — KA 3.2.1 already
established this as the single most damaging estimating error in project controls — but it must still be
*decision-useful*: precise enough, together with its honestly stated range, to tell a sponsor whether the
opportunity is realistically affordable before committing the far greater estimating effort a Class 3 or
Class 1 figure requires (KA 3.2's mini-case put that effort at roughly 4 hours for a Class 5 estimate against
roughly 140 hours for a Class 1 estimate on this same project). Feasibility screening compares the estimate's
full stated range — not just its point figure — against any known budget ceiling, because a point estimate
that comfortably fits under a ceiling can still carry a high-end range that does not.

**Formula.** Reusing KA 3.2.1's accuracy-range formula: `Expected range = Point estimate × (1 + Low%) to
Point estimate × (1 + High%)`, where Low% and High% are the AACE class's stated bounds (Class 5: −30% / +50%).

**Example 8.1.7 — feasibility-screening Pipeline Segment 4B's Class 5 estimate against the Client's indicative ceiling.**

*Scenario.* The Client's invitation to tender states an indicative budget ceiling of **$700,000 (SAR
2,625,000)** for Segment 4B. Meridian's Class 5 analogous estimate, already fully built in KA 3.2.2, is
**$644,000**, with a stated Class 5 accuracy range of −30%/+50% (KA 3.2.1): low bound `644,000 × 0.70 =
450,800`; high bound `644,000 × 1.50 = 966,000` (both figures exactly as computed in KA 3.2.1).

*Required.* The point estimate's headroom against the ceiling, and whether the full stated range fits within
it.

*Working.*
`Headroom (point estimate) = 700,000 − 644,000 = 56,000`, or `56,000 / 700,000 = 8.0%` of the ceiling.
`High-bound exceedance = 966,000 − 700,000 = 266,000`, or `266,000 / 700,000 = 38.0%` over the ceiling.

*Answer.* **The point estimate fits within the ceiling with 8.0% headroom, but the Class 5 estimate's high
bound exceeds the ceiling by 38.0%.**

*Interpretation.* Feasibility at Initiating is rarely a clean yes/no answer — it is exactly this kind of
range-against-ceiling picture. The point estimate alone would suggest comfortable affordability; the honest
Class 5 range instead flags a real, quantified residual risk that the project could ultimately cost more than
the Client's ceiling allows, a risk the sponsor should carry forward explicitly into the go/no-go decision
(§8.1.8) rather than have it silently disappear once only the point figure is quoted. As Planning progressively
narrows the estimate — the Class 3 figure of $610,000 and the Class 1 BAC of $600,000, both already built in
KA 3.2.3–3.2.4 — this range narrows in step, exactly as KA 3.2.1's accuracy funnel predicts.

**Mini-case — a tighter ceiling.** Suppose the Client's ceiling had instead been set at $650,000. The point
estimate ($644,000) would then leave almost no headroom at all (`650,000 − 644,000 = 6,000`, under 1%), and
the Class 5 range's high bound ($966,000) would exceed the ceiling by nearly 49%. *Interpretation.* A near-zero
headroom on the point estimate, combined with such a wide theoretical range, would not by itself prove the
project infeasible — Class 5 ranges are wide by construction, and real design development could well land
comfortably under the ceiling, as it in fact does in the real $700,000-ceiling scenario above — but it would
be a strong signal to the sponsor to treat design-development cost risk as a named, actively monitored item
from the very start of Planning, rather than only discovering the pressure once a Class 3 estimate is already
built.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Presenting the Class 5 point estimate to the sponsor as though it were a committed
budget figure — the reprised KA 3.2.1 pitfall, now applied at the specific moment a go/no-go decision is being
made on the strength of it. (2) Checking only the point estimate against the ceiling and never the range,
missing exactly the residual risk the worked example surfaces. (3) Treating "feasible" as a binary conclusion
rather than a probabilistic judgement — an overlapping range does not guarantee failure, any more than a
comfortable point-estimate headroom guarantees success; either way, it is a risk to carry forward and monitor,
not a verdict to file away.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted estimating and portfolio tools can rapidly screen many candidate
opportunities at once, comparing each one's high-level estimate and stated range against its indicative
ceiling and flagging which carry the most range-overlap risk — genuinely useful triage when Meridian is
weighing several bid opportunities simultaneously (cross-reference KA 3.2.1, KA 13.5). Such a tool cannot
itself decide whether a given overlap risk is acceptable to pursue; that is a risk-appetite judgement for the
sponsor and steering committee, exactly as KA 12.1.5 establishes for risk tolerance generally, applied here at
the bid-decision stage.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2.1 (the AACE classification system and accuracy-range formula in full); KA 3.2.2
(the $644,000 Class 5 estimate built in full); KA 3.2.3–3.2.4 (the Class 3/Class 1 maturation Planning
performs); §8.1.2 (the business case this estimate feeds); §8.1.8 (the go/no-go decision this feasibility
check informs).

### 8.1.8 Go/no-go authorisation and the transition to Planning

**Definition and purpose.** The **go/no-go decision** is the formal governance act, taken by the sponsor or
steering committee, that authorises (or declines to authorise) a project to proceed from Initiating into
Planning — Gate 1 in the phase-gate model set out in §8.1.1. It is not any single Initiating output taken in
isolation; it is a synthesis of everything this KA has built: the business case and investment appraisal
(§8.1.2), the charter's stated purpose and authority (§8.1.3), the stakeholder position (§8.1.4–8.1.5), the
high-level scope and success criteria (§8.1.6), and the feasibility of the high-level estimate against any
known ceiling (§8.1.7). In practice, the sponsor's signature on the charter (§8.1.3) is usually the formal
instrument through which "go" is recorded.

**The underlying principle.** A disciplined go/no-go decision resists being reduced to any single number — a
positive NPV alone, or stakeholder support alone, is necessary but not sufficient. The governance value of
the gate lies precisely in requiring the sponsor to weigh several, sometimes competing, kinds of evidence
together before committing resources, which is also why "go" should never be treated as an irreversible,
one-time event: later gates (Gate 2, baseline approval; Gate 3, handover — §8.1.1) exist specifically so the
sponsor can re-confirm, adjust, or, if the evidence has genuinely changed, halt the project as better
information becomes available through Planning and Executing.

**Example 8.1.8 — Meridian's Gate 1 decision pack for Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario/Required.* The synthesised evidence Meridian's steering committee reviews before authorising "go."

| Initiating output | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Business case / investment appraisal (§8.1.2) | Excavator purchase: payback 2.33 yrs, NPV +$21,690, IRR ≈17.5% vs 10% hurdle | Positive |
| Charter (§8.1.3) | Purpose, authority and delegated thresholds drafted and ready for signature | Ready |
| Stakeholder position (§8.1.4–8.1.5) | Client (Manage Closely) engaged and supportive; no unresolved objection from any Keep Satisfied party | Supportive |
| Scope and success criteria (§8.1.6) | In/out-of-scope boundary and five success criteria defined and measurable | Complete |
| High-level estimate and feasibility (§8.1.7) | $644,000 Class 5 estimate; 8.0% headroom against the $700,000 ceiling; high-bound range risk noted for active monitoring | Feasible, with a monitored range risk |

*Answer.* **Decision: GO.** The Sponsor signs the charter (§8.1.3), formally authorising Pipeline Segment 4B
to proceed into Planning (KA 8.2), where the Class 5 estimate is matured toward the Class 3 ($610,000) and
Class 1 ($600,000) figures already fully built in KA 3.2, and the charter's summary budget and schedule are
developed into the full cost, schedule and other baselines (cross-reference KA 3.1, KA 3.3, KA 10.1).

*Interpretation.* Nothing in this decision pack is, by itself, a guarantee of success — the range risk noted
in §8.1.7 remains real and unresolved at the moment "go" is recorded. What the pack demonstrates is that every
required Initiating output has been produced, reviewed and weighed together, and that the sponsor's decision
is traceable to specific, documented evidence rather than to instinct or schedule pressure alone.

**Mini-case — "go" does not mean "fixed."** Two months into Planning, refined design information reveals that
part of the alignment crosses ground conditions materially worse than the Class 5 estimate's comparator
assumed (foreshadowing the kind of adverse-ground risk later formalised as R-01 in KA 12.1). Rather than
treating the earlier "go" as binding regardless of new information, Meridian's steering committee revisits the
business case at Gate 2 (baseline approval, §8.1.1), using the now-more-mature Class 3 estimate and an updated
range. The programme is not cancelled — the revised numbers still clear the Client's ceiling — but the
decision to proceed at Gate 2 is a fresh, evidenced re-confirmation, not an automatic continuation of the
original Gate 1 "go." *Interpretation.* This is exactly the governance purpose phase gates serve: each one is
an opportunity to stop, adjust, or continue on the strength of current information, not a one-time hurdle
cleared once and never revisited.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating an initial "go" as irreversible, so that later gates become rubber-stamp
formalities rather than genuine re-confirmation points — defeating the purpose of having more than one gate at
all. (2) Allowing schedule pressure to force a "go" before the Initiating outputs are genuinely complete — a
rushed charter or an unresolved stakeholder objection that resurfaces, more expensively, during Planning or
Executing. (3) Leaving the decision authority for "go" undocumented or contested — an unclear or disputed
approval authority undermines the entire governance rationale for having a formal gate, echoing the same
authority gap the §8.1.3 mini-case shows going wrong at the charter-signing stage.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted governance and portfolio tools can assemble a go/no-go decision pack
automatically from the individual Initiating artefacts — pulling the appraisal figures, register status and
estimate range into a single dashboard for the steering committee (cross-reference KA 4.4, KA 13.5) — a
genuine reduction in preparation time for a routine, recurring governance activity across a portfolio of
segments. The go/no-go decision itself combines financial, strategic, stakeholder and risk judgement that
remains the sponsor's or steering committee's accountable call: **AI can assemble the pack; it cannot make, or
substitute for, the decision.**

**Cross-references.** §8.1.1–8.1.7 (every output synthesised here); KA 8.2 (Planning, the process group this
decision authorises entry to); KA 3.1, KA 3.3 (the baselines Planning builds); KA 3.2.3–3.2.4 (the Class 3/1
estimate maturation); KA 12.1 (the risk register this project's monitored range risk feeds into once
formalised).

### Key terms

- **Project life cycle** — the series of phases a project passes through from start to finish, providing the
  overarching governance structure regardless of delivery approach.
- **Process groups** — PMI's PMBOK Guide's five groupings of project management activity (Initiating,
  Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling, Closing), which overlap and recur rather than proceeding as
  strict sequential phases.
- **Phase gate / stage gate** — a formal sponsor checkpoint, at the end of a life-cycle phase, authorising (or
  declining) release of resources for the next phase.
- **Business case** — the documented justification for undertaking a project: opportunity, options, expected
  costs and benefits, and the recommended option; ideally a living document revisited at each gate.
- **Payback period** — the time required to recover an initial investment from its resulting cash flows.
- **Net present value (NPV)** — the sum of a project's cash flows, each discounted to today's value at the
  organisation's cost of capital, less the initial investment; NPV ≥ 0 supports acceptance.
- **Internal rate of return (IRR)** — the discount rate at which a project's NPV equals zero; IRR ≥ the hurdle
  rate supports acceptance.
- **Discount rate / hurdle rate / cost of capital** — the rate reflecting the time value of money and the
  organisation's required return, used to discount future cash flows.
- **Project charter** — the sponsor-issued document formally authorising a project and giving the project
  manager delegated authority over resources.
- **Stakeholder** — an individual, group or organisation that may affect, be affected by, or perceive itself
  to be affected by a project's decisions, activities or outcomes.
- **Stakeholder register** — the structured, documented list of identified stakeholders, their roles and their
  interests/expectations.
- **Power–interest grid** — a stakeholder-analysis tool (commonly attributed to Mendelow) classifying
  stakeholders by influence (power) and level of concern (interest) into four differentiated engagement
  quadrants: Manage Closely, Keep Satisfied, Keep Informed, Monitor.
- **Success criteria** — the specific, measurable conditions, agreed in advance, that define whether a project
  is judged successful, including outcome/benefit measures beyond the time/cost/scope-quality constraints.
- **Feasibility screening** — comparing a high-level estimate and its stated accuracy range against an
  indicative budget ceiling to test whether a project is realistically affordable before detailed planning.
- **Go/no-go decision** — the sponsor's or steering committee's formal authorisation (or refusal) to proceed
  from one phase-gated stage to the next, synthesising all preceding Initiating evidence.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 8.1.1 [topic 8.1.1, level: recall]**
Which statement best distinguishes PMI's PMBOK Guide process groups from a phase-gate life cycle?
A. They are two names for the same, strictly sequential structure
B. Process groups describe the type of management activity being performed and may overlap and recur; a
   phase-gate life cycle imposes sequential, checkpoint-based governance ✔
C. Process groups only apply once a phase-gate life cycle has ended
D. A phase-gate life cycle replaces the need for a project charter
*Rationale:* B correctly separates the two complementary lenses (§8.1.1). A wrongly collapses them into one
structure; C reverses their relationship; D confuses a governance structure with a specific document.

**MCQ 8.1.2 [topic 8.1.2, level: application — numerical]**
A project requires an initial investment of $50,000 and generates $30,000 at the end of Year 1 and $30,000 at
the end of Year 2. Using discount factors of 0.909 (Year 1) and 0.826 (Year 2) at a 10% discount rate, what is
the NPV?
A. $2,050 ✔
B. $52,050
C. $10,000
D. −$2,050
*Rationale:* `(30,000 × 0.909) + (30,000 × 0.826) = 27,270 + 24,780 = 52,050`; `NPV = 52,050 − 50,000 =
2,050`. B forgets to deduct the initial investment; C uses undiscounted cash flows; D has the right magnitude
but the wrong sign.

**MCQ 8.1.3 [topic 8.1.2, level: analysis]**
Which limitation applies specifically to the simple (undiscounted) payback period as an investment-appraisal
technique?
A. It cannot be calculated for any project with more than one cash inflow
B. It ignores cash flows occurring after the payback point and, in its simple form, ignores the time value of
   money ✔
C. It always gives the same accept/reject conclusion as NPV
D. It can only be used for projects with a negative NPV
*Rationale:* B is payback's core limitation (§8.1.2). A is false — payback commonly spans several inflows; C
is false, which is exactly why the two techniques should be read together, not interchangeably; D is a
nonsensical restriction.

**MCQ 8.1.4 [topic 8.1.3, level: recall]**
What is the primary purpose of a project charter?
A. To provide a complete, detailed Work Breakdown Structure for the project
B. To formally authorise the project's existence and give the project manager documented authority over
   resources ✔
C. To replace the need for a stakeholder register
D. To record the project's final lessons learned
*Rationale:* B is the charter's defining purpose (§8.1.3). A describes a Planning output (KA 8.2); C
confuses two distinct Initiating outputs; D describes a Closing activity (KA 8.5).

**MCQ 8.1.5 [topic 8.1.5, level: application]**
A stakeholder holds significant authority over the project (for example, the power to halt site access) but
has shown little day-to-day interest in the project's detail. On the power–interest grid, this stakeholder
should be classified as:
A. Manage Closely
B. Keep Satisfied ✔
C. Keep Informed
D. Monitor
*Rationale:* High power combined with low interest is the defining condition for Keep Satisfied (§8.1.5). A
requires high interest as well as high power; C and D both require low power, which this stakeholder does not
have.

**MCQ 8.1.6 [topic 8.1.7, level: application — numerical]**
A Class 5 estimate of $500,000 (accuracy range −30%/+50%) is compared against an indicative budget ceiling of
$600,000. What is the high-bound exceedance, if any, against the ceiling?
A. The high bound ($750,000) exceeds the ceiling by $150,000 ✔
B. The high bound exactly matches the ceiling
C. The point estimate exceeds the ceiling
D. There is no way to calculate a high bound without a Class 1 estimate
*Rationale:* `High bound = 500,000 × 1.50 = 750,000`; `750,000 − 600,000 = 150,000` over the ceiling
(§8.1.7). B and C misstate the arithmetic; D is false — the AACE-stated range applies directly to a Class 5
estimate, which is the entire point of the classification system (KA 3.2.1).

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Explain, in your own words, why PMI's PMBOK Guide process groups are described as overlapping and
   recurring rather than as strictly sequential phases, and why this matters for how Monitoring & Controlling
   should be understood.
2. Using the cash flows from Example 8.1.2 (Year 0: −$150,000; Years 1–2: $60,000; Year 3: $90,000), recalculate
   the NPV if Meridian's cost of capital is revised to 12% (discount factors: 0.893, 0.797, 0.712). Is the
   investment still worthwhile?
3. Explain what a stakeholder register captures that a power–interest grid does not, and what the grid adds
   that the register alone cannot show.
4. Explain why a "go" decision at Gate 1 should not be treated as irreversible, and what purpose later phase
   gates (such as Gate 2) serve as a result.

**Answers**

1. PMBOK's process groups describe *types of management activity*, not calendar phases: a project does not
   complete all its Planning before any Executing begins, and Monitoring & Controlling is not a late-stage
   activity but a continuous discipline running alongside Executing throughout delivery, comparing actual
   performance to the baseline and triggering corrective action or replanning as needed (§8.1.1).
2. `PV = (60,000 × 0.893) + (60,000 × 0.797) + (90,000 × 0.712) = 53,580 + 47,820 + 64,080 = 165,480`;
   `NPV = 165,480 − 150,000 = +15,480`. The investment remains worthwhile — NPV is still positive at the
   higher 12% rate, though the margin has narrowed from +$21,690 at 10%.
3. The stakeholder register captures *who* the stakeholders are and *what* their roles, interests and
   expectations are (§8.1.4); the power–interest grid adds a *differentiated engagement strategy* by
   classifying each one's relative influence and level of concern, telling the project team how much and what
   kind of attention each stakeholder actually needs (§8.1.5) — information the register alone, as a flat
   list, does not organise or prioritise.
4. Later information — from Planning's more mature estimate, from stakeholder feedback, from emerging risk —
   can genuinely change the picture that supported the original "go" decision; treating that decision as fixed
   regardless of new evidence defeats the purpose of having multiple gates. Gate 2 and later gates exist
   specifically so the sponsor can re-confirm, adjust, or halt the project on the strength of current
   information, as the §8.1.8 mini-case shows for Pipeline Segment 4B's revised ground-condition information.
