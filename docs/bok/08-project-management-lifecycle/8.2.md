# Domain 8 — Project Management Lifecycle

## KA 8.2 — Planning

Every calculation the rest of this book teaches — earned value in Domain 6, variance bridges in Domain 4,
schedule float in Domain 10, risk exposure in Domain 12 — is a comparison against something. That something
is built here. The **Planning process group**, as PMI's PMBOK Guide sets it out, is where an approved
project charter (KA 8.1) becomes an integrated **project management plan**: a scope statement and Work
Breakdown Structure (WBS), a schedule, a budget, and a family of subsidiary plans for quality, resource,
communications, risk, procurement and stakeholder engagement — all built from the *same* decomposed scope,
so that when they are frozen they form one consistent set of baselines, not several disconnected documents
that happen to share a project name. This KA is deliberately an **integrator**: the detailed mechanics of
network scheduling belong to Domain 10, cost estimating and time-phasing to Domains 3 and 5, and risk
identification and quantification to Domain 12 — each is cross-referenced here, not repeated. What belongs
here, and nowhere else in this book, is *how those outputs are assembled into one plan* and *why a baseline
built in silos fails the moment it is used for control*. The running illustration continues **Meridian Rail
Contracting LLC** (KA 1.1) and its **Pipeline Segment 4B** control account (BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at
an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**, first opened in KA 6.1), now viewed at the point, early in the
project, where its scope, schedule and cost baselines are first assembled — the same baselines every later
domain in this book measures performance against.

### 8.2.1 The Planning process group: purpose, principles and progressive elaboration

**Definition & purpose.** The **Planning process group**, in PMBOK's process-group model, comprises the
processes that establish the total scope of the project, define and refine objectives, and develop the
course of action required to attain them. It follows **Initiating** (KA 8.1), where a charter authorises the
project and names its sponsor, and it precedes — but does not fully finish before — **Executing** and
**Monitoring & Controlling** (KA 8.3, KA 8.4), which use the plan built here as their reference point.
AACE International's Total Cost Management (TCM) Framework treats planning and budgeting as a defined
process stage sitting between strategic asset planning and project control, producing exactly the same
family of outputs — scope, schedule, budget, risk register — under different labels.

**The underlying principle — progressive elaboration.** A project charter typically authorises a single
top-down figure (Pipeline Segment 4B's $600,000 BAC) and a target completion, not a fully engineered plan.
Planning is the process of **progressively elaborating** that authorisation into increasing levels of
detail as more becomes known — the same discipline KA 5.3.2 and KA 5.3.4 develop fully for a single control
account (planning packages maturing into work packages via rolling wave planning) now applied to the whole
project's scope, schedule and cost simultaneously. Planning is not, therefore, a single phase completed once
and never revisited: PMBOK is explicit that planning continues, in controlled form, throughout the project
via integrated change control (KA 8.4) whenever an approved change requires the baseline itself to move.

**Formula.** No numerical formula attaches to the process group itself; its output is tested against the
same reconciliation discipline used throughout this KA: every subsidiary plan and baseline must trace back
to, and reconcile with, the same approved WBS.

**Example 8.2.1 — Iteration in practice: how a single identified risk revises three plans at once.**

*Scenario.* During Pipeline Segment 4B's planning window, Meridian's estimating team surveys the trenching
corridor and identifies an unmapped utility crossing at approximately the 1,100 m mark (first flagged in
KA 3.2.6 and KA 5.3.2's mini-case) — a genuine planning-stage discovery, not a mid-execution surprise.

*Required.* Trace which planning outputs this single discovery must revise before the baseline is frozen.

*Working/Answer.* The discovery touches three plans simultaneously: the **WBS** (8.2.4), because WP3
Trenching & Earthworks may need an additional avoidance-detail work package; the **schedule** (cross-ref
KA 10.1), because the trenching activity's sequencing around the crossing changes; and the **risk register**
(8.2.8, cross-ref KA 12.2), because the crossing is now a documented, assessed risk rather than an unknown.
Crucially, KA 5.3.2's mini-case already showed the *cost-side* consequence of exactly this discovery: WP3 was
deliberately left as a single planning package, not decomposed too early, precisely so that this survey
finding could be absorbed without wasted rework.

*Interpretation.* One planning-stage discovery revised three separate outputs coherently *because* they were
all built from, and continuously reconciled against, the same WBS element (WP3) — the opposite of planning
in silos (8.2.9's central theme). Progressive elaboration is not a licence to plan vaguely; it is a
discipline for absorbing genuinely new information without corrupting what has already been baselined
elsewhere.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating Planning as a one-off phase completed entirely before execution begins, rather than a
  progressively elaborated activity that continues, formally, throughout the project (cross-ref KA 8.4).
- Freezing a baseline before genuinely necessary information is available, forcing premature detail exactly
  as KA 5.3.2 and KA 5.3.4 describe for a single control account, now at whole-project scale.
- Moving straight from charter to detailed schedule and cost estimates without first securing an approved
  scope statement and WBS, leaving schedule and cost activities with no common, agreed scope to decompose.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI tools can draft an initial planning-activity outline and a first-cut project
management plan structure from a charter and a library of comparable past projects (cross-reference KA 13.4,
KA 13.5), a genuine drafting accelerant at the start of planning. Such a tool cannot decide the genuine
sequencing or interdependency of planning activities for *this* project, or judge when enough is known to
freeze a baseline — that remains the planning team's professional judgement, echoing the identical caution
KA 5.3.2 and KA 5.3.4 already raised about decomposition timing at control-account level.

**Cross-references.** KA 8.1 (Initiating — the charter this KA elaborates); KA 8.3–8.5 (the process groups
this plan feeds); KA 8.4 (integrated change control, re-planning); KA 5.3.2, KA 5.3.4 (progressive
elaboration and rolling wave planning, developed fully at control-account level); KA 3.2.6, KA 5.3.2 (the
utility-crossing risk reused above); KA 8.6 (development approaches — this KA treats Planning as practised
in predictive/plan-driven delivery; adaptive and hybrid tailoring is covered there).

### 8.2.2 The project management plan and its subsidiary plans

**Definition & purpose.** The **project management plan** is the single, formally approved document that
describes how the project will be executed, monitored, controlled and closed. PMBOK is explicit that it is
not one document but an *integrating* one, assembled from a set of **subsidiary (component) management
plans** — one per knowledge area — and three **baselines**: the scope baseline, the schedule baseline and
the cost baseline. Together, the schedule and cost baselines, integrated onto the approved scope, form the
**Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB)** — defined in full in KA 3.3.5 and KA 6.4.1, and returned to as
this KA's capstone in 8.2.9.

**The underlying principle.** A project management plan is only genuinely a *plan*, rather than a folder of
separately written documents, if every subsidiary plan and baseline is built from — and can be traced back
to — the same approved WBS (8.2.4). This is the structural safeguard against the single biggest failure mode
in planning: producing documents that each read well in isolation but do not actually agree with one another
about what the project is, when it happens, or what it costs (developed fully in 8.2.9).

**Structure — Pipeline Segment 4B's project management plan components.**

| Component | What it defines | Developed in |
|---|---|---|
| Scope management plan / scope baseline | Scope statement, WBS, WBS dictionary | 8.2.3, 8.2.4 |
| Schedule management plan / schedule baseline | Activity network, durations, milestones | 8.2.5 (cross-ref KA 10.1) |
| Cost management plan / cost baseline (PMB) | Time-phased budget, contingency | 8.2.5 (cross-ref KA 3.1–3.3, KA 5.3) |
| Quality management plan | Quality standards, cost of quality | 8.2.6 |
| Resource management plan | RAM/OBS, roles and responsibilities | 8.2.6 (cross-ref KA 5.3.3) |
| Communications management plan | Reporting cadence, channels, audience | 8.2.6 (cross-ref KA 4.3) |
| Procurement management plan | Make-or-buy, contract strategy | 8.2.7 (cross-ref Domain 7) |
| Stakeholder engagement plan | Engagement strategy by stakeholder | 8.2.7 |
| Risk management plan / risk register (initiated) | Methodology, categories, thresholds | 8.2.8 (cross-ref Domain 12) |

**Example 8.2.2 — Assembling the plan index and confirming it is complete.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's project controls lead is asked, ahead of the baseline review, to confirm every
subsidiary plan required for a contract of Pipeline Segment 4B's size and risk profile exists in draft.

*Required.* Check the plan index above against PMBOK's nine subsidiary-plan areas and identify any gap.

*Working.* Cross-checking the nine rows in the table above against the nine knowledge areas: all nine are
present, each with a named "developed in" reference within this KA or a cross-referenced domain.

*Answer.* **The plan index is complete: nine subsidiary plans/baselines, no gaps.**

*Interpretation.* Completeness here means *coverage*, not depth — PMBOK's own tailoring guidance permits a
lighter-weight version of any subsidiary plan for a project of Pipeline Segment 4B's scale (a one-page
communications plan is entirely appropriate for a six-month, single-control-account package), but an
*absent* plan is a different failure from a *thin* one, and only the completeness check above catches the
former.

**Mini-case — the missing subsidiary plan.** Suppose Meridian's baseline review had skipped the risk
management plan entirely, reasoning that "the risks are obvious for a project this size." Six weeks into
execution, the unmapped utility crossing (8.2.1) is confirmed on site. Without a documented risk management
plan defining thresholds, an assessment methodology and named ownership (cross-ref KA 12.1), there is no
agreed process for deciding whether the resulting delay is significant enough to draw down contingency
(cross-ref KA 3.1.6, KA 12.3) or who is authorised to approve that draw-down — the *technical* discovery is
handled competently (KA 5.3.2 already showed WP3 absorbing it cleanly), but the *governance* response is
improvised under time pressure instead of following a plan agreed in advance.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Producing subsidiary plans as disconnected documents, each internally consistent but never checked against

- Treating the project management plan as a static, one-time deliverable rather than a document that is
  itself subject to formal change control once baselined (cross-ref KA 5.4, KA 8.4).
- Omitting a subsidiary plan altogether because the relevant knowledge area seems self-evident for a small
  project — tailoring permits a lighter-weight plan, never an absent one (the mini-case above).

**AI in this topic.** GenAI and document-drafting tools can assemble a first-draft project management plan
and its subsidiary plans from a template library and the approved charter (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.4),

mutually consistent — for example, that the risk management plan's stated response strategies are actually
reflected in the schedule and cost baselines it is meant to protect — and an unreviewed AI-assembled plan can
look complete while hiding exactly the kind of gap the mini-case above describes.

### 8.2.3 Requirements, the scope statement, assumptions and constraints

**Definition & purpose.** **Requirements collection** is the process of determining, documenting and
managing stakeholder needs to meet project objectives; each requirement is recorded and tracked in a
**requirements traceability matrix**, linking it forward to the deliverable that satisfies it and, later, to
the acceptance test that verifies it. The **project scope statement** then consolidates the agreed scope into
a single reference document: a description of the deliverables and the work required to produce them, product
acceptance criteria, explicit scope exclusions, and — the two elements most often under-documented —
**assumptions** (statements accepted as true for planning purposes, without proof) and **constraints**
(limiting factors that affect how the project can be executed).

**The underlying principle.** A requirement that cannot be traced to a specific deliverable is not
controllable — nobody can confirm whether it has been met. An assumption that is never written down cannot
later be tested, revisited, or blamed when it turns out to be false; a documented assumption, by contrast,
becomes a specific, assignable item to monitor, and its failure becomes a traceable cause of a later variance
rather than a mystery. This is the scope-side equivalent of the discipline KA 6.1's mini-cases apply
repeatedly to cost and schedule data: an unstated basis produces numbers that look precise but cannot be
defended.

**Formula.** None; a requirements traceability matrix is a structured table, not a calculation. Its
completeness is checked the same way the WBS's 100% rule is checked (8.2.4): every approved requirement must
map to at least one WBS element, and every WBS element should trace back to at least one requirement or
contractual obligation.

**Example 8.2.3 — A requirements traceability matrix extract for Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* Three of the client's contractual requirements for Pipeline Segment 4B, drawn from the rail
authority's technical specification:

| Req. ID | Requirement | Traced to WBS element | Acceptance criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| R-01 | Pipeline route must maintain minimum 2.0 m clearance from the live rail corridor | WP1 Design & Engineering | Design drawing shows clearance ≥ 2.0 m at every chainage |
| R-02 | All welds must pass non-destructive testing (NDT) before backfill | WP4 Pipe-laying & Welding (Weld/Test milestones) | 100% of welds NDT-certified, logged against weld ID |
| R-03 | Trenching must not proceed within 5 m of an unmapped utility without a hand-dig method statement | WP3 Trenching & Earthworks | Method statement approved before any trenching within 5 m of a flagged utility |

*Required.* Confirm each requirement has both a WBS owner and a verifiable acceptance criterion.

*Working/Answer.* All three rows are complete: each requirement traces to exactly one WBS element (8.2.4)
and carries a criterion that can be objectively checked, not merely asserted.

*Interpretation.* R-03 is the direct planning-stage descendant of the utility-crossing discovery already
introduced in 8.2.1 — a requirement did not exist for this scenario until the risk was identified, at which
point requirements collection, the WBS and the risk register were all updated together, exactly the
integrated response 8.2.1's worked example described in the abstract.

**Mini-case — an unvalidated assumption.** Pipeline Segment 4B's original schedule assumption, recorded in
the scope statement, states: "way-leave access to the full 2,000 m corridor will be granted by the local
regulator before trenching begins" (cross-ref KA 10.1's Activity B, Regulatory approval & way-leave). This is
a genuine assumption, not a certainty — the regulator's approval is outside Meridian's control. Because it
was documented, rather than left implicit, the risk management plan (8.2.8) can carry it forward as a named
risk with an owner and a monitoring trigger; had it been left unstated, a delay to way-leave approval would
surface only as an unexplained schedule slip, with no documented basis for anyone to have anticipated or
escalated it.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Writing requirements as vague statements of intent rather than verifiable, testable criteria traceable to
  a specific deliverable.
- Omitting assumptions and constraints from the scope statement, so a later decision "because we assumed X"
  has no documented basis to be revisited once X is tested.
- Confusing a requirement (what the deliverable must do) with a design solution (how it will be done),
  closing off design options before the requirement itself has been validated.

**AI in this topic.** NLP-based extraction tools can mine a contract or a stakeholder-interview transcript
for candidate requirements and populate a first-draft traceability matrix (cross-reference KA 13.4), speeding
up an often tedious manual task. Such tools cannot confirm a requirement is complete, unambiguous, or
genuinely traceable to an approved deliverable — a poorly worded or entirely missed requirement looks
identical to a well-formed one to an automated extraction pass until a professional reviews it against the
actual scope.

### 8.2.4 The Work Breakdown Structure: decomposition, the 100% rule and the scope baseline

**Definition & purpose.** The **Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)** is the deliverable-oriented, hierarchical
decomposition of the total scope of work, already defined in full — including the **100% rule** and its
mapping to cost codes — in KA 1.5.3. This topic does not repeat that mechanical treatment; it shows the WBS
as a **Planning output**: the artefact that, once combined with the WBS dictionary and the scope statement
(8.2.3), becomes the **scope baseline** — the approved reference against which schedule (8.2.5), cost (8.2.5)
and every subsidiary plan (8.2.6–8.2.8) are built, and the sole authority for what counts as an in-scope
change (cross-ref KA 5.4, KA 8.4).

**The underlying principle.** The 100% rule — child elements must, together, represent all of a parent's
scope, no more and no less (KA 1.5.3) — is not merely a scope-documentation nicety. It is the reason a WBS
can serve as the *single* structure that schedule, cost, quality and risk all decompose against: if the WBS
itself has a gap or an overlap, every plan built from it inherits the same defect, invisibly, because nothing
downstream re-checks scope completeness — each downstream plan simply trusts the WBS it was given.

**Formula.** No numerical formula attaches to decomposition itself; the 100% rule is checked, at every level,
as: `Σ (child element budget/scope) = parent element budget/scope` (no gap, no overlap) — applied here to
Pipeline Segment 4B's own two-level decomposition.

**Example 8.2.4 — Pipeline Segment 4B's WBS: two levels of decomposition, both satisfying the 100% rule.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B (BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000) decomposes at Level 1 into its five
established work packages (KA 6.1.4, KA 5.3.1), and WP4 Pipe-laying & Welding decomposes one level further,
at Level 2, into its four weighted-milestone components (KA 6.1.4).

*Required.* Confirm both levels of decomposition independently satisfy the 100% rule.

*Working.*
Level 1: `WP1 80,000 + WP2 150,000 + WP3 200,000 + WP4 120,000 + WP5 50,000 = 600,000`
Level 2 (WP4 only): `Prep 12,000 + Lay 48,000 + Weld 36,000 + Test 24,000 = 120,000`

*Answer.* **Level 1 sums exactly to the $600,000 BAC; Level 2 (WP4's own decomposition) sums exactly to
WP4's $120,000 budget.** The 100% rule holds independently at each level.

*Interpretation.* The 100% rule is not a single, one-time check performed once at the bottom of the WBS — it
must hold at *every* parent–child boundary, checked separately each time a level is decomposed further
(exactly as KA 5.3.2's planning-package-to-work-package example demonstrated for WP4 alone; this topic places
that same check inside the whole-project WBS it belongs to). A defect introduced at Level 1 would silently
corrupt every Level 2 breakdown beneath it, however cleanly that lower level itself reconciles.

**Mini-case — one level further, reusing an already-established split.** KA 10.1.1 separately decomposed WP2
Pipe & Valve Procurement ($150,000) into two schedule activities — C1 Pipe & valve fabrication ($90,000) and
C2 Pipe & valve transport & delivery ($60,000) — confirming `90,000 + 60,000 = 150,000`. That decomposition
is not repeated here; it is cited to make a structural point: a WBS element can be decomposed *for different
purposes* (WP4's milestones, for earned-value measurement; WP2's C1/C2 split, for scheduling — developed in
8.2.5) at different points in this book, and the 100% rule applies identically each time, regardless of why
the decomposition was performed.

Frame 2: five child boxes drop in beneath it, each labelled, summing on screen to "$600,000 ✓." Frame 3: WP4

**Common pitfalls.**
- Decomposing to the work-package level and stopping, without checking every branch actually sums to its
  parent — the 100% rule, applied here one level up from KA 10.1.1's activity-level treatment.
- Building the WBS around the organisation chart (who does the work) rather than deliverables (what work
  produces) — PMBOK is explicit the two are different structures (cross-ref KA 1.5.3, KA 1.5.4).
- Decomposing every branch to the same fine level of detail regardless of value or risk, inflating planning
  effort without a control benefit (cross-ref KA 5.3.2, KA 5.3.4's progressive-elaboration treatment of this
  exact trade-off).

**AI in this topic.** GenAI tools can propose a first-cut WBS decomposition from a scope narrative, pattern-
matched against a library of comparable past projects (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5, echoing KA 1.5.3
and KA 5.3.2's identical caution). Such a tool cannot itself confirm the 100% rule holds for *this* project's
true scope, nor judge whether a proposed decomposition basis (by deliverable, by discipline, by geography) is
the right one for the work at hand — only the professional planning team knows whether the scope itself is
complete.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.5.3 (the WBS, 100% rule and cost-code mapping in full); KA 5.3.1–5.3.2 (the CBS
and planning-package/work-package distinction); KA 6.1.4 (WP1–WP5's budgets and EV methods, reused here);
KA 10.1.1 (WP2's C1/C2 schedule decomposition, cited not repeated); KA 8.2.5 (the WBS becoming the schedule
and cost baseline).

### 8.2.5 From the WBS to the schedule and cost baselines

**Definition & purpose.** **Schedule planning** sequences the WBS's work packages into activities with
logic and durations, producing the schedule baseline (developed in full in KA 10.1). **Cost/budget
planning** estimates and time-phases the cost of that same scope, producing the cost baseline/PMB (developed
in full in KA 3.1–3.3, and structured into control accounts in KA 5.3). This topic's job is narrower than
either: to show, with one worked example, that the WBS, the schedule and the cost baseline are three views
of the *same* decomposition — not three separately derived documents that happen to agree.

**The underlying principle.** A cost baseline that is not genuinely built *from* the schedule is not
integrated, however precisely it is calculated — KA 6.4.1 demonstrates this numerically at project scale (a
naive, non-schedule-integrated PV spread overstates WP3's Month-2 planned value by $16,667 against the
schedule-integrated figure). This topic shows the same principle at work-package scale, using a
decomposition already established elsewhere in this book, so the mechanism is visible without re-deriving it.

**Formula.** No new formula; time-phasing itself is developed fully in KA 3.3.2 (uniform, weighted and
S-curve spreading methods). This topic reuses that logic once: `Period budget (schedule-integrated) =
Σ (budget of WBS sub-elements whose scheduled activity falls in that period)`.

**Example 8.2.5 — WP2 Pipe & Valve Procurement: the same $150,000, three ways.**

*Scenario.* WP2 (8.2.4, $150,000) is scheduled (cross-ref KA 10.1.1) as two sequential, week-based schedule
activities: C1 Pipe & valve fabrication (3 weeks, $90,000) then C2 Pipe & valve transport & delivery
(3 weeks, $60,000).

*Required.* Show that the WBS decomposition, the schedule decomposition and the time-phased cost baseline
for WP2 are the same split, and quantify the effect of *not* integrating them (a naive uniform spread across
WP2's own 6-week duration instead).

*Working.*
Scope view (WBS, 8.2.4): `C1 90,000 + C2 60,000 = 150,000` ✓
Schedule-integrated cost view: budget recognised in the cost baseline as each activity's scheduled window
opens — `90,000` due by the end of week 3 (C1 complete), a further `60,000` by the end of week 6 (C2
complete).
Naive (non-integrated) view: `150,000 / 6 weeks = 25,000/week`; cumulative by end of week 3 =
`3 × 25,000 = 75,000`.

*Answer.* **Schedule-integrated cumulative budget at week 3 = $90,000; naive uniform spread at week 3 =
$75,000 — a $15,000 understatement.**

*Interpretation.* The naive spread assumes fabrication is only half-recognised by the point the schedule
says it is *entirely* complete, purely because it ignores the schedule logic already fixed in KA 10.1.1. This
worked figure illustrates the same mechanism KA 6.4.1's whole-project example demonstrates ($16,667
overstatement there, versus a $15,000 understatement here — the direction depends on whether the naive
spread front- or back-loads relative to the true schedule, not on the size of the error, which can run either
way). *This work-package-level illustration uses KA 10.1.1's own week-based activity durations purely to show
the mechanism; the project's month-level reporting cadence, used for whole-project PV in KA 6.1.2, is a
separate, coarser view of the same underlying schedule.*

**Mini-case — why the split is defined once, not three times.** Suppose Meridian's cost engineer, unaware
that the schedule team had already fixed WP2's C1/C2 split at $90,000/$60,000, independently re-estimated a
different 55%/45% split for cost-baseline purposes based on an older supplier quotation. The WBS (8.2.4), the
schedule (KA 10.1.1) and the cost baseline would then each state a *different* number for the "same" WP2
decomposition — none individually wrong in isolation, but mutually inconsistent, and reconcilable only by
someone noticing the mismatch. This is why 8.2.4's mini-case insisted the split is defined once and reused:
duplicating a decomposition invites exactly this kind of silent drift.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Time-phasing a work package's budget independently of its actual schedule position — the naive spread

- Treating the schedule and cost baselines as separately owned artefacts, reconciled only at reporting time

- Re-deriving a WBS element's schedule/cost split independently in more than one place, so a later change to
  one copy is never propagated to the other (the mini-case above).

correct. Such automation cannot judge whether that underlying link is correct; an unreviewed automated

would have.

### 8.2.6 Quality, resource and communications planning

**Definition & purpose.** Three further subsidiary plans are established during Planning, each answering a
different question about the same WBS: the **quality management plan** (what standard of work is acceptable,
and at what cost of achieving it), the **resource management plan** (who is accountable for delivering each
WBS element — the Organisational Breakdown Structure, OBS, and Responsibility Assignment Matrix, RAM, first
defined in KA 1.5.4), and the **communications management plan** (who needs what information, how often, and
through which channel). This is where Pipeline Segment 4B's RAM is **first established** as a planning
output — KA 5.3.3 later develops it into full Control Account Manager (CAM) accountability and a formal RACI
matrix once execution approaches.

**The underlying principle — cost of quality.** PMBOK's **cost of quality (COQ)** framework separates quality
spend into **conformance cost** — **prevention** (avoiding defects before they occur) and **appraisal**
(inspecting for defects) — and **non-conformance cost** — **internal failure** (rework before handover) and
**external failure** (defects found after handover, typically far more expensive to remedy). The underlying
principle for resource planning is single-point accountability: a RAM gives one named owner per WBS element,
without which no one individual can be asked to explain a variance (cross-ref KA 5.3.3's identical principle).
For communications, the underlying principle is that reporting effort must be tailored to audience need
(cross-ref KA 4.3) — an "everyone gets everything" plan becomes unmanageable as team size grows, which the
formula below makes explicit.

**Formula(e).**
Communication channels: `Channels = n(n − 1) / 2`, where *n* = the number of identified communicators.
Cost of quality: `Conformance cost = Prevention + Appraisal`; `Non-conformance cost = Internal failure +
External failure`; `Total COQ = Conformance cost + Non-conformance cost`.

**Example 8.2.6a — Pipeline Segment 4B's initial RAM.**

*Scenario/Required.* At Planning, before Domain 5's fuller control-account structure exists, Meridian assigns
one accountable owner per work package.

*Working/Answer.*

| WBS element | Owner (OBS role) |
|---|---|
| WP1 Design & Engineering | Head of Engineering |
| WP2 Pipe & Valve Procurement | Procurement Manager |
| WP3 Trenching & Earthworks | Construction Manager |
| WP4 Pipe-laying & Welding | Construction Manager |
| WP5 Site Supervision | Site/Project Manager |
| Cross-cutting QA/HSE oversight | QA/HSE Manager |

*Interpretation.* This is deliberately coarser than KA 5.3.3's six named control accounts (CA-01–CA-06,
each with its own CAM and RACI matrix) — Planning establishes *who is accountable for what scope*; Domain 5
later refines that into named control accounts with a formal RACI matrix once rolling wave planning
(KA 5.3.4) brings each account to full work-package detail.

**Example 8.2.6b — Cost of quality: allocating CA-06's QA/HSE budget.**

*Scenario.* CA-06, the cross-cutting QA/HSE control account (KA 5.3.3, budget $18,000), is allocated across
the four COQ categories at the planning stage: Prevention (training, procedure development) $4,000;
Appraisal (weld NDT, trench inspection) $9,000; Internal failure (pre-handover rework) $4,000; External
failure (post-handover defect cost) $1,000.

*Required.* Conformance and non-conformance cost, each as a percentage of the $18,000 total.

*Working.*
`Conformance = 4,000 + 9,000 = 13,000`; `13,000 / 18,000 × 100 = 72.2%`
`Non-conformance = 4,000 + 1,000 = 5,000`; `5,000 / 18,000 × 100 = 27.8%`
Check: `13,000 + 5,000 = 18,000` ✓

*Answer.* **Conformance cost = $13,000 (72.2%); non-conformance cost = $5,000 (27.8%).**

*Interpretation.* A conformance-heavy split is generally the sign of a well-planned quality budget: money
spent preventing and catching defects before handover is cheaper, on average, than money spent fixing them
afterwards — the underlying justification for prevention spend, even though it produces no directly visible
"output" the way an inspection report does.

**Example 8.2.6c — Communication channels as the team scales.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's core team of six named owners (Example 8.2.6a's six roles) is compared with
a wider group of nine once the client's rail authority representative and the local regulator are added as
active planning participants.

*Required.* Channels at each team size.

*Working.* `Channels(6) = 6 × 5 / 2 = 15`; `Channels(9) = 9 × 8 / 2 = 36`

*Answer.* **15 channels at 6 people; 36 channels at 9 people** — a 50% increase in people more than doubles
the channels.

*Interpretation.* This is the quantitative justification for tailoring the communications plan by audience
(cross-ref KA 4.3) rather than broadcasting every update to everyone: channels grow quadratically with team
size, so an undifferentiated communications plan becomes unmanageable long before the team itself feels
large.

- Assigning a quality, resource or communications plan without reference to the WBS/RAM it is meant to

### 8.2.7 Procurement and stakeholder engagement planning

**Definition & purpose.** **Procurement planning** determines what will be bought rather than built
in-house, and how — the **make-or-buy analysis** — before the contract-type and commercial mechanics
developed in Domain 7 are applied. **Stakeholder engagement planning** builds on stakeholder identification
(cross-ref KA 8.1) to define, for each identified stakeholder, their current and desired level of engagement
and the strategy for closing that gap — commonly visualised on a **power/interest grid**.

**The underlying principle.** A make-or-buy decision should reflect the project's *actual* usage duration
and risk, not a generic preference for owning assets; a **breakeven analysis** makes this explicit rather
than leaving it to intuition. Stakeholder engagement planning rests on the principle that influence and
interest are not static or uniform across a stakeholder group — treating every stakeholder identically wastes
effort on those who need little engagement and under-serves those whose support is critical.

**Formula(e).**
Make-or-buy breakeven: `Breakeven duration = (Purchase price − Residual value) / Lease rate per period`.
For a required usage duration *t*: buying is cheaper if `t > Breakeven duration`; leasing is cheaper if
`t < Breakeven duration`.

**Example 8.2.7a — Make-or-buy: the trenching excavator.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's estimating team assesses that WP3 Trenching & Earthworks requires exclusive use of one
excavator for an estimated 3 months of the programme. Purchase price is $60,000 (SAR 225,000), with an
expected $12,000 residual (resale) value at project completion; lease cost is $4,000 per month.

*Required.* The breakeven duration, and the cheaper option for a 3-month need.

*Working.*
`Net purchase cost = 60,000 − 12,000 = 48,000`
`Breakeven duration = 48,000 / 4,000 = 12 months`
`Lease cost for the actual 3-month need = 3 × 4,000 = 12,000`

*Answer.* **Breakeven = 12 months; for the 3-month need, leasing ($12,000) is far cheaper than buying (a net
$48,000 cost), saving $36,000.**

*Interpretation.* Because the required duration (3 months) is well below the 12-month breakeven, leasing
dominates decisively, not marginally — a useful illustration of why the breakeven calculation, not a blanket
buy or lease policy, should drive the decision. Had Pipeline Segment 4B instead been the first of several
successive pipeline packages needing the same excavator for 15 months in total, buying would have become the
cheaper option.

**Example 8.2.7b — Stakeholder power/interest grid.**

*Scenario.* Four stakeholder groups relevant to Pipeline Segment 4B's planning: the client's rail authority
(the contracting party); the local regulator (way-leave approval, KA 10.1's Activity B); landowners along the
trenching corridor (way-leave grantors); the welding-rig and crane-hire subcontractor (KA 5.3.5's $33,600
invoice).

*Required.* Classify each on power and interest, and the resulting engagement strategy.

*Working/Answer.*

| Stakeholder | Power | Interest | Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Client (rail authority) | High | High | Manage closely — frequent, detailed reporting |
| Local regulator | High | Medium | Keep satisfied — proactive, milestone-driven updates |
| Corridor landowners | Low | High | Keep informed — regular, accessible communication |
| Crane-hire subcontractor | Medium | Medium | Monitor and coordinate — schedule-linked communication |

*Interpretation.* The local regulator's classification is not fixed: if way-leave approval (the assumption
flagged in 8.2.3) is delayed, its interest rises sharply and its engagement strategy should shift toward
"manage closely" without waiting for a scheduled review point to notice.

  while positions can still be shaped proactively.

stakeholder-analytics tools can propose a first-draft power/interest classification from organisational data.
Neither can verify that a specific stakeholder's actual influence, or a specific supplier's actual
reliability, matches what the data suggests — both require direct professional verification before the plan
is finalised.

**Cross-references.** KA 7.1 (contract types, the commercial mechanics procurement planning selects between);

### 8.2.8 Risk planning: from the risk management plan to the initial risk register

**Definition & purpose.** **Risk planning** produces the **risk management plan** — the methodology,
categories, roles, timing and thresholds that will govern how risk is identified, analysed and responded to
(developed in full in KA 12.1–12.3) — and, during Planning, populates an **initial risk register** with
risks identified while scope, schedule and cost are first being decomposed. This topic's job is narrow: it
shows the risk register as a Planning *output*, drawing together risks already surfaced elsewhere in this KA;
the full identification, qualitative/quantitative analysis and response-planning process belongs to Domain
12 and is not repeated here.

**The underlying principle.** Risks identified during Planning are rarely discovered by a single, dedicated
"risk workshop" — they surface naturally while decomposing scope (8.2.1's utility crossing), documenting
assumptions (8.2.3's way-leave assumption) and assigning accountability (8.2.6). A **Risk Breakdown
Structure (RBS)** organises risks by category rather than by the order they happened to be discovered,
supporting pattern recognition across risks that share an underlying cause even when they surfaced in
different planning activities.

**Formula.** None; risk categorisation is structural, not numerical. Qualitative and quantitative risk
analysis, and the link from risk to contingency reserve, are developed fully in KA 12.2 and KA 12.3.

**Example 8.2.8 — Building Pipeline Segment 4B's initial RBS from planning-stage discoveries.**

*Scenario.* Three risks have already surfaced in this KA's worked examples, each during a different planning
activity, plus one drawn from KA 12.1's own worked illustration.

*Required.* Organise all four under RBS categories.

*Working/Answer.*

| RBS category | Risk | Surfaced during | Cross-ref |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical | Unmapped utility crossing near 1,100 m | WBS decomposition (8.2.1) | KA 3.2.6, KA 5.3.2 |
| External | Seasonal rainfall delaying trenching | Risk identification (awareness, cross-ref) | KA 12.1.1 |
| External | Way-leave approval delayed by the regulator | Scope statement, assumption R (8.2.3) | KA 10.1, Activity B |
| Organisational | Estimating-team resourcing gap delaying decomposition | Resource/rolling-wave planning (8.2.6, cross-ref) | KA 5.3.4's mini-case |

*Answer.* **Four risks, none newly invented, consolidated under three RBS categories (Technical, External,
Organisational) from four separate planning activities.**

*Interpretation.* This is the concrete demonstration of 8.2.1's abstract claim: Planning does not identify
risk through one isolated step, it surfaces risk continuously as scope, schedule, stakeholder and resource
plans are built — and the RBS is the structure that catches these otherwise-scattered discoveries in one
place before the baseline is frozen. KA 12.2 takes this same register forward into full qualitative and
quantitative analysis (probability, impact, expected value) and response planning; that treatment is not
repeated here.

**Mini-case — a risk category with no entries is a warning, not a clean bill of health.** Suppose Pipeline
Segment 4B's RBS, at baseline, showed zero risks under "Project management" (a standard fourth RBS category
alongside Technical, External and Organisational, covering risks such as poor estimating accuracy or unclear
scope definition). An empty category is rarely evidence that no such risk exists — it is more often evidence
that risk identification concentrated on the categories that happened to surface naturally (as in the working
table above) and never deliberately prompted the others. A risk management plan's methodology (cross-ref
KA 12.1) should require every RBS category to be actively considered, not merely populated with whatever
turned up incidentally.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating risk planning as producing a static register once, at baseline, rather than the living document
  KA 12.2 develops throughout the project.
- Sizing contingency reserve as an arbitrary percentage of BAC rather than from the risk register's own

- Leaving an RBS category empty without deliberately checking whether that reflects genuine absence of risk
  or simply incomplete identification (the mini-case above).

**AI in this topic.** GenAI tools can draft a first-cut risk register and RBS from a scope description and a
library of risks from comparable past projects (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5), a genuine time-saver at
the planning stage. They cannot know which of a comparable project's risks are actually relevant to *this*
project's specific site, contract and regulatory context — echoing KA 12.2's identical caution — and an
unreviewed AI-drafted register risks looking complete while missing the risks that matter most for this
project, or reproducing the mini-case's empty-category blind spot at scale.

**Cross-references.** KA 12.1 (the risk framework, ISO 31000, and the risk management plan in full);

already-established risks reused above).

### 8.2.9 Integrating the baselines: the Performance Measurement Baseline and the cost of planning in silos

**Definition & purpose.** This closing topic makes explicit what every earlier topic in this KA has been
building toward: once the scope baseline (8.2.4), schedule baseline (8.2.5, KA 10.1) and cost baseline
(8.2.5, KA 3.1–3.3) are each approved, their integration onto one common WBS produces the **Performance
Measurement Baseline (PMB)** — defined precisely in KA 3.3.5 and KA 6.4.1, and used, without further
derivation, by every performance-measurement technique in Domains 4 and 6 (cross-reference KA 4.1, KA 6.4.1).
Planning's job ends here: the baselines it produces are not analysed for performance in this KA — that is
KA 8.4's job — but every subsequent performance measurement in this book depends on Planning having produced
baselines that are genuinely integrated, not merely three separately approved documents sharing a project
name.

**The underlying principle.** A genuinely integrated plan lets a controls professional move freely between
three lenses on the *same* number, because all three were built from the same WBS in the first place: a
percentage of scope complete (from the WBS), a set of activities scheduled to be complete by a given date
(from the schedule baseline), and a dollar figure of budget scheduled to have been earned by that date (from
the cost baseline/PMB). **Planning in silos** — building any one of these independently of the others — is
the single failure mode this whole KA has been constructed to prevent, and it is why 8.2.1 through 8.2.8 each
insisted on tracing every output back to the same WBS rather than deriving it afresh.

**Formula.** `PMB(t) = PV(t)` (KA 6.4.1), where PV is built from schedule-integrated, WBS-derived, time-phased
budget (KA 3.3, 8.2.5). Reserve is excluded: `Total project budget = PMB + Contingency + Management reserve`
(cross-ref KA 3.1.6, KA 3.3.5) — reserve is deliberately *not* time-phased into the PMB, because it has not
yet been assigned to specific, scheduled scope.

**Example 8.2.9 — Confirming Pipeline Segment 4B's three baselines integrate to one figure.**

*Scenario.* At baseline: the scope baseline (8.2.4) totals $600,000 across five work packages; the cost
baseline/PMB (KA 6.1.2) cumulates to $600,000 by the project's final reporting period; the Month-3 status
date already established in KA 6.1.6 reports PV = $270,000.

*Required.* Confirm the scope and cost baseline totals reconcile, and interpret the Month-3 PV figure through
all three lenses at once.

*Working.*
`Scope baseline total = 80,000 + 150,000 + 200,000 + 120,000 + 50,000 = 600,000`
`Cost baseline (PMB) cumulative total = 600,000` (KA 6.1.2)
`PV(3) / BAC = 270,000 / 600,000 × 100 = 45.0%`

*Answer.* **Scope baseline total = Cost baseline (PMB) total = $600,000. At Month 3, PV = $270,000 = 45.0%
of the total.**

*Interpretation.* $270,000 is not a cost figure alone: it is simultaneously "45.0% of the WBS's total scope,"
"the schedule activities scheduled to be complete by Month 3," and "$270,000 of time-phased budget" — three
descriptions of the same fact, available without separate recalculation, precisely because Planning built all
three baselines from one WBS (cross-ref KA 4.1, KA 6.4.1).

**Mini-case — the cost of planning in silos.** Suppose Meridian's quality team had instead planned CA-06's
QA/HSE inspection budget ($18,000, 8.2.6) in isolation, spreading it evenly across all six months of the
programme regardless of when the trenching and pipe-laying work it inspects actually occurs — a plan built
without reference to the schedule-integrated WBS.

`Isolated quality plan at Month 3 = 3/6 × 18,000 = 9,000 (50.0% "planned")`

against the schedule-integrated trenching/pipe-laying progress it is meant to inspect, independently
established in KA 6.1.4 as 45.0% physically complete at the same date. **A $9,000 (50.0%) isolated quality
plan against a 45.0% schedule-integrated reality is a small but entirely avoidable mismatch** — not because
either figure is individually wrong, but because the quality plan was never built from the same schedule as
the work it is supposed to track (echoing KA 5.3.6's own mini-case, where CA-06a's real inspection progress
similarly diverged from an assumed shared rate). Multiply this mismatch across every subsidiary plan not built
from the same WBS and schedule as everything else, and a project can accumulate a whole family of baselines
that each look reasonable individually while never actually agreeing with one another — the defect this KA
exists to prevent.

**Fig 8.2.9 — Baseline integration: scope, schedule and cost converging on the PMB.**
Chart type: a three-stream convergence diagram. Three parallel input streams, each labelled ("Scope baseline
— WBS," "Schedule baseline — KA 10.1," "Cost baseline — KA 3.1–3.3"), flowing into a single confluence point
labelled "Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB), $600,000," brand blue `#1D4ED8`, with a smaller diverging

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Planning in silos** — the core failure mode of this KA: producing scope, schedule, cost and subsidiary
  plans independently, each internally coherent but never reconciled against a shared WBS and schedule, so
  the resulting "baseline" only looks integrated (the mini-case above).
- Baselining the cost baseline before the schedule it is meant to be time-phased against is itself finalised,
  forcing an un-integrated PV curve exactly as KA 6.4.1's naive-spread example demonstrates.
- Treating an approved PMB as permanent rather than subject to formal, traceable re-baselining through change
  control whenever scope, schedule or cost genuinely changes (cross-ref KA 3.3.5, KA 5.4, KA 8.4).

**AI in this topic.** Workflow and governance tools can automatically check that scope, schedule and cost
baselines reconcile to the same total whenever any one of them changes, flagging a mismatch before it reaches
a status report (cross-reference KA 13.5, KA 13.6) — a genuinely valuable integration control, directly
analogous to the mismatch this topic's mini-case describes. Such a tool can confirm the numbers reconcile; it
cannot confirm the underlying plan is realistic, or that a proposed change is genuinely warranted rather than
scope creep — that remains the accountable professional's decision throughout, consistent with **"AI
proposes; the professional disposes"** across every topic in this KA.

### Key terms

- **Planning process group** — the PMBOK process group that establishes total scope, refines objectives and
  develops the course of action to attain them, following Initiating (KA 8.1).
- **Progressive elaboration / rolling wave planning** — adding planning detail as more becomes known and as
  execution approaches (full treatment KA 5.3.2, KA 5.3.4).
- **Project management plan** — the single, approved, integrating document assembled from subsidiary plans
  and baselines.
- **Subsidiary (component) management plan** — a knowledge-area-specific plan (scope, schedule, cost,
  quality, resource, communications, procurement, risk, stakeholder) within the project management plan.
- **Requirements traceability matrix** — the structured record linking each requirement to its deliverable
  and acceptance criterion.
- **Project scope statement** — the consolidated record of deliverables, acceptance criteria, exclusions,
  assumptions and constraints.
- **Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) / 100% rule** — the deliverable-oriented scope decomposition and its
  completeness rule (full treatment KA 1.5.3).
- **Scope baseline** — the approved WBS, WBS dictionary and scope statement.
- **Schedule baseline / cost baseline** — the approved, time-phased schedule and budget (full treatment
  KA 10.1, KA 3.1–3.3).
- **Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB)** — the integration of scope, schedule and cost baselines; by
  convention, BAC = PMB (KA 3.3.5, KA 6.4.1).
- **Organisational Breakdown Structure (OBS) / Responsibility Assignment Matrix (RAM)** — the accountability
  structure mapping WBS elements to named owners (first defined KA 1.5.4; full CAM/RACI maturity KA 5.3.3).
- **Cost of quality (COQ)** — conformance cost (prevention + appraisal) and non-conformance cost (internal +
  external failure).
- **Communication channels formula** — `n(n − 1) / 2`.
- **Make-or-buy analysis / breakeven duration** — `(Purchase price − Residual value) / Lease rate per
  period`.
- **Power/interest grid** — a stakeholder-classification tool for engagement strategy.
- **Risk Breakdown Structure (RBS)** — risk categories organising the risk register (full treatment
  KA 12.1–12.2).
- **Planning in silos** — building subsidiary plans and baselines independently rather than from one shared
  WBS and schedule, producing plans that look complete but do not integrate.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 8.2.1 [topic 8.2.4, level: application — numerical]**
Pipeline Segment 4B's WBS decomposes into five work packages. WP1 = $80,000, WP2 = $150,000, WP3 = $200,000
and WP5 = $50,000, against a total BAC of $600,000. What must WP4 be budgeted at for the WBS to satisfy the
100% rule?
A. $100,000
B. $110,000
C. **$120,000** ✔
D. $130,000
*Rationale:* `600,000 − (80,000 + 150,000 + 200,000 + 50,000) = 120,000`, matching WP4's established budget
(KA 6.1.4). The other options are arithmetic slips that do not reconcile to BAC.

**MCQ 8.2.2 [topic 8.2.5, level: application — numerical]**
WP2 Pipe & Valve Procurement is budgeted at $150,000 and decomposed into two schedule-linked activities: C1
Pipe & valve fabrication and C2 Pipe & valve transport & delivery. If C1 is budgeted at $90,000, what must C2
be budgeted at for the decomposition to satisfy the 100% rule?
A. $50,000
B. **$60,000** ✔
C. $70,000
D. $90,000
*Rationale:* `150,000 − 90,000 = 60,000`, matching the schedule-based split established in KA 10.1.1. D
wrongly repeats C1's figure; A and C do not reconcile to WP2's total.

**MCQ 8.2.3 [topic 8.2.6, level: application — numerical]**
Pipeline Segment 4B's core control-account team has 6 named owners. Using the communication-channels formula,
how many channels exist among this team alone?
A. 6
B. 12
C. **15** ✔
D. 30
*Rationale:* `n(n − 1)/2 = 6 × 5 / 2 = 15`. B halves the correct answer; D omits the division by 2; A simply
restates team size.

**MCQ 8.2.4 [topic 8.2.7, level: application — numerical]**
Meridian is deciding whether to buy or lease a trenching excavator needed for 3 months of Pipeline Segment
4B's programme. Purchase price is $60,000 with an expected $12,000 residual value; lease cost is $4,000 per
month. What is the make-or-buy breakeven duration, and which option is cheaper for the 3-month need?
A. Breakeven = 15 months; leasing is cheaper
B. **Breakeven = 12 months; leasing is cheaper** ✔
C. Breakeven = 12 months; buying is cheaper
D. Breakeven = 3 months; the two options cost the same
*Rationale:* Net purchase cost `= 60,000 − 12,000 = 48,000`; breakeven `= 48,000 / 4,000 = 12` months. Because
the actual need (3 months) is well below the 12-month breakeven, leasing (`3 × 4,000 = 12,000`) is far cheaper
than the $48,000 net cost of buying. C wrongly favours buying; D misstates the breakeven point.

**MCQ 8.2.5 [topic 8.2.9, level: analysis]**
Why must a project's scope baseline, schedule baseline and cost baseline (PMB) all reconcile to the same
total once integrated during Planning?
A. Because PMBOK requires all three to be measured in the same currency
B. **Because they are three views of the same approved plan — the WBS states 100% of scope, the schedule
   places that scope in time, and the cost baseline is that scope's budget time-phased against the same
   schedule — so a mismatch means one was not actually built from the others** ✔
C. Because only the cost baseline is ever formally approved; the other two remain informal working documents
D. Because contingency and management reserve must be included in all three baselines
*Rationale:* B is the integration principle developed across this KA and formalised in KA 6.4.1. A states a
true but incidental fact, not the reason totals must match. C misstates PMBOK, which requires all three to be
formally baselined. D contradicts KA 3.3.5 — reserve is deliberately excluded from the PMB.

**MCQ 8.2.6 [topic 8.2.3, level: recall]**
Under PMBOK's Planning process group, which document typically records assumptions and constraints alongside
a project's deliverables, boundaries and acceptance criteria?
A. The risk register
B. **The project scope statement** ✔
C. The stakeholder register
D. The communications management plan
*Rationale:* B — assumptions and constraints are core components of the project scope statement (8.2.3). A
records assessed risk events, not planning assumptions generally; C and D serve different planning purposes.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using Pipeline Segment 4B's WP1, WP2, WP3 and WP5 budgets ($80,000, $150,000, $200,000 and $50,000) and
   its $600,000 BAC, confirm that WP4's budget satisfies the 100% rule.
2. Explain, in your own words, why Meridian's contingency and management reserve sit outside the scope,
   schedule and cost baselines rather than being time-phased into them.
3. Using the communication-channels formula, calculate how many channels exist if Pipeline Segment 4B's
   stakeholder list grows from 6 to 9 people.
4. Explain the "planning in silos" pitfall in your own words, and name one control from this KA that
   prevents it.

**Answers**

1. `600,000 − (80,000 + 150,000 + 200,000 + 50,000) = 600,000 − 480,000 = 120,000`, matching WP4's
   established $120,000 budget — the 100% rule is satisfied.
2. Contingency and management reserve are, by definition, not yet assigned to specific, scheduled scope;
   time-phasing them into the cost baseline would overstate what work is "supposed" to have happened by any
   given date and corrupt every later earned-value comparison (cross-ref KA 3.3.5). Reserve is released into
   the baseline only through a formal, documented change (cross-ref KA 5.4, KA 8.4).
3. `n(n − 1)/2`: at 6 people, `6 × 5 / 2 = 15`; at 9 people, `9 × 8 / 2 = 36`. Channels grow much faster than
   team size — a 50% increase in people more than doubles the number of channels.
4. Planning in silos is producing scope, schedule, cost and subsidiary plans (quality, resource,
   communications, risk and others) independently of one another, each internally coherent but never
   reconciled against a single, shared WBS and schedule — so the resulting baselines only look integrated.
   The control that prevents it is building every subsidiary plan and every baseline from the same WBS and
   schedule and formally integrating them into one project management plan and one Performance Measurement
   Baseline (8.2.2, 8.2.9), so a change to one is never accepted without checking its effect on the others.
