# Domain 8 — Project Management Lifecycle

## KA 8.3 — Executing

PMI's PMBOK Guide names **Executing** as the process group in which the project management plan built during
Planning (KA 8.2, once written) is carried out: work is directed and managed, deliverables are produced, the
team and resources are acquired and led, quality is assured, procurements are conducted, communications and
stakeholder engagement are managed, and approved changes (KA 5.4) are implemented. AACE International's Total
Cost Management (TCM) Framework describes the same territory from the controls side of the same coin: TCM's
continuing project-control cycle runs plan → **measure** → analyse → forecast → respond, and Executing is
where the "measure" step begins — where physical work first generates the raw observations a controls
professional will later turn into cost variance, schedule variance and a forecast (Domain 6). This KA sits
between KA 8.2 (Planning, which builds the baselines) and KA 8.4 (Monitoring & Controlling, once written,
which compares performance against them); Executing is the process group that makes the baseline true or
false in the first place, by doing the work the baseline describes.

The reason this KA matters specifically to a **project controls** professional, as distinct from a site or
delivery manager, is that Executing is the *source* of essentially every number this Body of Knowledge treats
as an input elsewhere: earned value's AC and the physical evidence behind EV (Domain 6), the commitments and
accruals in the cost ledger (KA 5.2, KA 11.2), the change requests and trends that populate the change
register (KA 5.4), and the risk realisations that draw down contingency (KA 12.2–12.3). None of that data
originates in a spreadsheet or a report — it originates on site, in a design office, at a supplier's premises,
the moment work is directed, performed, inspected, procured, communicated or changed. Treating Executing as
"somebody else's job — I just report on it" misses the single highest-leverage point in the lifecycle for
improving data quality: at source, before an error compounds through every downstream calculation that
assumes it is correct.

The worked examples in this KA continue **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**'s **Pipeline Segment 4B** (BAC
$600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**), anchored on the Month-3 data date already
established in KA 6.1 (PV $270,000, EV $330,000, AC $280,000, accrual-adjusted AC $305,000; work packages
WP1–WP5), and threading forward through the Month-1, Month-4 and Month-5 events already published in
KA 12.3 (risks R1–R3) and KA 5.4 (trend TR-04, variation VO-01). This KA does not recompute any of those
published figures; it shows the execution-level activity — the crew log, the inspection, the bid evaluation,
the RFI, the site instruction — that generated each one, and adds new, clearly illustrative detail (a
subcontractor selection, a cost-of-quality breakdown, a productivity log) one level of granularity finer than
the control-account figures already on record, in the same spirit as KA 5.4.4's Level-3 schedule review.

### 8.3.1 Directing and managing project work: from baseline to deliverables and data

**Definition & purpose.** PMBOK's **Direct and Manage Project Work** process is, in essence, the act of
carrying out the activities defined in the project management plan to produce the project's deliverables,
while generating **work performance data** as a by-product of doing so. It converts three planning-stage
abstractions into their execution-stage realities: the *scope baseline* becomes physical or functional
*deliverables*; the *cost baseline* becomes *actual cost*; the *schedule baseline* becomes *measured
progress*. Nothing about this process is optional or skippable — every other topic in this KA (section 8.3.2–section 8.3.6)
is a specific facet of directing and managing project work; this topic sets out the whole and the data
hierarchy that runs through all of it.

**The underlying principle.** PMBOK distinguishes three tiers of information that only ever flow one
direction, upstream to downstream: **work performance data** (raw, unanalysed observations captured at the
point of execution — "900 m excavated," "PO placed," "milestone signed off"); **work performance information**
(data analysed against the baseline — "45% complete," "EV $90,000," "SPI 0.92"); and **work performance
reports** (information compiled and distributed for a decision — a monthly cost report, a dashboard).
Monitoring & Controlling (KA 8.4, once written) mainly turns data into information and information into
reports; **Executing is where the first tier — the raw data itself — is created**, by people focused, in the
moment, on doing the work, not on producing clean data for later analysis. That tension is why
data-quality-at-source is this KA's recurring theme.

**Formula.** No calculation attaches to the process overview itself; the governing relationship is a one-way
data flow: `Source event (work performed, inspected, procured, instructed) → Work performance data (raw) →
Work performance information (analysed against baseline, KA 8.4) → Work performance report (compiled for a
decision)`. Data quality established (or lost) at the first arrow cannot be recovered at the third.

**Example 8.3.1 — one control account's data point, followed through the hierarchy.**

*Scenario.* On Pipeline Segment 4B, WP3 (Trenching & earthworks) is measured by percentage complete against
a physical unit of measure (KA 6.1 section 6.1.4). At the Month-3 data date, Meridian's site engineer records a
survey-confirmed excavated length.

*Required.* Trace this single fact through the three tiers.

*Working/Answer.*

| Tier | Content | Source / owner |
|---|---|---|
| Work performance data (raw) | "900 m excavated, confirmed by survey" | Site engineer, direct field measurement |
| Work performance information | "45.0% complete (900/2,000 m); EV = $90,000" (KA 6.1 section 6.1.4) | Project controls, applying the % complete (units) method |
| Work performance report | "WP3 is 45.0% complete against a 45.0% planned position; control account EV $330,000 tracking $60,000 ahead of PV" (KA 6.1–6.2) | Monthly cost report (KA 8.4, once written) |

*Interpretation.* Every figure in the second and third rows is a *derivative* of the first row; if the 900 m
had been a rough guess rather than a survey-confirmed measurement, the 45.0%, the $90,000 EV and the
downstream SV would all have inherited that uncertainty silently — the reader of the report has no way to
tell a measured fact from an estimate once it has been rolled up. Section 8.3.7 develops this principle in full
across all five of Pipeline Segment 4B's work packages.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating "execution" as purely a delivery/operations concern with no project-controls
interest — in fact every downstream EVM, cost and forecasting calculation in this Body of Knowledge depends on
data this process group generates. (2) Confusing work performance *data* with work performance
*information* — a raw fact ("900 m excavated") is not yet a conclusion ("ahead of plan") until analysed
against a baseline, and reporting the raw fact as if it were already the conclusion skips a step. (3) Assuming
data quality is Monitoring & Controlling's problem to fix — by the time information reaches KA 8.4, the
underlying data has usually already left the only people who could verify it against physical reality.

**AI in this topic.** Natural-language generation tools can draft a first-pass narrative summary of a period's
work performance data, saving drafting time on a routine status note (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.5). Such
a summary is only as reliable as the data fed to it; it cannot verify that "900 m excavated" was actually
surveyed rather than estimated, and a fluent, confident AI-drafted summary of poor-quality source data is, if
anything, more dangerous than an obviously rough one, because it reads as more authoritative than its inputs
justify.

### 8.3.2 Managing the project team and resources: acquiring, developing and directing effort

**Definition & purpose.** PMBOK groups **Acquire Resources**, **Develop Team** and **Manage Team** among the
Executing-stage Resource Management processes: securing the people, plant and materials the plan calls for,
building them into an effective working team, and then actively tracking performance, resolving issues and
adjusting effort as work proceeds. For a project controls professional, the specific output of interest is
**productivity** — the rate at which resourced effort converts into physical progress — because a shortfall
here is frequently the *earliest* observable signal of a cost or schedule problem, well before it shows up as
an adverse CV or SV in a monthly report.

**The underlying principle.** A control account's monthly percentage-complete figure (KA 6.1) is a
low-frequency, aggregated read of progress. A site-level productivity log — output per crew per working day —
is a higher-frequency, finer-grained read of the same underlying activity, and it is where a genuine emerging
problem (adverse ground conditions, a skills shortfall, a plant breakdown) is visible days or weeks before it
has accumulated enough to move a monthly EV figure noticeably. This is a management and early-warning tool at
the work-package level, distinct from (and does not replace) the control-account EV calculation already fixed
in KA 6.1 — in the same way KA 5.4.4 uses a schedule review one grain finer than the master CPM network.

**Formula.**

`Actual productivity rate = Quantity completed / Elapsed working days`
`Productivity factor = Actual productivity rate / Planned productivity rate`
`Revised days to complete remaining scope = Remaining quantity / Actual productivity rate`

**Example 8.3.2 — WP3 trenching productivity, Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 1–3.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's site method statement plans two excavation crews on WP3, combined at **25 m/day**. By
the Month-3 data date, **45 working days** have elapsed, and the site engineer's daily crew log shows
**900 m** excavated in total — the same figure already reported as WP3's actual progress in KA 6.1 section 6.1.4.
Total WP3 scope is 2,000 m.

*Required.* (a) The actual productivity rate and the productivity factor; (b) the working days needed to
complete the remaining scope at the current actual rate, versus at the originally planned rate.

*Working.*

(a) `Actual rate = 900 / 45 = 20 m/day`; `Productivity factor = 20 / 25 = 0.80 (80.0%)`

(b) Remaining scope = `2,000 − 900 = 1,100 m`
`Days at actual rate = 1,100 / 20 = 55 working days`
`Days at planned rate = 1,100 / 25 = 44 working days`
`Additional days if productivity does not recover = 55 − 44 = 11 working days`

*Answer.* **Productivity factor = 0.80 (80.0% of plan); 11 additional working days** are implied if the
current rate persists, over and above the originally planned pace.

*Interpretation.* An illustrative combined crew day-rate of **$800/day** (Meridian's internal site-monitoring
figure, distinct from WP3's $100/m all-in control-account rate) values the visible cost signal of those 11
days at `11 × 800 = $8,800` (SAR 33,000) — a leading indicator only, not a formal cost impact assessment
(that mechanism is KA 5.4.4's). This is the kind of execution-level signal that, investigated for cause,
becomes either a trend entry (KA 5.4.5) or — as later proves the case here — the first visible evidence of
the adverse ground conditions formally realising as risk **R1** at Month 4 (KA 12.3.3, actual cost $42,000).
The log does not *prove* R1 is occurring; it is the data that prompted the investigation that confirmed it.

log alone. (3) Using a site-level rate to *override* the control account's own EV method (KA 6.1 section 6.1.4)
rather than as a supplementary early-warning signal — the two serve different purposes.

**AI in this topic.** Telematics and IoT-linked equipment sensors, alongside computer-vision progress-tracking
from site cameras, can capture productivity data continuously, at a frequency no manual log can match
(cross-reference KA 13.5). These tools report *what* the rate is; they cannot diagnose *why* it has fallen —
ground conditions, a skills gap, a plant fault and a supervision issue can all produce an identical reading,
and distinguishing them remains an engineering judgement, not a sensor output.

KA 5.4.4 (the Level-3 schedule-granularity device this example reuses).

### 8.3.3 Managing quality: quality assurance and the cost of quality

**Definition & purpose.** PMBOK's **Manage Quality** process (formerly termed "Perform Quality Assurance")
translates the quality management plan into audits, process checks and inspections carried out *during* the
work, aimed at building conformance in rather than inspecting failure out after the fact — the same principle
that underlies ISO 9001's process-based approach to quality management (named here at awareness level; its
detailed clause structure is outside this Body of Knowledge's scope). The **Cost of Quality (COQ)** framework
— a standard tool within this PMBOK process — groups every quality-related dollar into four categories:
**prevention** (avoiding defects before they occur — training, procedure development), **appraisal**
(inspecting for defects — testing, audits), **internal failure** (fixing a defect found before the client
takes delivery) and **external failure** (fixing, or compensating for, a defect found after handover).
Prevention and appraisal together are the **cost of conformance**; internal and external failure together are
the **cost of non-conformance**.

**The underlying principle.** As with KA 5.4.1's cost-of-change curve, money spent early (conformance) is
economically different from money spent late (non-conformance): a dollar of appraisal spend that prevents a
defect from ever occurring displaces not just its own repair cost but, for a safety- or contract-critical
defect, potentially a far larger external-failure cost — reputational, contractual or safety-related — that
never has the chance to materialise. Unlike KA 5.4.1's curve, however, the COQ framework is not about *scope*
that changed but about *conformance to specification* — a distinct, though economically analogous, idea.

**Formula.**

`Total cost of quality = Prevention + Appraisal + Internal failure + External failure`
`Cost of conformance = Prevention + Appraisal`
`Cost of non-conformance = Internal failure + External failure`

**Example 8.3.3 — the cost of quality on WP4's welding programme, Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* KA 12.3.2's risk register carried **R2 — weld defect rework** (WP4) at a 40.0% probability and a
$50,000 impact (expected value $20,000); KA 12.3.3 records that "R2 never occurs (avoided via extra QA
inspection)" without detailing the cost of that inspection. Meridian's actual WP4 quality spend, Months 1–4:

| Category | Item | Cost ($) |
|---|---|---|
| Prevention | Welding procedure specification (WPS) & welder qualification | 3,000 |
| Appraisal | Incremental NDT coverage (100% radiographic testing, up from a planned 20% sample) | 9,000 |
| Internal failure | Minor rework on two joints, caught before final test | 2,000 |
| External failure | Warranty/defect claims after handover | 0 |
| **Total cost of quality** | | **14,000** |

*Required.* (a) Cost of conformance and cost of non-conformance; (b) whether the incremental appraisal spend
was, in expected-value terms, a worthwhile investment against R2.

*Working.*

(a) `Cost of conformance = 3,000 + 9,000 = 12,000`; `Cost of non-conformance = 2,000 + 0 = 2,000`
`Check: 12,000 + 2,000 = 14,000` ✓ (matches the total above)

(b) The $9,000 incremental appraisal spend (the extra NDT coverage beyond the originally planned 20% sample)
is the cost directly attributable to averting R2; the $3,000 prevention spend was part of the original quality
plan regardless of R2 and is not incremental. Comparing the incremental spend against R2's expected value
(KA 12.3.2): `Net benefit = 20,000 − 9,000 = 11,000`

*Answer.* **Total cost of quality = $14,000** (SAR 52,500); **cost of conformance $12,000, cost of
non-conformance $2,000**; the **$9,000** incremental appraisal spend against R2's **$20,000** expected value
gives a **net benefit of $11,000**.

*Interpretation.* This is the numeric substance behind KA 12.3.3's statement that R2 "never occurs" — it did
not vanish on its own; $9,000 of additional inspection converted a 40.0%-probability, $50,000-impact exposure
into two minor, cheaply-fixed internal-failure defects. Separately, because R2 never drew against its $20,000
share of contingency, that portion of the pool remained available and helped absorb R1's Month-4 shortfall
(KA 12.3.3) — quality assurance spend during Executing had a direct, traceable effect on the reserve position
reported in Domain 12.

**Mini-case — appraisal without prevention.** A different Meridian package runs 100% weld inspection but skips
welder requalification to save cost. Inspection catches every defect before handover — external failure stays
at zero — but internal failure (rework) runs to $18,000, far above WP4's $2,000, because appraisal alone does
not reduce the underlying defect rate; it only finds defects after they are made. Appraisal without prevention
converts external failure into internal failure; it does not reduce total non-conformance cost the way
combining both categories does.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Comparing the *total* cost of quality against an avoided risk's *full impact*
($50,000) rather than the *incremental* conformance spend against its *expected value* — both errors overstate
the apparent return. (2) Cutting appraisal cost to save budget without recognising that reduced inspection
coverage does not reduce the underlying defect rate — it only delays discovery, typically into more expensive
failure territory. (3) Treating a zero external-failure figure as proof spend can safely be cut next period —
it is evidence the *current* programme works, not that less spend would work equally well.

**AI in this topic.** Computer-vision defect-detection tools, trained on radiographic or visual weld imagery,
can flag candidate defects for a certified inspector's review faster and more consistently than manual review
alone (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). Such tools do not replace the inspector's sign-off, particularly for
safety-critical welds: a false negative carries real consequences, and the professional inspector, not the
model, remains accountable for the accept/reject decision on any given weld.

probability, impact and expected value); KA 12.3.3 (the reserve-position consequence of R2 not occurring);

### 8.3.4 Procurement execution: engaging and managing suppliers and subcontractors

**Definition & purpose.** PMBOK's **Conduct Procurements** process covers obtaining seller responses,
selecting a seller and awarding a contract; once awarded, that relationship is administered throughout
Executing as work proceeds. This topic covers the *project-management* side of that relationship — selection,
kick-off, and ongoing performance management of suppliers and subcontractors such as Meridian's WP4 welding
subcontractor, **Al-Noor Site Services**. It deliberately does not repeat the *transactional* mechanics of
requisitions, purchase orders, goods receipt and the three-way match — those are KA 11.2's Procure-to-Pay
cycle in full, cross-referenced here rather than restated.

**The underlying principle.** A source-selection decision is rarely a single-criterion (lowest price) choice
on work where quality, safety and schedule reliability materially affect total project outcome. A **weighting
system** — pre-agreed criteria, each given a weight summing to 100%, scored per bidder and combined into a
single weighted score — makes that multi-criteria trade-off explicit and auditable, rather than an
unstructured, potentially challengeable judgement call.

**Formula.**

`Weighted score (bidder) = Σ (Criterion weight_i × Criterion score_i)`, summed across all criteria, each
criterion scored on a common scale (illustrated here 0–10).

**Example 8.3.4 — selecting the WP4 welding subcontractor.**

*Scenario.* Before Al-Noor Site Services was awarded PO SC-PO-4B-014 (KA 11.2.2), Meridian evaluated three
bidders against four weighted criteria (Price 40%, Technical capability 25%, HSE record 20%, Schedule
commitment 15% — summing to 100%), each scored 0–10:

| Bidder | Price (40%) | Technical (25%) | HSE (20%) | Schedule (15%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Al-Noor Site Services | 8 | 9 | 8 | 7 |
| Bidder B | 9 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| Bidder C | 7 | 7 | 9 | 6 |

*Required.* The weighted score for each bidder, and the selected subcontractor.

*Working.*

`Al-Noor = (0.40×8) + (0.25×9) + (0.20×8) + (0.15×7) = 3.20 + 2.25 + 1.60 + 1.05 = 8.10`
`Bidder B = (0.40×9) + (0.25×6) + (0.20×7) + (0.15×8) = 3.60 + 1.50 + 1.40 + 1.20 = 7.70`
`Bidder C = (0.40×7) + (0.25×7) + (0.20×9) + (0.15×6) = 2.80 + 1.75 + 1.80 + 0.90 = 7.25`

*Answer.* **Al-Noor Site Services scores highest at 8.10**, ahead of Bidder B (7.70) and Bidder C (7.25).

*Interpretation.* Bidder B scored the highest on price alone (9 vs Al-Noor's 8) but loses overall on its
weaker technical capability and HSE scores — exactly the outcome a weighted system is designed to produce: a
lowest-price bidder does not automatically win where quality and safety carry real weight in the decision.
This is the award that produced PO SC-PO-4B-014, already established in KA 11.2.2, and the relationship
managed operationally through the rest of this topic.

**Mini-case — managing performance after award.** Three months into the subcontract, Al-Noor's weld output
begins tracking below the productivity implied by its PO schedule. Rather than waiting for a formal
Procure-to-Pay exception (KA 11.2.5), Meridian's site team raises the shortfall directly with Al-Noor's site
supervisor at a weekly coordination meeting, and jointly identifies a fixable cause — a single under-resourced
crew. Active performance management during execution, not only transactional processing, is what catches a
fixable problem while it is still fixable.

(3) Treating award as the end of procurement management — Al-Noor's mini-case shows ongoing performance
monitoring is where much of procurement's real project-controls value sits.

**AI in this topic.** E-sourcing and bid-evaluation platforms can auto-populate weighted-scoring templates from
submitted bid documents and flag missing or inconsistent information, speeding up the mechanical scoring step
(cross-reference KA 13.4). The criteria, weights and individual scores remain judgement calls — particularly
technical-capability and HSE scoring, which require domain expertise a general-purpose tool lacks — and the
award decision itself is never delegated to the tool.

### 8.3.5 Managing communications and stakeholder engagement

**Definition & purpose.** PMBOK's **Manage Communications** and **Manage Stakeholder Engagement** processes
cover executing the communications plan (routine reporting, meetings, correspondence) and actively working
with stakeholders to meet their needs and resolve issues as they arise, rather than only informing them after
the fact. A **request for information (RFI)** — a formal query raised to clarify a design, specification or
instruction — is one of the most common, and most consequential, communication instruments on an engineering
or construction project: an RFI response is frequently on the schedule's critical path.

**The underlying principle.** Communications management is judged less by volume than by **responsiveness on
the items that matter** — a healthy average response time can coexist with one severely delayed, high-impact
item that alone causes real schedule and cost consequences. Monitoring the *distribution* of response times,
not only the average, is therefore essential; an average can conceal exactly the outlier that most needs
attention.

**Formula.**

`Average turnaround = Σ (Response dates − Raised dates) / Number of items`, computed both including and
excluding any known outlier, to test how much the outlier alone is distorting the average.

**Example 8.3.5 — RFI cycle time, Pipeline Segment 4B, Months 1–4.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's communications management plan sets a target RFI turnaround of **≤5 working days**.
Eleven RFIs are raised and closed in the period. Ten are routine technical queries with turnaround times (in
working days) of 3, 4, 5, 4, 3, 5, 4, 5, 3 and 6. The eleventh, RFI-11, seeks the client's utility-interface
team's clarification ahead of the WP4 tie-in permit and takes **23 working days** to answer.

*Required.* (a) The average turnaround for the ten routine RFIs; (b) the overall average including RFI-11; (c)
how far RFI-11 alone exceeds the target.

*Working.*

(a) `Sum = 3+4+5+4+3+5+4+5+3+6 = 42`; `Average = 42 / 10 = 4.2 working days`

(b) `Overall sum = 42 + 23 = 65`; `Overall average = 65 / 11 = 5.9 working days` (1 d.p.)

(c) `RFI-11 excess = 23 − 5 = 18 working days over target`

*Answer.* **Routine average = 4.2 working days** (comfortably within target); **overall average = 5.9 working
days** (breaches the 5-day target once RFI-11 is included); **RFI-11 alone is 18 working days over target.**

*Interpretation.* A single high-impact RFI turned an otherwise healthy communications KPI from "on target" to
"breached" — and, more importantly for a controls professional, a permit-critical RFI answered 18 working
days late is precisely the kind of stakeholder-engagement delay that later surfaces as a schedule claim: this
is the same tie-in permit delay that appears in KA 5.4.2's change register as claim **CL-01** (10 working days
EOT + prolongation). The RFI log is part of the evidentiary trail a claims assessment (Domain 7, once
written) would draw on to substantiate or contest CL-01 — this topic does not attempt that substantiation
itself, only shows where the underlying communications data originates.

**Mini-case — a stakeholder engagement matrix.** Meridian's project controls manager scores four key
stakeholders' current and desired engagement level (Unaware / Resistant / Neutral / Supportive / Leading): the
client's sponsor (current Supportive, desired Leading — wants closer involvement in change decisions after
VO-01), the adjacent utility owner (current Neutral, desired Supportive — needed for permit cooperation,
directly relevant to RFI-11's delay), Al-Noor's site supervisor (current Supportive, desired Supportive — no
gap) and a residents' association near the site (current Unaware, desired Neutral). The *gaps* between
current and desired, not the raw levels, drive the communications plan's actions for the next period.

### 8.3.6 Implementing approved changes during execution

**Definition & purpose.** KA 5.4 develops the full change control process — identify, assess, approve/reject,
implement, update baseline. This topic covers only the **implement** step in detail: the point at which an
approved change stops being a governance decision and becomes site reality — resources remobilised, updated
instructions issued, and, critically for a controls professional, the point at which the new or revised cost
code (KA 5.4.7) begins collecting *actual* cost data for the first time.

**The underlying principle.** KA 5.4.7's mini-case already showed the risk of a gap between approval and
field implementation (a drawing revision lagging behind an already-updated cost baseline). The reverse gap
matters equally to a controls professional: once implementation *does* begin, the new cost code needs the
same budget-versus-commitment-versus-actual discipline as any other control account from day one — a newly
approved change is not exempt from ordinary cost control simply because it is new.

**Formula.** Reusing KA 11.2.2's available-budget formula, applied here to a change's own cost code:

`Available (uncommitted) budget on the cost code = Approved change budget − Actual costs posted to date −
Open commitments (unreceived PO balances) − Accruals not yet converted to a PO`

**Example 8.3.6 — implementing VO-01, first two weeks, cost code 4B-WP4-010.**

*Scenario.* VO-01 (KA 5.4.4, total cost impact $38,000: direct scope $32,000 — civil works $12,000, valve &
materials $14,000, installation labour $6,000 — plus $6,000 schedule-driven indirect cost) is approved at
Month 5 (KA 5.4.6) and implementation begins immediately under the new cost code **4B-WP4-010** (KA 5.4.7).
Two weeks in: the civil works crew has completed the chamber excavation and base slab, at an actual cost of
**$11,200** (firmer ground at this specific chamber location than WP3's alignment average, per section 8.3.2's
productivity log); the materials PO for the valve and specialist materials has been issued in full at
**$14,000** (a commitment, not yet received); installation labour has not yet started.

*Required.* (a) The variance on the civil works line; (b) the available (uncommitted) budget remaining on
cost code 4B-WP4-010's $32,000 direct-cost allocation.

*Working.*

(a) `Civil works variance = 12,000 − 11,200 = 800` (favourable)

(b) `Available budget = 32,000 − 11,200 (actual) − 14,000 (commitment) − 0 (accrual) = 6,800`

*Check:* the remaining, not-yet-started installation labour budget is $6,000; available budget ($6,800) less
that remaining need ($6,000) leaves exactly the **$800** favourable civil-works variance as genuine headroom:
`6,800 − 6,000 = 800` ✓

*Answer.* **$800 favourable variance on civil works; $6,800 available (uncommitted) budget** remains on the
cost code, of which $6,000 is earmarked for the not-yet-started labour and $800 is genuine headroom.

*Interpretation.* A freshly approved change generates its own actual-cost and commitment data within days of
implementation, subject to exactly the same available-budget discipline (KA 11.2.2) as any other cost code —
VO-01's approval did not create a separate, less-controlled pocket of spend. This $800 of early favourable
variance will itself feed next period's control-account report (KA 8.4, once written) and the closed-out
change record (KA 5.4.7).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating an approved change's budget as a single lump sum tracked loosely, rather than
applying the same budget/commitment/actual discipline as an established control account. (2) Reading early
favourable variance on one line item (civil works) as evidence the whole change will come in under budget,
before labour — often the more variable element — has even started. (3) Delaying the cost-code opening until
the *first invoice* arrives rather than at approval, losing visibility of the commitment stage entirely (the
same PO-as-commitment principle KA 11.2.2 establishes generally).

**AI in this topic.** Workflow-automation tools can auto-open a new cost code the moment a change is approved,
pre-populate it with the assessed budget, and route the first requisitions against it without manual set-up
delay (cross-reference KA 13.4). Such tools cannot verify that field implementation actually matches the
approved design (the drawing-revision risk KA 5.4.7 describes) — that remains an engineering sign-off, not a
system configuration step.

**Cross-references.** KA 5.4.4 (VO-01's cost build-up, reused here unchanged); KA 5.4.6, KA 5.4.7 (approval
and configuration management, of which this topic is the field-implementation counterpart); KA 11.2.2 (the
available-budget formula applied here); section 8.3.2 (the ground-condition context behind the $800 civil variance).

### 8.3.7 Capturing execution data for monitoring and controlling: data quality at source

**Definition & purpose.** This capstone topic draws together section 8.3.1–section 8.3.6 into the theme that runs beneath
the whole KA: every actual cost, every progress reading, every commitment posting a controls professional
relies on in Monitoring & Controlling (KA 8.4, once written) and in EVM (Domain 6) originates as work
performance data captured *during Executing*, by people whose primary focus at that moment is doing the work,
not producing analysable data. **Data quality at source** — accuracy, consistency of method, and timeliness —
is therefore not a downstream reporting concern; it is an execution-stage discipline.

**The underlying principle.** "Garbage in, garbage out" (developed generally in KA 13.2) applies with
particular force here because execution-stage errors are frequently *invisible* once aggregated: a rough
visual estimate substituted for a survey looks, in a monthly report, identical to a properly measured figure —
both are just a number in a cell. The professional's defence is not a downstream check on the final report
(by then, the error is baked into every calculation built on it) but disciplined, source-level data capture:
the *right* method (KA 6.1 section 6.1.4) applied by the *right* person, from the *right* evidence, every period.

**Formula.** No new calculation; a data-lineage requirement, restated from section 8.3.1:

`Every work performance data point = {value, measurement method, source event, person/system of record, date
captured}` — a figure with any element missing is not yet auditable data, whatever its apparent precision.

**Example 8.3.7 — tracing Month-3 work performance data to its source, Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* KA 6.1 section 6.1.4 already reports EV = $330,000 across WP1–WP5 at the Month-3 data date. This example
traces each work package's contribution back to the execution-stage event that generated it.

*Required.* The source event and system of record behind each figure already published in KA 6.1.

*Working/Answer.*

| WP | Method | Work performance data (raw) | Source event | EV (KA 6.1 section 6.1.4) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WP1 Design | 0/100 | "Deliverable issued and accepted" | Design-office document control sign-off | $80,000 |
| WP2 Procurement | 50/50 | "Purchase order placed" | Procurement PO issuance (KA 11.2.2) | $75,000 |
| WP3 Trenching | % complete (units) | "900 m excavated" | Site survey measurement | $90,000 |
| WP4 Pipe-laying | Weighted milestones | "Prep & Lay milestones achieved" | Engineering/QA milestone sign-off | $60,000 |
| WP5 Supervision | LOE | "3 of 6 planned months elapsed" | Approved timesheets | $25,000 |
| **Total** | | | | **$330,000** |

*Interpretation.* No two work packages generate their raw data the same way — a document sign-off, a PO
issuance, a physical survey, an engineering sign-off and a timesheet are five entirely different processes,
owned by five different roles, each with its own failure modes. This is also true of the Month-3 accrual: the
$25,000 GRNI accrual that lifts AC from $280,000 to $305,000 (KA 6.1 section 6.1.3, KA 11.2.6) originates in a site
engineer's physical inspection of Al-Noor's welding (a **goods/services receipt**, KA 11.2.3) — an execution
event, captured weeks before Monitoring & Controlling ever sees the resulting accrual entry.

**Mini-case — a rough estimate, quietly overstating EV.** On a different Meridian package, under schedule
pressure, a site engineer reports "roughly 1,200 m" of trenching complete from a visual walk, rather than
commissioning a survey. A survey four weeks later confirms the true figure was **1,050 m** — a **150 m
(12.5%) overstatement**. At that package's $100/m rate, the estimate had overstated EV by
`150 × 100 = $15,000` for the intervening month, quietly inflating both EV and the resulting CPI/SPI — a
distortion invisible in the report until the survey corrected it. Nothing about the estimate was dishonest;
it was simply the wrong method (a visual guess) applied where the control account required a measured one
(KA 6.1 section 6.1.4), and the report gave no indication which had been used.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Poor data at source — a rough estimate, a stale figure, or the wrong measurement

reaching the cost ledger without passing through KA 5.4's process first — work performed against an unapproved
instruction (KA 5.4.1's mini-case) generates real actual cost with no corresponding approved budget,
corrupting variance analysis just as thoroughly as a bad estimate, from the opposite direction. (3) Assuming
Monitoring & Controlling can "clean up" execution-stage data issues after the fact — by the time information
reaches a report, the reviewer usually has no independent way to verify the raw data behind it.

**AI in this topic.** Data-quality and anomaly-detection tools can screen incoming work performance data for
implausible jumps, stale figures (unchanged for suspiciously many periods) or values inconsistent with a
related data source — for example, a productivity claim inconsistent with logged crew-hours (cross-reference
KA 13.2, KA 13.5) — surfacing candidates for source verification at a scale manual review cannot match. Such
tools flag statistical anomalies, not ground truth: a genuinely fast month is also a statistical anomaly, and
only a human check against physical reality (a survey, a site visit) can tell the two apart — **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable**, nowhere more literally than at the point data first enters the system.

### Key terms

- **Direct and Manage Project Work** — the PMBOK process of carrying out planned activities to produce
  deliverables, generating work performance data as a by-product.
- **Work performance data / information / reports** — the three-tier hierarchy: raw observations; data
  analysed against the baseline; information compiled and distributed for a decision.
- **Deliverable** — a physical or functional output the project produces, verified and, ultimately, accepted.
- **Productivity rate / productivity factor** — output per unit of elapsed time, and its ratio to the planned
  rate; an execution-level early-warning signal, distinct from the control-account EV method.
- **Cost of Quality (COQ)** — prevention, appraisal, internal failure and external failure costs; cost of
  conformance (prevention + appraisal) versus cost of non-conformance (internal + external failure).
- **Manage Quality** — the PMBOK Executing-stage process of building conformance in through audits, checks and
  inspection, rather than relying solely on inspecting failure out afterwards.
- **Weighted scoring system** — a multi-criteria source-selection method combining pre-agreed criteria weights
  and per-criterion scores into a single comparable figure.
- **Conduct Procurements** — the PMBOK process of obtaining seller responses, selecting a seller and awarding
  a contract.
- **RFI (request for information)** — a formal query raised to clarify a design, specification or instruction;
  turnaround time is a common communications KPI and a frequent critical-path item.
- **Stakeholder engagement assessment matrix** — a tool comparing a stakeholder's current versus desired
  engagement level (Unaware / Resistant / Neutral / Supportive / Leading).
- **Change implementation** — the execution-stage step of carrying out an approved change (KA 5.4.6) in the
  field and opening its cost code to actual and committed cost.
- **Data quality at source** — the accuracy, consistency of method and timeliness of work performance data as
  it is first captured, before any downstream analysis or reporting occurs.
- **Data lineage** — the traceable record connecting a reported figure back to its measurement method, source
  event and system of record.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 8.3.1 [topic 8.3.1, level: recall]**
Which statement correctly distinguishes "work performance data" from "work performance information"?
A. Work performance data is raw, unanalysed observation; work performance information results from analysing
   that data against the project baseline ✔
B. Work performance information is raw, unanalysed observation; work performance data results from analysing
   it against the baseline
C. The two terms describe the same thing at different points in the project lifecycle
D. Work performance data is generated only in Monitoring & Controlling, never in Executing

*Rationale:* A states the governing PMBOK distinction (section 8.3.1): data is the raw fact ("900 m excavated"),
information is the same fact analysed against a baseline ("45% complete"). B reverses the definitions; C
collapses a distinction the whole KA depends on; D is factually wrong — Executing is precisely where work
performance data originates.

**MCQ 8.3.2 [topic 8.3.2, level: analysis]**
A work package's site productivity log shows a productivity factor of 0.80 (actual rate 80% of planned).
What is the correct interpretation of this figure on its own?
A. It automatically proves the risk register must be revised
B. It is an early-warning signal that should be investigated for cause, and may feed the trend register or
   risk monitoring if confirmed, but it does not by itself establish a formal cost or schedule variance ✔
C. It has no project-controls relevance until the monthly control-account report is issued
D. It means the control account's EV method (KA 6.1) should be replaced with a productivity-based method

*Rationale:* B reflects section 8.3.2's principle — the log is a leading indicator prompting investigation, not a
conclusion. A overstates what one figure proves; C ignores the entire point of finer-grained, high-frequency
monitoring; D confuses a monitoring tool with a change to an already-fixed EV method.

**MCQ 8.3.3 [topic 8.3.3, level: application — numerical]**
A project's quality spend for a period is: prevention $4,000, appraisal $6,000, internal failure $1,500,
external failure $500. What is the cost of conformance, and the total cost of quality?
A. Cost of conformance $10,000; total cost of quality $12,000 ✔
B. Cost of conformance $2,000; total cost of quality $12,000
C. Cost of conformance $12,000; total cost of quality $10,000
D. Cost of conformance $10,000; total cost of quality $10,000

*Rationale:* Cost of conformance = prevention + appraisal = 4,000+6,000 = 10,000; total COQ =
10,000+1,500+500 = 12,000 (section 8.3.3). B mistakes non-conformance cost for conformance cost; C and D each
transpose or omit a component.

**MCQ 8.3.4 [topic 8.3.4, level: application — numerical]**
A bidder scores Price 7 (weight 40%), Technical 8 (weight 25%), HSE 9 (weight 20%) and Schedule 6 (weight
15%). What is the bidder's weighted score?
A. 6.9
B. 7.5 ✔
C. 8.0
D. 30.0

*Rationale:* `(0.40×7)+(0.25×8)+(0.20×9)+(0.15×6) = 2.80+2.00+1.80+0.90 = 7.50` (section 8.3.4). A and C are plausible
arithmetic slips; D sums the raw unweighted scores.

**MCQ 8.3.5 [topic 8.3.5, level: application — numerical]**
Ten RFIs average 4.2 working days' turnaround. An eleventh RFI takes 23 working days. What is the overall
average turnaround across all eleven RFIs, to one decimal place?
A. 4.2 days
B. 5.9 days ✔
C. 13.6 days
D. 23.0 days

*Rationale:* Sum of the ten = `4.2×10 = 42`; overall sum = `42+23 = 65`; overall average = `65/11 = 5.9` days
(section 8.3.5). A restates only the routine average; C wrongly averages the two averages; D restates only the
outlier.

**MCQ 8.3.6 [topic 8.3.6, level: application — numerical]**
An approved change's direct-cost budget is $32,000. Actual costs posted to date are $11,200, and open
commitments (unreceived PO balances) total $14,000. No accruals have been raised. What is the available
(uncommitted) budget remaining?
A. $6,800 ✔
B. $17,000
C. $20,800
D. $32,000

*Rationale:* `Available = 32,000 − 11,200 − 14,000 − 0 = 6,800` (section 8.3.6, reusing KA 11.2.2's formula). B nets
off only the commitment; C nets off only the actual; D ignores both.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using section 8.3.1's data hierarchy, explain why a controls professional reviewing a monthly cost report cannot,
   from the report alone, tell whether a work package's progress figure was measured or estimated — and state
   what section 8.3.7 identifies as the professional's defence against this risk.
2. **Numerical.** A crew's planned productivity is 30 m/day. After 20 elapsed working days, actual output is
   480 m. Calculate (a) the actual productivity rate, (b) the productivity factor, and (c) the additional
   working days needed to complete a remaining 600 m at the current actual rate, compared with the originally
   planned rate.
3. Using Example 8.3.3, explain why the $9,000 incremental appraisal cost, rather than the full $14,000 cost
   of quality, is the correct figure to compare against R2's $20,000 expected value when judging whether the
   additional NDT coverage was a worthwhile investment.
4. **Numerical.** A cost code has an approved budget of $45,000. Actual costs to date are $18,500, open
   commitments total $20,000, and a $2,000 accrual for goods received but not yet invoiced has been raised.
   Calculate the available (uncommitted) budget remaining.

**Answers**

1. The report shows only the final, aggregated figure (e.g. "45% complete"); a visually estimated figure and a
   properly surveyed figure look identical once entered into the same cell, because the report does not
   normally carry the measurement method or source event alongside the value (section 8.3.1's three-tier hierarchy).
   Section 8.3.7 identifies disciplined, source-level data capture — the right method, applied by the right person,
   from the right evidence, every period, with a traceable data lineage — as the professional's defence, since
   downstream review of the final report cannot recover information the source-level process failed to
   capture.
2. (a) Actual rate = `480 / 20 = 24 m/day`. (b) Productivity factor = `24 / 30 = 0.80 (80.0%)`. (c) Days at
   actual rate = `600 / 24 = 25 days`; days at planned rate = `600 / 30 = 20 days`; additional days =
   `25 − 20 = 5 working days`.
3. The $3,000 prevention cost (welder qualification and procedure development) was part of the original
   quality plan regardless of R2 and would have been spent whether or not R2 was ever a risk; only the $9,000
   of *additional* NDT coverage, beyond the originally planned 20% sample, was spent specifically because of
   R2. Comparing that incremental, R2-specific spend against R2's expected value isolates the actual return on
   the decision to spend more on appraisal; including the non-incremental prevention cost would overstate the
   apparent cost of the decision and understate its net benefit.
4. `Available budget = 45,000 − 18,500 (actual) − 20,000 (commitment) − 2,000 (accrual) = 4,500`.
