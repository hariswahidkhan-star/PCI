# Domain 8 — Project Management Lifecycle

## KA 8.5 — Closing

Every process group examined earlier in this domain (Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring &
Controlling — once written) exists to deliver a product or service that someone else then operates. Closing
is where that handover is made real, permanent and auditable. PMI's PMBOK Guide names **Close Project or
Phase** as the concluding process within Project Integration Management: formally completing the project or
phase, confirming that all work and deliverables meet acceptance criteria, transferring the finished product
to operations, and finalising every open activity across every other knowledge area — cost, procurement,
risk, quality, resources, communications — so that nothing is left administratively or contractually open.
Other major frameworks converge on the same idea under different names: PRINCE2 dedicates a distinct
process, *Closing a Project*, to much the same set of activities; AACE International's Total Cost Management
(TCM) Framework treats project close-out and the final cost report as the concluding step of its cost
engineering process map. This Knowledge Area works through that closure in the order a controls professional
actually meets it: confirming the project or phase is genuinely finished and triggering the close-out
process; administrative and contractual close-out (including resolving any outstanding claims); the **final
account and final cost reconciliation** — this KA's flagship topic, and the one in which a controls
professional's role is most direct — releasing resources; handover, acceptance and the transition to
operations; defects, warranty and retention release; the lessons-learned retrospective and knowledge capture;
archiving records; and the post-implementation/benefits realisation review. Two Domain 7 Knowledge Areas sit
either side of this one and are deliberately not repeated here: KA 7.2 (once written) covers the *contractual*
mechanics of contract close-out — final completion certificates, discharge and release, dispute-resolution
routes for anything unresolved — in full; KA 7.4 (once written) covers the *commercial* mechanics of the
final account itself — final valuation, final certificate, retention accounting and payment — in full. This
KA's job is the *project-controls* view of closing: what a controls professional does, calculates and
verifies as the project winds down, cross-referring to those two KAs rather than duplicating their content.
The worked examples continue **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**'s **Pipeline Segment 4B** (original BAC
$600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**), picking up the thread carried through
Domains 3, 4, 5, 6 and 12 and bringing it to a close: the Month-5 status established in KA 6.3–6.4 (BAC
$600,000; PV $540,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250; CPI 0.80; SPI 0.75), the reserve position and change VO-01
established in KA 12.3 and KA 5.4 (final approved BAC $643,000, management reserve exhausted, planned
completion pushed five working days into Month 7), and the leading indicators tracked in KA 4.1 (RFI ageing
and submittal approval rate) are all drawn on below without being re-derived.

### 8.5.1 Closing the project or phase: purpose, triggers and the closure process

**Definition & purpose.** Close Project or Phase is triggered by one of two events: the project's objectives
have been achieved (a **normal closure**), or the project is being terminated early — by client decision,
funding withdrawal or an unrecoverable position — before its objectives are met (an **early/abnormal
closure**). Both trigger the same underlying discipline, though an early closure adds work an on-schedule
closure does not: settling commitments for scope that will now never be executed, and documenting why the
project stopped. Either way, closure is not a single event but a checklist of interdependent activities that
this KA works through in sequence — confirming completion, closing out contracts, reconciling final cost,
releasing resources, securing acceptance, releasing retention, capturing lessons, archiving records and,
later, reviewing benefits.

**The underlying principle.** A project is not closed when the last deliverable is technically finished; it
is closed when every obligation it created — contractual, financial, administrative, informational — has been
formally discharged or handed to whoever owns it next (operations, finance, the archive). An unfinished
close-out is a live source of risk long after the project team has moved on: an unclosed subcontract can
still generate an invoice, an unreleased retention can still be disputed, an unresolved claim can still
escalate — all with no project team left to answer for them.

**Formula.** No calculation; a sequenced closure checklist, the spine the rest of this KA fills in:

`Confirm scope/deliverables complete → Administrative & contractual close-out (8.5.2) → Final account & cost
reconciliation (8.5.3) → Release resources (8.5.4) → Handover & acceptance (8.5.5) → Retention/warranty
release, deferred to end of DLP (8.5.6) → Lessons learned (8.5.7) → Archive records (8.5.8) → Post-
implementation/benefits review, deferred (8.5.9)`

**Example 8.5.1 — Pipeline Segment 4B's closure trigger and readiness check.**

*Scenario.* At the point KA 5.4.6 left the project (Month 5, VO-01 approved, planned completion pushed to
early Month 7), Meridian's project controls lead is asked whether the project can now be formally closed.

*Required.* Assess closure readiness.

*Working/Answer.* No — physical completion has not yet occurred (planned early Month 7), the final account
is not yet agreed (VO-01's cost is approved but not yet reconciled against final actual cost, §8.5.3), and
CL-01's claim is still under review (§8.5.2). Closure is triggered only once mechanical/physical completion
is achieved and acceptance testing begins — the checklist above becomes active from that point, not before.

*Interpretation.* Closure readiness is itself a governed decision, not an assumption — starting the close-out
checklist prematurely, before physical completion, wastes effort re-doing steps as late changes continue to
arrive.

**Mini-case — an early closure.** A different Meridian pipeline package is terminated at 60% physical
completion when the client's funding is withdrawn. The same checklist applies, but §8.5.2's contractual
close-out must additionally settle the cost of demobilising from incomplete work, any termination-for-
convenience compensation owed under the contract, and the accounting treatment of work-in-progress that will
now never be billed under the original scope — a materially heavier administrative and financial close-out
than a normal, on-schedule closure, even though fewer deliverables exist to hand over.

early/abnormal termination without adding the termination-specific steps the mini-case describes.

(§8.5.5) or whether a closure is premature — those remain the professional's calls.

### 8.5.2 Administrative and contractual close-out

**Definition & purpose.** Administrative close-out confirms every internal record is complete and consistent
with what was actually delivered: final scope confirmed against the baseline, all internal approvals on
file, all cost codes closed to further postings. Contractual close-out is the corresponding external
discipline: every subcontract and purchase order formally closed, every outstanding claim or dispute
resolved or referred to the correct resolution route, all bonds and guarantees released or allowed to lapse,
and formal written confirmation from both parties that the contract's obligations are discharged. KA 7.2
(once written) covers the full contractual mechanics — completion certificates, discharge and release
clauses, dispute-resolution escalation — in depth; this topic covers the project-controls professional's part
in that process: verifying, from the controls side, that nothing remains open.

**The underlying principle.** A contract cannot be closed with a known, unresolved item sitting inside it —
every claim, variation and trend still open in the change register (KA 5.4.2) must be resolved, one way or
another, before a final account can be certified (§8.5.3). "Resolved" does not always mean "paid in full": a
claim can be resolved by full acceptance, partial settlement, rejection, or referral to formal dispute
resolution — but it cannot be resolved by simply letting the project end with its status still showing "under
review."

**Formula.** No calculation; a closure checklist per open item:

`For every open change-register item (KA 5.4.2): Resolve (accept / settle / reject / refer to dispute
resolution) → Record final agreed value → Confirm no further register entries remain open`

**Example 8.5.2 — resolving CL-01 at contractual close-out.**

*Scenario.* KA 5.4.2's Month-5 change register left claim **CL-01** — 10 working days' extension of time (EOT)
and $18,000 of prolongation cost, for a client-caused late tie-in permit release — with status "under
review, pending substantiation." At contractual close-out, Meridian's commercial team and the client's
Engineer complete the substantiation review together.

*Required.* Resolve CL-01 and record the outcome.

*Working/Answer.* Correspondence confirms the permit delay was genuinely client-caused, but only 6 of the 10
claimed working days are substantiated as critical-path impact (4 days ran concurrently with float already
available elsewhere in the programme, KA 10.2); prolongation cost is agreed pro-rata. **CL-01 is settled at 6
working days' EOT and $10,800** (6/10 × $18,000 — a clean pro-rata settlement, consistent with the agreed
day count), formally recorded and closed in the register.

*Interpretation.* CL-01's $10,800 settlement is a **contractual entitlement Meridian recovers from the
client** — a final-account/revenue matter for KA 7.4 (once written), not a further addition to the BAC-versus-
actual-cost reconciliation this KA builds in §8.5.3, which measures Meridian's own delivery efficiency against
its own approved budget. Confusing the two — folding a claim settlement into a cost-performance variance — is
a common and misleading practice, addressed further in the pitfalls below. The 6-day EOT is, however, directly
relevant to §8.5.5's acceptance date and to confirming no liquidated-damages exposure remains on the days
properly excused.

**Mini-case — a register that closes clean.** By the time of contractual close-out, every other Month 3–5
register item (KA 5.4.2's TR-04→VO-01, CR-02, and CL-01 itself) shows a final status: converted, approved, or
settled — none left as "open" or "pending." The controls lead confirms this in a one-line close-out statement
to the client: *"the change register contains no open items as at the date of this notice."* That single
sentence is only true because every item was tracked to resolution, not merely raised — the entire practical
value of KA 5.4.2's register discipline, realised at the moment it matters most.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Allowing a claim to remain "under review" past project completion, with no formal
resolution — the single most common cause of a contract that cannot be formally, cleanly closed. (2) Folding
a settled claim's value into the cost-performance (BAC vs. actual cost) reconciliation, conflating a
commercial recovery with a delivery-efficiency measure — CL-01's $10,800 changes what Meridian recovers from
the client, not how efficiently it executed its own scope.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-analytics tools can extract every close-out obligation from the contract text
(notice periods, required certificates, bond-release conditions) and cross-check them against the change
register and correspondence, flagging anything unresolved (cross-reference KA 13.4) — a genuine time-saver on
a contract running to hundreds of clauses. Such tools cannot assess the merits of a contested claim like
CL-01 (was the delay genuinely client-caused, and for how many days) — that substantiation judgement remains
commercial and professional, not automatable.

**Cross-references.** KA 5.4.2, KA 5.4.3 (the change register and claim classification this topic resolves);
KA 7.2 (once written — completion certificates, discharge, dispute resolution, in full); KA 7.4 (once
written — where CL-01's settled value is recognised in the final account); §8.5.3 (why a settled claim is
kept separate from the cost reconciliation).

### 8.5.3 The final account and final cost reconciliation

**Definition & purpose.** The **final cost reconciliation** is the controls professional's core deliverable
at closure: a complete, auditable bridge from the approved budget (BAC, as revised by every change approved
during execution) to the final actual cost incurred, with every variance explained and traceable to a
specific, already-documented cause. It is the project-controls counterpart to KA 7.4's (once written)
**final account** — the commercial document that reconciles the contract's final value, all certified
payments, retention and any claim settlements into a single agreed closing figure. The two are related but
distinct: this topic reconciles **cost** (what it cost Meridian to deliver); KA 7.4's final account reconciles
**value** (what the client owes and what has been paid) — cross-referenced here, not repeated.

**The underlying principle.** A defensible final cost reconciliation has exactly two kinds of input: the
final, fully updated **approved budget** (original BAC plus every change formally approved through KA 5.4's
process) and the final, fully reconciled **actual cost** drawn from the cost ledger (KA 5.2) at project
completion. Every dollar of variance between the two must trace to a specific, already-documented cause — a
shortfall already visible in an earlier EVM report (KA 6.2–6.3), a reserve drawdown (KA 12.3) or an approved
change (KA 5.4) — never to an unexplained residual. AACE International's TCM Framework treats this final cost
report as the concluding, auditable record of the project's cost-engineering effort — the single figure a
client, auditor or future estimator can rely on.

**Formula(e).**

`Final approved BAC = Original BAC + Σ (approved baseline changes, KA 5.4.6)`
`Final actual cost (AC final) = Actual cost to the last EVM status date + Cost to complete remaining
approved scope`
`Final cost variance = Final approved BAC − Final actual cost` (positive = favourable; negative = adverse,
shown in parentheses, per the style convention used throughout this book)

**Example 8.5.3 — Pipeline Segment 4B's final cost reconciliation.**

*Scenario.* Physical completion is achieved in Month 7, consistent with KA 5.4.6's revised planned date.
The final approved BAC and final actual cost are assembled from figures already established elsewhere in
this book.

*Required.* (a) The final approved BAC; (b) the final actual cost; (c) the final cost variance, explained.

*Working.*

**(a) Final approved BAC**

| Item | Amount (USD) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Original BAC | 600,000 | KA 6.1 |
| + Management-reserve transfer funding a contingency shortfall (R1 ground-conditions risk) | 5,000 | KA 12.3.3 |
| + Approved change VO-01 (isolation valve chamber) | 38,000 | KA 5.4.4–5.4.6 |
| **= Final approved BAC** | **643,000** | KA 5.4.6 |

**(b) Final actual cost**

| Item | Amount (USD) | Source / basis |
|---|---|---|
| Actual cost to Month 5 (covering EV $405,000 of original scope) | 506,250 | KA 6.3.2 |
| + Cost to complete remaining original scope (BAC − EV = 600,000 − 405,000 = $195,000 of budgeted work), delivered at breakeven efficiency (CPI 1.00) following the root-cause corrective actions traced in KA 4.2.7/4.2.9 and the schedule-recovery measures in KA 10.3 | 195,000 | This KA's closing assumption — flagged for SME verification below |
| = Final actual cost, original scope | 701,250 | — |
| + VO-01 actual cost (delivered at its approved estimate exactly) | 38,000 | KA 5.4.4 |
| **= Final actual cost (AC final)** | **739,250** | — |

**(c)** `Final cost variance = 643,000 − 739,250 = (96,250)`, adverse.

*Explaining the variance, by component:*

| Component | Amount (USD) | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Base-scope cost variance | (101,250) | `Original BAC 600,000 − final actual, original scope 701,250`. Identical in magnitude to the Month-5 CV (KA 6.3.2) — because the remaining work was delivered at exact breakeven (CPI 1.00), none of the adverse cost variance already booked by Month 5 was clawed back, but no further erosion occurred either. |
| Management-reserve transfer | 5,000 | Legitimately funds part of the R1 risk realisation already embedded in actual cost above (KA 12.3.3), narrowing the apparent overrun by the amount properly pre-approved. |
| VO-01 | 0 | Delivered at its approved estimate exactly — contributes equally to BAC and actual cost, so no net variance. |
| **Total** | **(96,250)** | Check: `(101,250) + 5,000 + 0 = (96,250)` ✓ |

*Answer.* **Final approved BAC = $643,000; final actual cost = $739,250; final cost variance = $(96,250),
adverse** — SAR (361,000 approx.) at the illustrative rate — **14.97% (≈15.0%) of the final approved BAC, and
16.0% of the original $600,000 BAC.**

*Interpretation.* The final reconciliation confirms, rather than overturns, what KA 6.3's Month-5 EAC range
already signalled: the base-scope adverse variance is frozen at exactly the Month-5 CV ($101,250) because
corrective action brought remaining-work efficiency back to breakeven but could not retroactively recover
cost already spent inefficiently — a central, sobering lesson of final cost reconciliation: **a cost variance
already incurred is not, in general, recoverable by later good performance; only future spend can still be
controlled.** Note, too, that the reconciliation deliberately excludes CL-01's $10,800 settlement
(§8.5.2) — that is a commercial recovery reconciled in KA 7.4's (once written) final account, not a
cost-efficiency item.

**Mini-case — a bridge that doesn't close.** A different project's final cost reconciliation shows a $47,000
gap that the controls team cannot trace to any specific change, risk drawdown or documented cost-performance
trend — an "other/unexplained" residual. The client's finance team refuses to accept the final account until
it is explained; investigation eventually traces it to an unrecorded scope addition executed without ever
passing through change control (KA 5.4.1's scope-creep failure mode). A final cost reconciliation is only as
good as the change-control and cost-ledger discipline maintained throughout execution — it cannot manufacture
an explanation retroactively for a control that was never exercised.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Building the final reconciliation against the *original* BAC ($600,000) rather than

a different question and should be kept in KA 7.4's final account instead.

**AI in this topic.** Reconciliation-automation tools can match ledger transactions against the change

approved change or a known EVM trend for investigation (cross-reference KA 13.5) — genuinely valuable at the
transaction volume a completed project's ledger contains. Such tools cannot judge whether the "cost to
complete remaining scope" assumption used above (breakeven efficiency, following corrective action) is
itself a reasonable one for a specific project — that professional judgement, and the final sign-off on the
reconciliation, remain with the controls professional. **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 5.2 (the cost ledger this reconciliation draws its final actual cost from); KA 5.4.6

confirms); KA 12.3 (the reserve transfer); §8.5.2 (why CL-01 is excluded); KA 7.4 (once written — the
commercial final account this cost reconciliation feeds, but does not replace).

### 8.5.4 Releasing resources and demobilisation

**Definition & purpose.** Releasing resources means formally standing down labour, plant, subcontractors and
temporary facilities once their scope is complete, and returning project team members to the organisation's
resource pool or their next assignment. Done well, it is timed precisely to when each resource's work is
actually finished — not before (risking rework without the people who understand it) and not after (paying
for capacity the project no longer needs).

**The underlying principle.** Different work packages finish at different times, so resource release is
staggered, not a single end-of-project event — and the cost of releasing too late is a direct, quantifiable
addition to actual cost (idle labour, standing plant) exactly analogous to the extended-overhead cost
mechanics already derived in KA 5.4.4.

**Formula.** No calculation; a staggered release schedule keyed to each work package's completion:

`For each work package: confirm 100% EV achieved → confirm no outstanding rework → release the
resource/plant/subcontract on its scheduled release date`

**Example 8.5.4 — staggered resource release, Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.*

| Work package | Scope complete | Resource released |
|---|---|---|
| WP1 Design & engineering | Month 3 (100% EV, KA 6.1) | Design team released Month 3 |
| WP3 Trenching & earthworks | Month 6 | Trenching plant and crew released Month 6 |
| WP4 Pipe-laying & welding (incl. VO-01) | Month 7 | Welding crew released Month 7 |
| WP5 Site supervision (PM/QA/HSE, LOE) | Month 7 (final acceptance) | Supervision team released last, Month 7 |
| Contingency and management reserve | — | Fully drawn (KA 5.4.6: $0 remaining in each) — no unspent reserve to release |

*Required.* Confirm the release schedule is efficient.

*Working/Answer.* Each resource is released on its own work package's completion date, not held to the
project's overall end date — WP1's design team is released four months before final project completion,
avoiding four months of unnecessary retained capacity. The final line item is a genuine, if unusual, closing
step: because both reserves were fully drawn during execution (KA 5.4.6), there is no unspent contingency or
management reserve left to formally release back to the organisation — itself worth stating explicitly in the
close-out record, since a silent absence could otherwise be mistaken for an omission rather than a fact.

**Mini-case — releasing too early.** On a different work package, a specialist testing subcontractor is
released immediately after the main weld-test sequence completes, before the client's independent
verification inspection. A minor non-conformance is found during that inspection, and the subcontractor must
be remobilised at a $6,500 call-back cost — a direct, avoidable cost caused by sequencing the release one
step too early in the acceptance process (§8.5.5).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Releasing a specialist resource before acceptance testing/verification is complete
(the mini-case). (2) Retaining resources past their work package's completion "just in case," a direct,
avoidable cost.

**AI in this topic.** Resource-scheduling tools can flag a resource whose assigned work package has reached
100% EV but has no release date yet logged, and can forecast release dates from the schedule automatically
(cross-reference KA 13.4) — useful, low-risk automation given the underlying logic is largely mechanical.
They cannot judge whether a resource should be retained slightly longer against acceptance risk (the
mini-case) — that sequencing judgement is the professional's.

**Cross-references.** KA 5.4.6 (the reserve position confirmed as fully drawn here); §8.5.5 (the acceptance
sequence a resource release must not outrun); KA 10.4 (schedule control, the source of each work package's
completion date).

### 8.5.5 Handover, acceptance and the transition to operations

**Definition & purpose.** Handover is the formal transfer of the completed deliverable — and the
documentation needed to operate it — from the project to whoever owns it next, typically the client's
operations function. **Acceptance** is the client's formal confirmation, usually against pre-agreed
acceptance criteria and a **punch list** (or snag list) of minor outstanding items, that the deliverable is
fit to take over. PMBOK treats **validate scope** (confirming deliverables against acceptance criteria) as a
distinct step from **verify scope** (the project team's own internal quality check) precisely because the
client's sign-off, not the project team's own assessment, is what actually closes this step.

**The underlying principle.** A punch list exists because "complete" and "perfect" are not the same
standard: acceptance at **substantial** (or practical) **completion** is normal with a small, documented list
of minor, non-critical items still outstanding, provided none of them prevents safe, intended use — a
distinction from **final completion**, reached only once every punch-list item is closed out (and, on many
contracts, the trigger for the second half of retention release, §8.5.6).

**Formula.** No calculation; the acceptance sequence:

`Internal verification (project team) → Client inspection against acceptance criteria → Punch list agreed →
Certificate of substantial/practical completion issued → Punch-list items closed → Certificate of final
completion issued`

**Example 8.5.5 — Pipeline Segment 4B's acceptance and punch list.**

*Scenario.* At the Month-7 client inspection, three items are noted:

| # | Item | Category | Closed within |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Minor coating touch-up, exposed pipe section | Cosmetic | 5 days |
| 2 | As-built drawing for VO-01's valve chamber not yet issued | Documentation | 10 days |
| 3 | Isolation valve chamber operator training not yet delivered | Operational readiness | 20 days |

*Required.* Determine whether substantial completion can be certified.

*Working/Answer.* None of the three items prevents safe, intended use of the pipeline — coating touch-up is
cosmetic, the as-built drawing is a documentation gap correctable without further site work, and operator
training, while important, does not itself affect the asset's physical readiness. **Substantial completion is
certified**, with all three items logged on the punch list against the deadlines above; final completion
follows once they close.

*Interpretation.* Item 3 (operator training) matters well beyond this KA's checklist — it is the direct
operational precondition for the benefit VO-01 was built to deliver (§8.5.9), and a slow start on it is
exactly what §8.5.9's worked example finds actually happened.

**Mini-case — accepting past the punch list's deadline.** Item 2's as-built drawing slips to 34 days,
well past its agreed 10-day deadline, with no formal extension recorded and no escalation raised. By the time
it is finally issued, the client's own document-control system has already superseded it with a
site-marked-up interim version, creating two conflicting "as-built" records — precisely the configuration-
management failure mode described in KA 5.4.7, recurring here at handover rather than during execution.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating substantial completion as if it were final completion, letting punch-list
items drift with no deadline enforcement (the mini-case). (2) Certifying substantial completion with an item
that *does* affect safe or intended use still open — the test is materiality to use, not simply "small." (3)
Allowing two versions of a handover document (an official as-built and a site-marked interim copy) to
coexist, precisely the configuration-management failure KA 5.4.7 already warned against.

**AI in this topic.** Computer-vision and inspection-report tools can generate a draft punch list
automatically from site photographs or scanned inspection sheets, and can track each item's ageing against
its agreed deadline (cross-reference KA 13.4) — a real efficiency gain on a large handover with many minor
items. Such tools cannot judge whether an item is material enough to block substantial completion — that
acceptance decision is the client's, informed by the professional's assessment, never an automated one.

**Cross-references.** KA 5.4.7 (configuration management, the failure mode recurring in the mini-case); KA
7.2 (once written — completion certificates in full); §8.5.6 (retention release tied to final completion);
§8.5.9 (the operational-readiness punch-list item's link to benefits realisation).

### 8.5.6 Defects, warranty and retention release

**Definition & purpose.** Most contracts withhold a small percentage of certified value as **retention** —
security against defects appearing after completion — released in two tranches: typically half at substantial
completion, and the remainder at the end of the **defects liability period (DLP)**, commonly around 12 months,
provided any defects notified during that period have been properly remedied. A **defect** discovered and
notified within the DLP that the contractor fails to remedy within the notice period entitles the client to
have it fixed by others and deduct the cost from the retention still held — the practical teeth behind the
mechanism. KA 7.4 (once written) covers the full commercial and contractual mechanics of retention
certification and release; this topic covers the calculation and the controls professional's role in tracking
it to closure.

**The underlying principle.** Retention exists because acceptance (§8.5.5) confirms the asset is fit for use
*now* — it cannot confirm the absence of defects that only manifest under load, weather or time. Holding back
value, rather than simply trusting a warranty promise, gives the client a self-executing remedy that does not
depend on the contractor's continued cooperation or solvency.

**Formula(e).**

`Total retention = Retention rate × Contract value`
`Release 1 (at substantial/practical completion) = 50% × Total retention` (contract-specific split; 50/50 is
a common, not universal, convention)
`Release 2 (at end of DLP) = 50% × Total retention − Any unremedied-defect deduction`

**Example 8.5.6 — Pipeline Segment 4B's retention release.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's contract for Pipeline Segment 4B specifies retention at **5% of the certified contract
value**, based on the original $750,000 fixed-price contract (KA 4.3) — treatment of VO-01's value within the
final retention base is a KA 7.4 (once written) matter, not resolved here — released 50% at substantial
completion and 50% at the end of a 12-month defects liability period. During the DLP, the client notifies
Meridian of a minor gasket seep at the VO-01 isolation valve chamber; Meridian does not attend within the
contractual notice period, and the client engages another contractor to fix it at a cost of **$2,400**,
deductible from the second retention release.

*Required.* (a) Total retention; (b) Release 1; (c) Release 2, net of the deduction; (d) confirm the totals
reconcile.

*Working.*

(a) `Total retention = 5% × 750,000 = 37,500`

(b) `Release 1 = 50% × 37,500 = 18,750`, at Month 7 (substantial completion)

(c) `Release 2 = 18,750 − 2,400 = 16,350`, at the end of the 12-month DLP

(d) `Check: Release 1 + Release 2 + Defect deduction = 18,750 + 16,350 + 2,400 = 37,500` ✓, reconciling exactly
to total retention.

*Answer.* **Release 1 = $18,750; Release 2 (net) = $16,350; $2,400 permanently retained/used to fund the
defect fix; total retention fully reconciled at $37,500** (SAR 140,625 total, at the illustrative rate).

*Interpretation.* The $2,400 deduction is a small fraction (6.4%) of total retention, but it is a direct,
avoidable consequence of missing the notice-period response window, not of the defect itself — a defect
promptly remedied by Meridian at, say, $600 of its own cost would have cost roughly a quarter as much and
left the full retention intact.

**Mini-case — a defect notified after the DLP closes.** A separate minor leak is reported by the client one
month *after* the 12-month DLP has formally ended and the second retention tranche has already been released.
Because the DLP defines the window during which the retention mechanism applies, this report falls instead
under the contract's general warranty terms (if any survive the DLP) rather than a retention deduction — a
distinction the controls professional must be able to explain precisely, since "the defects period has ended"
and "Meridian has no further liability" are not automatically the same statement.

**AI in this topic.** Retention-tracking tools can hold the release-date calendar, calculate each tranche

dates are fixed by contract. Such tools cannot judge whether a reported issue is a genuine defect within the
contractor's liability or normal wear — that assessment remains a professional and, where contested, a
contractual judgement (KA 7.2, once written).

### 8.5.7 Lessons learned, the retrospective and knowledge capture

**Definition & purpose.** A **lessons-learned review** (or post-project retrospective) is a structured
session, held at or shortly after closure, that captures what worked, what did not, and what should change
next time — recorded in a form the organisation can actually retrieve and reuse, not just a discussion that
happened once and was forgotten. This is distinct from, though related to, the **Sprint Retrospective** used
in adaptive delivery (KA 9.2, once written), which is a recurring, in-flight event during execution rather
than a single closing review; the underlying discipline of honest, blame-free reflection is the same in both.

**The underlying principle.** A lessons-learned review is only valuable if it produces *specific, actionable,
traceable* findings — tied to a documented event, not a vague generality — and if the organisation actually
stores and later retrieves them for the next similar project. A retrospective that restates known problems
without a specific recommendation, or one whose findings are filed and never consulted again, has captured
nothing of lasting value.

**Formula.** No calculation; a structured capture format:

`Lessons-learned entry = {Observation (with evidence/reference), Root cause, Recommendation, Owner for the
recommendation, Where it is stored for reuse}`

**Example 8.5.7 — a leading-indicator lesson from Pipeline Segment 4B's retrospective.**

*Scenario.* At the closing retrospective, the controls team reviews the leading indicators already tracked
in KA 4.1 across Months 3–5: RFI ageing rose from 6 days to 18 days; submittal approval rate fell from 96%
to 82% — both well before the adverse Month-5 CV/SV became visible, and before the Month-5 exception report
(KA 4.3) escalated the position on RAG status alone.

*Required.* Turn this observation into a specific, traceable lessons-learned entry.

*Working/Answer.*

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Observation | RFI ageing tripled (6→18 days) and submittal approval fell 14 percentage points (96%→82%) between Month 3 and Month 5, both preceding the adverse CV/SV first reported at Month 5 (KA 4.1, KA 6.3). |
| Root cause | The exception-reporting framework (KA 4.3) escalated only on lagging CPI/SPI thresholds; no explicit threshold existed for the leading indicators themselves, so the early signal was visible in the data but did not trigger action. |
| Recommendation | Add explicit leading-indicator thresholds to the exception-reporting framework (e.g., RFI ageing > 10 days, submittal approval < 90%) so escalation triggers a reporting cycle earlier than a lagging cost/schedule breach alone. |
| Owner | Project Controls Manager, for the next programme's reporting design. |
| Stored | Organisational lessons-learned register, tagged "leading indicators / exception reporting," cross-referenced to KA 4.1 and KA 4.3. |

*Interpretation.* This is a genuinely reusable finding: it names the exact data, the exact gap in the existing
process, and a concrete process change for the *next* project — the standard a lessons-learned entry should
meet, well above "communication could have been better."

**Mini-case — a lesson that stays a slide.** A different project's retrospective produces a well-attended
session and a polished summary slide identifying "poor early warning" as a theme, but no register entry is
created, no owner is assigned, and the slide is filed in a folder no future project ever opens. Eighteen
months later, a sister project suffers an almost identical late-escalation failure. The retrospective was not
wasted effort — the discussion was genuine — but without the structured capture format above, its value never
left the room it was held in.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI drafting tools can summarise correspondence, status reports and meeting minutes
into a first-draft set of candidate lessons for the team to review (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.5) —
genuinely useful for surfacing patterns across a large volume of project records that a manual review might
miss. Such drafts risk sounding authoritative while being superficial or simply wrong if not checked against
actual root-cause evidence, exactly the discipline KA 4.2.7 already requires for variance root-cause findings
— an AI-drafted "lesson" must be verified against real project records, never filed as-is. **AI proposes; the
professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 4.1 (the leading-indicator data this lesson is built on); KA 4.3 (the exception-
reporting framework this lesson recommends changing); KA 9.2 (once written — the Sprint Retrospective, the

### 8.5.8 Archiving records

**Definition & purpose.** Archiving is the disciplined retention of project records — contracts and
correspondence, the cost ledger and final cost reconciliation, the change and risk registers, drawings and
as-builts, QA/test records, and the lessons-learned register — in a form the organisation, an auditor, or a
future project can retrieve for as long as it may be needed.

**The underlying principle.** Retention periods are driven by more than convenience: a jurisdiction's statute
of limitations for contract claims, tax and statutory record-keeping requirements (cross-reference Domains 1
and 2), and the DLP/warranty period itself (§8.5.6 — records must outlive the liability they might need to
defend or evidence) all set a floor under how long records must be kept, commonly in the order of six to ten
years for a construction/engineering contract, though the specific period is jurisdiction- and
contract-specific and must be confirmed, not assumed.

**Formula.** No calculation; a minimum record set and retention driver:

`Retain until the LATEST of: (statute of limitations for a contract claim) OR (organisational record-
retention policy) OR (any contract-specified minimum) — never simply "until the project team disbands"`

**Example 8.5.8 — Pipeline Segment 4B's archive index.**

*Scenario.* At closure, the controls lead compiles the final archive index.

| Record category | Example |
|---|---|
| Contractual | Contract, VO-01 instruction, CL-01 settlement correspondence |
| Financial | Cost ledger extract, final cost reconciliation (§8.5.3), retention release record (§8.5.6) |
| Schedule | As-built programme, EOT substantiation (CL-01) |
| Technical | As-built drawings (incl. VO-01's revised P&ID, KA 5.4.7), test/commissioning records |
| Governance | Change register, risk register, lessons-learned entries (§8.5.7) |

*Required.* Confirm the index is complete enough to defend a future query.

*Working/Answer.* Every category a future audit, claim, or defect dispute might reference is represented, and
each item is cross-referenced to the KA/topic where it was generated — meaning a query arriving years later
("why was retention only 5%, and what was deducted?") can be answered directly from the archive without
relying on any individual's memory.

**Mini-case — an archive with a gap.** Two years after closure, a dispute arises over whether VO-01's
schedule impact was properly assessed. The cost and schedule baseline revisions are on file, but the
underlying Level-3 field schedule review that produced the 8-day/3-day float figures (KA 5.4.4) was never
formally archived — it existed only in an estimator's working file, since deleted. Without it, Meridian can
evidence the *conclusion* (5 net working days, $6,000 indirect cost) but not the *analysis* behind it,
materially weakening its position in the dispute.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Archiving conclusions (a final figure) without the underlying working analysis that
supports it (the mini-case). (2) Storing records in a personal or team file structure that becomes
inaccessible once the project team disbands.

**AI in this topic.** Document-classification and retrieval-augmented (RAG-style) tools can index archived
records for natural-language search years after closure, and can flag categories with thin or missing
coverage against a standard checklist like the one above (cross-reference KA 13.4) — valuable for making a
large archive genuinely usable rather than merely stored. Such tools cannot judge whether a given working
file is *evidentially significant enough* to keep — that classification judgement, as the mini-case shows,
is best made at archiving time, not reconstructed years later when it is too late.

**Cross-references.** KA 5.4.4, KA 5.4.7 (the working analysis and configuration records the mini-case shows
must be archived, not just their conclusions); §8.5.7 (the lessons-learned register archived here); Domains 1
and 2 (statutory record-keeping requirements this topic's retention period must satisfy).

### 8.5.9 The post-implementation review and benefits realisation

**Definition & purpose.** A **post-implementation review (PIR)**, conducted shortly after handover, assesses
how well the project was delivered against its own plan — cost, schedule, quality — much of it already
covered by §8.5.3's final cost reconciliation and §8.5.7's lessons learned. A **benefits realisation review**,
conducted later — commonly some months after operational use has stabilised — asks a different question
entirely: did the project actually deliver the *business outcome* its original business case promised? The
APM Body of Knowledge, like PMBOK, distinguishes project **outputs** (what was delivered — cross-reference KA
8.1, once written, for the original business case) from the **benefits** those outputs were meant to enable —
and insists the second is measured, not assumed, once the first is achieved.

**The underlying principle.** A project can be delivered on cost, on schedule and to full acceptance
(§8.5.3, §8.5.5) and still fail to deliver its intended benefit, if the benefit depended on something beyond
physical completion — operational adoption, training, a change in working practice — that the project itself
did not fully secure. Measuring benefits after handover, not just outputs at handover, is how that gap is
caught.

**Formula.** No formula unique to this topic; benefits are measured the same way any performance metric is —
actual outcome compared against the business case's forecast:

`Benefit realisation ratio = Actual measured benefit / Forecast benefit (from the original business case)`

**Example 8.5.9 — VO-01's benefit, twelve months after handover.**

*Scenario.* VO-01's original justification (KA 5.4.4) was to allow future maintenance isolation of Segment
4B without shutting down the full line. The business case forecast this would reduce annual planned-
maintenance downtime by **40 hours/year**, avoiding an illustrative **$1,500/hour** operational-disruption
cost. Twelve months after handover, Meridian's operations liaison obtains the client's actual downtime data:
a measured reduction of **33 hours/year**. Recall from §8.5.5 that operator training for the new valve
chamber (punch-list item 3) was the slowest of the three items to close.

*Required.* (a) The forecast benefit; (b) the actual benefit; (c) the realisation ratio; (d) an explanation.

*Working.*

(a) `Forecast benefit = 40 hours × 1,500/hour = 60,000/year`

(b) `Actual benefit = 33 hours × 1,500/hour = 49,500/year`

(c) `Realisation ratio = 49,500 / 60,000 = 0.825 = 82.5%`

(d) The 7-hour, $10,500 shortfall (17.5%) is attributed to a slower-than-planned operator-training ramp-up
for the new valve chamber, not to any design deficiency in VO-01 itself — a follow-up recommended action,
not a written-off investment.

*Answer.* **Actual benefit = $49,500/year against a $60,000/year forecast — an 82.5% realisation ratio.**

*Interpretation.* This is the point at which §8.5.5's punch-list item 3 stops being a minor documentation
matter and becomes the direct, quantified explanation for a real, if partial, benefits shortfall — precisely
why a benefits review, deferred well beyond handover, catches something a post-implementation review taken
at handover itself cannot: operational adoption takes time to mature, and its cost of not maturing on
schedule is measurable.

**Mini-case — a benefit never checked.** A different Meridian project delivers a new signalling control room
on cost and on schedule, with full client acceptance. No benefits review is ever scheduled, because the
project is considered "successful" at handover. Eighteen months later, an unrelated operations audit finds
the control room's core efficiency benefit — a target reduction in incident response time — was never
achieved, for reasons that trace back to an under-specified staffing plan the project itself never addressed.
Because no review was scheduled, the gap went undetected for a year and a half, and the lesson arrived by
accident rather than by design.

operational-adoption cause, as this topic's worked example correctly does.

**AI in this topic.** Trend-analysis tools can compare post-handover operational KPIs against the business
case's forecast automatically once operational data feeds are connected (cross-reference KA 13.5), and can
flag a benefit tracking materially below forecast for review. Establishing that the project's output actually
*caused* an observed change in operational performance — as opposed to some other operational factor entirely
— remains a professional judgement an automated trend comparison cannot make on its own; correlation in the
data is not the same as the causal link a benefits review must actually establish.

**Cross-references.** KA 8.1 (once written — the original business case and its forecast benefits); §8.5.5

### Key terms

- **Close Project or Phase** — the PMBOK process that formally completes a project or phase: confirming
  deliverables, transferring the product, and finalising every open activity.
- **Normal vs. early/abnormal closure** — closure triggered by achieving objectives, versus closure triggered
  by early termination before objectives are met.
- **Administrative close-out** — confirming all internal project records are complete and consistent with
  what was delivered.
- **Contractual close-out** — formally closing every subcontract/purchase order, resolving all claims, and
  releasing bonds/guarantees once obligations are discharged.
- **Final account** — the commercial reconciliation of a contract's final value, certified payments, retention
  and claim settlements (KA 7.4, once written).
- **Final cost reconciliation** — the project-controls bridge from the final approved BAC to the final actual
  cost, with every variance traced to a documented cause.
- **Punch list / snag list** — the agreed list of minor outstanding items at substantial/practical completion.
- **Substantial (practical) completion vs. final completion** — the point at which a deliverable is fit for
  intended use with minor items outstanding, versus the point at which every outstanding item is closed.
- **Retention** — a percentage of certified contract value withheld as security against defects, released in
  agreed tranches.
- **Defects liability period (DLP)** — the contractually defined period after completion during which
  notified defects must be remedied or may be deducted from retention.
- **Lessons-learned review / retrospective** — a structured, closing (or recurring, in adaptive delivery)
  review capturing observations, root causes and actionable recommendations for reuse.
- **Post-implementation review (PIR)** — an assessment, shortly after handover, of delivery performance
  against plan.
- **Benefits realisation review** — a later assessment of whether the project's original business-case
  benefits were actually achieved in operation.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 8.5.1 [topic 8.5.1, level: recall]**
Under PMBOK, which of the following best distinguishes a normal project closure from an early/abnormal
closure?
A. Normal closure requires a lessons-learned review; early closure does not
B. **Early closure additionally requires settling commitments for scope that will now never be executed and
documenting why the project stopped** ✔
C. Only early closure requires administrative close-out
D. Normal closure never involves contractual close-out
*Rationale:* §8.5.1 states early closure adds work a normal, on-schedule closure does not — settling
commitments for cancelled scope and documenting the reason for termination. A, C and D all wrongly restrict
activities (lessons learned, administrative and contractual close-out) that apply to both closure types.

**MCQ 8.5.2 [topic 8.5.3, level: application — numerical]**
Original BAC is $600,000. During execution, a $5,000 management-reserve transfer and a $38,000 approved
change are both incorporated into the baseline. Final actual cost at completion is $739,250. What is the
final cost variance?
A. $(139,250)
B. **$(96,250)** ✔
C. $(101,250)
D. $(43,000)
*Rationale:* Final approved BAC = 600,000 + 5,000 + 38,000 = 643,000; variance = 643,000 − 739,250 =
(96,250) (Example 8.5.3). A uses the unrevised BAC; C restates only the frozen base-scope component; D
restates only the approved-changes total.

**MCQ 8.5.3 [topic 8.5.6, level: application — numerical]**
Total retention is $37,500, released 50% at substantial completion and 50% at the end of the defects
liability period. A defect notified during the DLP is not remedied within the notice period, and the client
deducts $2,400 from the second release. What is the net second retention release?
A. $18,750
B. **$16,350** ✔
C. $15,000
D. $21,150
*Rationale:* Second release = 50% × 37,500 − 2,400 = 18,750 − 2,400 = 16,350 (Example 8.5.6). A omits the
deduction; C and D are arithmetic distractors.

**MCQ 8.5.4 [topic 8.5.2, level: analysis]**
A claim originally valued at $18,000 is settled at close-out for $10,800. How should this settlement be
treated in the project's final cost reconciliation (§8.5.3)?
A. Added to final actual cost as an adverse cost-performance item
B. Deducted from the final approved BAC
C. **Excluded from the cost-performance reconciliation and recognised instead in the commercial final
account (KA 7.4)** ✔
D. Averaged with the original claimed value before reconciling
*Rationale:* §8.5.2/§8.5.3 distinguish a commercial recovery (what Meridian recovers from the client) from a
cost-efficiency measure (how efficiently Meridian executed its own scope) — the settlement belongs in the
final account, not the BAC-vs-actual-cost bridge. A, B and D all improperly fold a commercial item into the
cost-performance reconciliation.

**MCQ 8.5.5 [topic 8.5.9, level: application — numerical]**
A business case forecasts an annual benefit of 40 hours of avoided downtime at $1,500/hour. Twelve months
after handover, actual avoided downtime is measured at 33 hours. What is the benefit realisation ratio?
A. 75.0%
B. 80.0%
C. **82.5%** ✔
D. 90.0%
*Rationale:* Realisation ratio = 33/40 = 0.825 = 82.5% (Example 8.5.9); equivalently $49,500/$60,000. A, B and
D are plausible but incorrect rounding/arithmetic distractors.

**MCQ 8.5.6 [topic 8.5.5, level: analysis]**
At a client inspection, three items remain outstanding: a cosmetic coating touch-up, a missing as-built
drawing, and pending operator training — none of which prevents safe, intended use of the asset. What should
happen next?
A. Reject acceptance until all three items are closed
B. **Certify substantial completion, and log all three items on the punch list against agreed closure
deadlines** ✔
C. Certify final completion immediately, since the items are minor
D. Delay any certificate until final completion, whichever comes first
*Rationale:* §8.5.5 states substantial completion is normal with a documented punch list of minor,
non-critical items, provided none affects safe/intended use — exactly this scenario (Example 8.5.5). A is
overly conservative; C conflates substantial and final completion; D is not a defined process step.

**Answers**

1. The Month-5 CV of $(101,250) measured cost performance on the original scope only, against the original
   BAC. The final variance of $(96,250) differs from it by two adjustments introduced after Month 5: a

   risk realisation already embedded in actual cost, and a $0 net effect from VO-01, which was delivered
   exactly at its approved estimate and so adds equally to both BAC and actual cost. `(101,250) − 5,000 − 0 =

2. A settled claim is a commercial recovery — it changes what Meridian is entitled to recover from the client

   reconciliation would conflate two different questions (delivery efficiency versus contractual

3. A specific, actionable entry names the exact evidence (RFI ageing 6→18 days; submittal approval 96%→82%,

   leading-indicator thresholds, owned by the Project Controls Manager) — as opposed to a generic statement

4. (a) First release = 50% × 50,000 = **$25,000**. (b) Net second release = 50% × 50,000 − 3,000 = 25,000 −
   3,000 = **$22,000**. (c) Check: 25,000 + 22,000 + 3,000 = **$50,000** ✓, reconciling exactly to total
