# Domain 4 — Performance Management, Variance Analysis & Management Reporting

## KA 4.2 — Variance analysis

KA 4.1 established the general vocabulary of performance management — KPIs, targets, thresholds and
tolerances. This KA puts that vocabulary to work on the single technique a project controls professional
uses more than any other: **variance analysis** — the systematic comparison of what was planned, budgeted
or standard against what actually happened, to isolate *how much* performance deviated and, critically,
*why*. Domain 6 (Earned Value Management) already introduced two variances — Cost Variance (CV) and
Schedule Variance (SV) — and this KA does not repeat their derivation. Instead it treats CV as a starting
point: CV tells a controls professional *that* a cost overrun exists on the work performed to date, but it
cannot say, on its own, whether the overrun came from paying more than expected for materials and labour, or
from using/consuming more of them than expected. The **management-accounting variance model** — a
long-established technique taught throughout the ACCA and CIMA management-accounting syllabi and used
throughout AACE International's Total Cost Management (TCM) Framework for cost control — supplies that
missing decomposition. This KA works through the price/rate and quantity/usage/efficiency split for
materials and labour, a fixed-overhead spend/volume split, root-cause analysis, materiality thresholds, and
the variance bridge (waterfall) that turns a page of numbers into a defensible corrective-action plan. The
running illustration continues **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**'s **Pipeline Segment 4B** (BAC $600,000 /
SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1), the entity and project already carried through Domains 1,
3 and 6, so notation and figures stay identical across the book.

### 4.2.1 Purpose, scope and the favourable/adverse sign convention

**Definition & purpose.** Variance analysis is the discipline of comparing an actual result against a
pre-agreed reference point — a budget, a standard cost, a schedule baseline, or an earned-value measure — and
expressing the difference as a **variance**: a signed, quantified gap that becomes the trigger for
investigation and, where warranted, corrective action. In project controls it operates at two levels that
this KA treats in sequence: the **project/earned-value level** (CV, SV — recapped in 4.2.2, already derived
in full in KA 6.2) and the **cost-element level** (materials, labour, overhead — the core content of this
KA, 4.2.4–4.2.6), which decomposes a cost-element variance into its price/rate and quantity/usage/efficiency
components.

**The underlying principle.** Variance analysis exists because a single "over/under budget" figure is not
actionable. Management accounting formalises **management by exception**: rather than scrutinising every
transaction, a controls function sets a standard or budget once, measures the actual result, and directs its
limited investigative effort toward variances that are large enough to matter (4.2.8) and that a decomposed
analysis (4.2.3–4.2.6) can trace to a specific, correctable cause (4.2.7). A variance is a diagnostic signal,
not a verdict — it says *where* to look, not automatically *why* or *whose fault*.

**The sign convention.** Throughout this KA, a cost variance is calculated as:

`Variance = Standard/budgeted cost of the output achieved − Actual cost incurred`

A **positive** result is **favourable (F)** — the work cost less than its standard/budgeted value. A
**negative** result is **adverse (A)** — the work cost more than its standard/budgeted value. This is the
same direction as CV (`CV = EV − AC`, KA 6.2.1): both place the *budgeted/earned* figure first and the
*actual* figure second, so a positive number is always good news and a negative number always bad news
throughout this KA and Domain 6 alike — one consistent convention across the whole book. Per the style
convention used throughout this book (§4 of the style spine), adverse variances are shown in prose as
"adverse" or "(A)" and, in tables, in parentheses — e.g. an adverse variance of $1,200 is shown as **$(1,200)**.

**Example 4.2.1 — reading the sign convention.**

*Scenario.* A work package has a standard/budgeted cost for the output actually achieved of $50,000. Actual
cost incurred was $53,000.

*Required.* The variance, and whether it is favourable or adverse.

*Working.* `Variance = 50,000 − 53,000 = (3,000)`

*Answer.* **Variance = $(3,000), adverse** (SAR (11,250) at the illustrative rate).

*Interpretation.* The work cost $3,000 more than its budgeted value — an adverse outcome requiring
investigation under 4.2.7–4.2.8. This single figure cannot yet say whether the cause lies in price, quantity,
both, or something else entirely; that decomposition is exactly what 4.2.3 onward provides.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating "the variance" as a single number without first checking which direction is favourable — a
  reversed sign turns good news into an apparent crisis, or vice versa.
- Stopping at stage 3 (calculating the variance) and never reaching stages 5–6 — a calculated but
  uninvestigated, unactioned variance has no management value.
- Assuming "adverse" always means "someone made a mistake" — an adverse variance can equally arise from a
  standard that was wrong in the first place (cross-ref KA 3.2), which is itself a valid and important
  finding.

**AI in this topic.** A dashboarding tool can calculate and refresh variances the moment source data updates,
removing the manual recalculation step entirely (cross-reference KA 4.4, KA 13.5). It has no way to judge
*materiality* (4.2.8) or *causation* (4.2.7) on its own — an automatically calculated variance is stage 3 of
the loop only; a human must still decide whether it is worth investigating and what, if anything, to do about
it. **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Cross-references.** KA 4.1 (KPIs, targets, thresholds); KA 6.2 (CV, SV — the project-level variances this
KA decomposes further); KA 3.2 (how standards/estimates are built in the first place); KA 5.2 (the cost
control cycle that supplies actual data); KA 8.4 (variance analysis within Monitoring & Controlling).

### 4.2.2 Cost and schedule variance in EVM: CV, SV, CPI, SPI and their link to management-accounting analysis

**Definition & purpose.** This topic is a deliberately brief bridge, not a re-derivation. KA 6.2 defines and
fully works Cost Variance (`CV = EV − AC`), Schedule Variance (`SV = EV − PV`), the Cost Performance Index
(`CPI = EV/AC`) and the Schedule Performance Index (`SPI = EV/PV`); nothing in that derivation is repeated
here. This topic's job is narrower: to show precisely what CV *can* and *cannot* tell a controls professional
on its own, and to hand off to 4.2.3 the question CV cannot answer.

**The underlying principle.** CV is, in management-accounting terms, a **total cost variance** for the work
performed: it nets together every possible cause of a cost difference — price paid, quantity/hours consumed,
overhead absorption, even measurement-method effects (KA 6.1.4) — into one signed number. That is CV's
strength (a single, comparable, project-wide figure) and also its limit: two work packages can show
identical CV for completely different underlying reasons — one from paying more per unit, the other from
using more units — and CV alone cannot distinguish them. The price/quantity decomposition in 4.2.3 onward is
what management accounting adds specifically to answer *why*, once EVM has already answered *how much* and
*whether it matters* (via SV/SPI, for the schedule dimension).

**Example 4.2.2 — EVM CV/SV interpretation: Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5.**

*Scenario.* Continuing the Month-5 Pipeline Segment 4B data already established in KA 6.3 (Examples 6.3.2–
6.3.4): BAC $600,000; PV $540,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250.

*Required.* CV, SV, CPI and SPI at Month 5 (recomputed here only as a check, per this book's rule that every
figure used must be shown to add up); the health-quadrant classification (KA 6.2.5); and what management
accounting can add beyond this result.

*Working.*
`CV = EV − AC = 405,000 − 506,250 = (101,250)`
`SV = EV − PV = 405,000 − 540,000 = (135,000)`
`CPI = EV / AC = 405,000 / 506,250 = 0.80`
`SPI = EV / PV = 405,000 / 540,000 = 0.75`

*Answer.* **CV = $(101,250), adverse** (SAR (379,688), rounded) — equivalent to a 25.0% cost overrun relative
to the value of work performed (`101,250 / 405,000 = 0.25`, consistent with `CPI = 0.80` since `AC = EV/0.80 =
1.25 × EV`). **SV = $(135,000), adverse** (SAR (506,250)) — equivalent to a 25.0% shortfall relative to the
value of work planned (`135,000 / 540,000 = 0.25`, consistent with `SPI = 0.75`).

*Interpretation.* With CPI 0.80 (< 1.00, adverse) and SPI 0.75 (< 1.00, adverse), Pipeline Segment 4B sits in
the **Q4 "highest risk" quadrant** of KA 6.2.5's health matrix: behind schedule and over budget
simultaneously — the combination that KA 6.2.5 flags for immediate escalation. CV confirms *that* $101,250 of
adverse cost performance exists on the work done to date, and SV confirms *that* the project is materially
behind its earned-value schedule — but neither number, by itself, tells the controls team whether the cost
overrun is because materials and labour cost more per unit than budgeted, because more materials and labour
hours were consumed than budgeted for the output achieved, or both. 4.2.4–4.2.6 work through exactly that
decomposition for the cost elements behind this account — materials, labour and overhead — using the
management-accounting price/quantity model.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting CV alone as a complete explanation of cost performance — CV answers "how much," not "why."
- Assuming a favourable CV means no further analysis is needed — a favourable total CV can still mask an
  adverse price variance offset by a larger favourable usage variance (or the reverse), each requiring a
  different management response (illustrated numerically in 4.2.4's mini-case).
- Confusing this KA's cost-*element* variances (materials, labour, overhead — a sub-account-level analysis of
  *why*) with KA 6.2's project-level CV/SV (an earned-value-based measure of *how much and how urgent*) —
  they are complementary, not competing, techniques.

**AI in this topic.** As covered in KA 6.2, automated tools can compute and trend CV/SV continuously and
raise a threshold-based alert (cross-reference KA 4.4, KA 13.5). Such a tool can flag *that* a Q4 condition
exists; it cannot determine *why* without the further price/quantity decomposition this KA supplies, and a
plausible-sounding AI-drafted narrative that stops at "the project is over budget and behind schedule"
without that decomposition is incomplete, not wrong — the professional must ensure the deeper analysis is
actually performed before corrective action is decided.

### 4.2.3 The price/quantity decomposition principle: standard cost, the flexed budget and splitting a total variance

**Definition & purpose.** This topic gives the general mathematical model that 4.2.4 (materials), 4.2.5
(labour) and 4.2.6 (overhead) each apply to a specific cost element. The model rests on a single idea: any
cost is the product of a **quantity of input** and a **price per unit of input** (materials: kilograms ×
price per kilogram; labour: hours × rate per hour), so any difference between a standard/budgeted cost and an
actual cost can always be split cleanly into a **price/rate effect** and a **quantity/usage/efficiency
effect**, with no remainder.

**The underlying principle — the standard cost card and the flexed budget.** Before any period begins, a
**standard cost** is set for each unit of output: a standard **quantity** of input per unit (e.g. 2.5 tonnes
of bedding material per metre of trench) and a standard **price** per unit of input (e.g. $40 per tonne),
documented on a standard cost card built from the estimating disciplines of KA 3.2. Once the *actual* output
achieved in a period is known, that standard is **flexed** — scaled to the actual output level — to give the
fair comparison point: not "what did we originally budget for the whole project" but "what *should* this
much output have cost, at standard rates." Comparing the actual cost against this flexed standard, rather
than against the original fixed budget, is what makes the variance a fair measure of *efficiency*, not merely
of *volume* (a work package that does more work than planned should cost more in total — that is not, by
itself, a performance problem).

**Formula(e).** For any input with **standard quantity allowed for the actual output achieved (SQ)**,
**standard price per unit (SP)**, **actual quantity used (AQ)** and **actual price per unit (AP)**:

`Standard cost of actual output = SQ × SP`
`Actual cost = AQ × AP`
`Total variance = (SQ × SP) − (AQ × AP)`

This splits, without approximation, into:

`Price/rate variance = (SP − AP) × AQ`
`Quantity/usage/efficiency variance = (SQ − AQ) × SP`
`Total variance = Price/rate variance + Quantity/usage/efficiency variance`

*Why the split is exact.* Adding and subtracting `AQ × SP`:
`(SQ×SP) − (AQ×AP) = SP×(SQ−AQ) + AQ×(SP−AP)`
— the quantity effect is priced at the **standard** price (isolating the volume of input consumed from any
price movement), and the price effect is measured on the **actual** quantity (isolating the rate paid from
any volume movement). Because the two terms are derived algebraically from the same starting identity, they
always sum, exactly, back to the total variance — this is what "reconciled" means throughout the worked
examples in 4.2.4–4.2.6, and is a mechanical check that should always be performed, not assumed.

**Quick-reference — the three cost elements covered in this KA.**

| Cost element | Quantity measure | Price measure | Price/rate variance | Quantity/usage/efficiency variance | Worked in |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Materials | Quantity used (kg, tonnes, m³, units) | Price per unit of material | Materials price variance | Materials usage variance | 4.2.4 |
| Labour | Hours worked | Rate per hour | Labour rate variance | Labour efficiency variance | 4.2.5 |
| Fixed overhead | Activity/absorption base (e.g. labour hours) | Overhead absorption rate | Expenditure (spend) variance | Volume variance | 4.2.6 |

**Example 4.2.3 — confirming the split reconciles, on simple numbers.**

*Scenario.* A generic input has SQ = 100 units, SP = $10/unit, AQ = 90 units, AP = $11/unit.

*Required.* Standard cost, actual cost, total variance, price variance, quantity variance, and confirmation
that the two sub-variances reconcile to the total.

*Working.*
`Standard cost = SQ × SP = 100 × 10 = 1,000`
`Actual cost = AQ × AP = 90 × 11 = 990`
`Total variance = 1,000 − 990 = 10` (favourable — cost less than standard, in total)
`Price variance = (SP − AP) × AQ = (10 − 11) × 90 = (90)` (adverse — paid $1 more per unit on all 90 units used)
`Quantity variance = (SQ − AQ) × SP = (100 − 90) × 10 = 100` (favourable — used 10 fewer units than standard allowed, valued at standard price)
`Check: Price variance + Quantity variance = (90) + 100 = 10` ✓ matches the total variance exactly.

*Answer.* **Total variance = $10, favourable**, composed of a **$(90) adverse price variance** and a **$100
favourable quantity variance**.

*Interpretation.* A favourable *total* variance here hides an adverse price effect entirely offset, and then
some, by a favourable quantity effect — precisely the situation flagged as a pitfall in 4.2.2: reporting only
the total would miss that unit prices rose. This is the general pattern 4.2.4–4.2.6 now apply to Pipeline
Segment 4B's real cost elements.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Flexing the wrong output level — using the *original* budgeted output rather than the *actual* output
  achieved produces a variance contaminated by a volume effect that has nothing to do with efficiency.
- Reversing which quantity/price is used in which sub-variance — the price variance must use the **actual**
  quantity (AQ), and the quantity variance must use the **standard** price (SP); using the wrong one in
  either formula breaks the algebraic reconciliation and silently double-counts or drops part of the
  variance.
- Applying this simple two-way split to a genuinely **mixed** input (e.g. a work package that blends two
  grades of material or two labour skill levels) without first separating a **mix variance** — a further
  decomposition beyond this introductory KA's scope, flagged here so it is not mistaken for an omission.

**AI in this topic.** A spreadsheet or ERP-integrated AI assistant can build a standard cost card from
historical estimating data and mechanically apply the price/quantity split formulae the instant actual
quantities and prices are posted (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.5), removing manual calculation entirely.
It cannot judge whether the standard itself (SQ, SP) remains realistic — an out-of-date standard, never
revised after a genuine change in material specification or labour productivity, will generate spurious
variances every period regardless of how accurately the arithmetic is automated; keeping standards current is
a professional judgement, not a calculation.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2 (cost estimation — how SQ and SP are originally built); KA 1.5, KA 5.3 (cost
coding and control accounts that supply AQ/AP); KA 4.2.4–4.2.6 (the three applications of this model); KA
5.1 (cost drivers and allocation, relevant to the overhead application in 4.2.6).

### 4.2.4 Materials variances: price variance and usage variance

**Definition & purpose.** The **materials price variance (MPV)** measures the effect of paying more or less
than the standard price per unit of material purchased/used. The **materials usage variance (MUV)** measures
the effect of consuming more or less material than the standard allows for the output actually achieved.
Together they decompose the total materials cost variance for a work package or control account.

**The underlying principle.** Materials cost is the clearest application of 4.2.3's general model: quantity
(kilograms, tonnes, cubic metres, or discrete units) multiplied by price per unit. The two variances are
frequently, and informatively, **linked**: a buyer who secures a lower price by switching to a cheaper or
lower-specification source may simultaneously trigger higher wastage or over-consumption on site — a
favourable price variance bought at the cost of an adverse usage variance, and often a worse net outcome than
paying standard price for the specified material. Reading the two together, not in isolation, is essential.

**Formula(e).**
`MPV = (SP − AP) × AQ` — SP, AP in $/unit of material; AQ in units of material.
`MUV = (SQ − AQ) × SP` — SQ, AQ in units of material; SP in $/unit.
`Total materials variance = MPV + MUV`

**Example 4.2.4 — Materials price and usage variance: bedding/backfill material, Pipeline Segment 4B trench
work package (WP3), Month 3.**

*Scenario.* WP3, Trenching & earthworks, excavated 900 metres of trench by Month 3 (the physical progress
figure already established in KA 6.1.4's Example 6.1.4). The trench requires granular bedding/backfill
material around the pipe at a standard usage rate of 2.5 tonnes per metre, at a standard price of $40.00 per
tonne. Actual procurement and site records for the 900 m of completed trench show 2,400 tonnes of bedding
material purchased and used, at an actual price of $38.00 per tonne.

*Required.* MPV, MUV, the total materials variance, and confirmation that the two reconcile.

*Working.*
`SQ = 900 m × 2.5 t/m = 2,250 tonnes`
`Standard cost of actual output = SQ × SP = 2,250 × 40 = 90,000`
`Actual cost = AQ × AP = 2,400 × 38 = 91,200`
`MPV = (SP − AP) × AQ = (40 − 38) × 2,400 = 4,800`
`MUV = (SQ − AQ) × SP = (2,250 − 2,400) × 40 = (150) × 40 = (6,000)`
`Check: MPV + MUV = 4,800 + (6,000) = (1,200)`, and `90,000 − 91,200 = (1,200)` ✓ reconciles exactly.

*Answer.* **MPV = $4,800, favourable** (SAR 18,000). **MUV = $(6,000), adverse** (SAR (22,500)). **Total
materials variance = $(1,200), adverse** (SAR (4,500)) (standard $90,000/SAR 337,500; actual $91,200/SAR
342,000).

*Interpretation.* The buying team secured material $2.00/tonne cheaper than standard on all 2,400 tonnes
purchased — a genuinely favourable price outcome worth $4,800. But 150 tonnes more than the standard 2,250-
tonne allowance were consumed for the same 900 m of trench, an adverse usage effect of $6,000 that more than
offsets the price saving. The **net** effect is a small adverse total variance of $1,200 — a materially
different, and more actionable, finding than either the price variance or the usage variance would suggest
alone.

**Mini-case — root cause: the cheaper-material/higher-wastage pattern.** Investigation (following the
structured approach of 4.2.7) finds that the $38/tonne material came from an alternative supplier with a
higher fines/moisture content than the specified bedding grade. The material compacted less predictably in
the trench, requiring additional backfill passes and producing greater material loss to over-excavation and
spillage — explaining both the favourable price (a genuinely cheaper source) and the adverse usage (more of
it was needed to achieve a properly compacted bed) from a single root cause. This is one of the most common
patterns in materials variance analysis: a favourable price variance and an adverse usage variance are not
always independent events, and a decision to change supplier or specification purely to chase a favourable
MPV can produce a worse net outcome, as it does here.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting MPV alone as "good news" (favourable) without checking MUV — the mini-case above shows why this
  can mask a worse underlying problem.
- Computing MPV on the standard quantity (SQ) instead of the actual quantity (AQ) purchased — the price
  effect must be measured on what was actually bought, not what should have been bought.
- Failing to distinguish a genuine usage/efficiency problem (wastage, rework, theft, poor handling) from a
  **specification change** that was never fed back into the standard cost card (cross-ref KA 3.2, KA 5.4) —
  the latter is a standard-setting issue, not a site-performance issue, and calls for a different corrective
  action.

**AI in this topic.** RPA and AI-assisted reconciliation tools can extract AQ and AP directly from purchase
orders, goods-received notes and supplier invoices, and compute MPV and MUV automatically the moment actual
data is posted (cross-reference KA 5.2, KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — a substantial speed gain over manual extraction
from the cost ledger. It cannot inspect the material actually delivered to site, confirm it met specification,
or determine whether the usage variance reflects genuine wastage versus a legitimate specification difference
— those determinations require a site-based or quality-based verification the data alone cannot supply.

### 4.2.5 Labour variances: rate variance and efficiency variance

**Definition & purpose.** The **labour rate variance (LRV)** measures the effect of paying more or less per
hour than the standard wage rate. The **labour efficiency variance (LEV)** measures the effect of taking more
or fewer hours than the standard allows for the output actually achieved. Together they decompose the total
labour cost variance, applying exactly the same price/quantity logic as 4.2.4, with hours in place of material
quantity and the wage rate in place of unit price.

**The underlying principle.** Labour rate movements are typically driven by workforce mix (using a more
senior, higher-paid resource than planned), overtime/shift premiums, or wage escalation. Labour efficiency
movements are typically driven by productivity — crew experience, working conditions, rework, or genuine
process improvement. As with materials, the two are often causally linked: authorising overtime to protect a
schedule (a deliberate, often justified, rate premium) can itself contribute to fatigue-related errors and
rework, producing an adverse efficiency variance from the same underlying schedule-recovery decision.

**Formula(e).**
`LRV = (SR − AR) × AH` — SR, AR in $/hour; AH in hours.
`LEV = (SH − AH) × SR` — SH, AH in hours; SR in $/hour.
`Total labour variance = LRV + LEV`

**Example 4.2.5 — Labour rate and efficiency variance: pipe-welding labour, Pipeline Segment 4B (WP4), Month
5.**

*Scenario.* WP4, Pipe-laying & welding, includes a "Weld" milestone (KA 6.1.4). By Month 5, welding activity
has progressed and 480 metres of pipe have been welded, against a standard labour productivity rate of 0.5
hours per metre and a standard wage rate of $45.00 per hour. Because the Month-5 schedule position is
materially behind plan (SPI 0.75, Example 4.2.2), the welding crew was authorised to work extended shifts at
an overtime premium to protect the critical path (cross-reference KA 6.3.4's Method 3 rationale for exactly
this kind of schedule-driven cost pressure). Timesheets show 260 actual hours worked at an actual average
rate of $48.00 per hour.

*Required.* LRV, LEV, the total labour variance, and confirmation that the two reconcile.

*Working.*
`SH = 480 m × 0.5 hr/m = 240 hours`
`Standard cost of actual output = SH × SR = 240 × 45 = 10,800`
`Actual cost = AH × AR = 260 × 48 = 12,480`
`LRV = (SR − AR) × AH = (45 − 48) × 260 = (3) × 260 = (780)`
`LEV = (SH − AH) × SR = (240 − 260) × 45 = (20) × 45 = (900)`
`Check: LRV + LEV = (780) + (900) = (1,680)`, and `10,800 − 12,480 = (1,680)` ✓ reconciles exactly.

*Answer.* **LRV = $(780), adverse** (SAR (2,925)). **LEV = $(900), adverse** (SAR (3,375)). **Total labour
variance = $(1,680), adverse** (SAR (6,300)) (standard $10,800/SAR 40,500; actual $12,480/SAR 46,800).

*Interpretation.* Both sub-variances are adverse here, unlike the materials example — the overtime premium
paid ($3.00/hour above standard on all 260 hours) cost $780, and the crew also took 20 hours longer than the
240-hour standard allowance, costing a further $900. Unlike 4.2.4's offsetting pattern, this is a case where
price and quantity effects **compound** rather than partially cancel — the schedule-recovery decision has
produced a cost overrun on both dimensions of the labour cost.

**Mini-case — root cause: schedule pressure driving both variances from one decision.** Investigation traces
both adverse variances to a single root cause: the schedule-recovery overtime authorised in response to the
Month-5 SPI shortfall (Example 4.2.2) not only cost more per hour (LRV) but also produced a higher rate of
weld defects failing non-destructive testing (NDT) on the extended shifts, requiring re-welds that consumed
the additional 20 hours (LEV). This is the reverse of 4.2.4's independent-causes pattern: here, one management
decision (authorise overtime to protect schedule) is the common driver of both cost-element variances,
illustrating why root-cause analysis (4.2.7) must sometimes look *across* variance categories, not just
within one.

Chart type: top-down variance tree diagram, structurally identical to Fig 4.2.4. Root box: "Total labour

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating LRV and LEV as always independent, unrelated causes — the mini-case shows a single decision can
  drive both, and a corrective action addressing only one (e.g. reversing the overtime without fixing the
  rework cause) will not resolve the total variance.
- Computing LRV on standard hours (SH) instead of actual hours (AH) worked — as with materials, the rate
  effect must be priced on what was actually worked.
- Using Level of Effort (LOE)-measured labour (KA 6.1.4) in this kind of price/quantity analysis without
  adjustment — LOE credits value by elapsed time regardless of physical output, so "standard hours for the
  output achieved" is not a meaningful concept for LOE labour; this decomposition applies to labour measured
  against a discrete, quantifiable output (as WP4's welding is here), not to LOE packages like WP5 supervision.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted timesheet and payroll-extraction tools can pull AH and AR directly from time
capture and payroll systems and compute LRV/LEV automatically (cross-reference KA 5.2, KA 13.4, KA 13.5),
removing manual data assembly. It cannot verify that hours logged reflect genuine productive welding time
rather than idle time, rework, or miscoded activity — timesheet data quality issues are a common, easily
overlooked source of a spurious efficiency variance, and confirming data integrity before trusting an
automatically computed LEV remains the professional's responsibility.

### 4.2.6 Fixed overhead variances: expenditure (spend) variance and volume variance

**Definition & purpose.** Fixed overhead — site establishment, temporary facilities, site management and
supervision infrastructure, insurance and similar indirect costs that do not vary directly with each unit of
output — is recovered into project cost via an **overhead absorption rate (FOAR)**, typically expressed per
direct labour hour (or another activity base, cross-reference KA 5.1). The **fixed overhead expenditure
(spend) variance** measures whether the overhead itself was spent within its period budget. The **fixed
overhead volume variance** measures the effect of the actual activity level (e.g. labour hours worked)
differing from the budgeted activity level, and therefore recovering more or less overhead into cost than
planned, at the standard rate.

**The underlying principle.** Overhead is not purchased per unit the way materials are, so its variance model
is not a literal price × quantity split, but a closely analogous one: the **expenditure variance** is the
"price-like" effect (did the total overhead cost come in on budget?), and the **volume variance** is the
"quantity-like" effect (did the activity that overhead is spread across happen at the planned rate?). Because
the overhead budget is fixed in total (it does not flex with activity the way materials and labour do), a
shortfall in activity — for example, fewer productive labour hours than planned, itself often a symptom of
schedule slippage — means less overhead is absorbed into the value of work done, even if the overhead itself
was spent exactly as budgeted.

**Formula(e).** With **budgeted fixed overhead (BFOH)**, **budgeted activity/absorption base (BH)**,
**standard/fixed overhead absorption rate `FOAR = BFOH / BH`** ($/hour), **actual activity (AH)** and **actual
fixed overhead incurred (AFOH)**:

`Overhead absorbed = AH × FOAR`
`Expenditure (spend) variance = BFOH − AFOH`
`Volume variance = (AH − BH) × FOAR`
`Total fixed overhead variance = Overhead absorbed − AFOH = Expenditure variance + Volume variance`

*Why the split reconciles.* `(AH×FOAR) − AFOH = (AH×FOAR − BH×FOAR) + (BH×FOAR − AFOH) = (AH−BH)×FOAR +
(BFOH − AFOH)`, since `BH × FOAR = BFOH` exactly by the definition of FOAR — the same additive-identity logic
as 4.2.3, applied to a fixed cost recovered on an activity base rather than to a purchased input.

**Example 4.2.6 — Fixed overhead expenditure and volume variance: Pipeline Segment 4B site overheads, a
reporting period.**

*Scenario.* Site overhead for Pipeline Segment 4B is recovered on a direct-labour-hour basis. The period
budget is $60,000 of fixed site overhead against a budgeted 3,000 direct labour hours, giving a standard
absorption rate of `FOAR = 60,000 / 3,000 = $20.00` per direct labour hour. Actual results for the period:
2,800 direct labour hours actually worked (below budget, consistent with the schedule slippage already seen
in Example 4.2.2), and $64,000 of actual fixed overhead incurred.

*Required.* Overhead absorbed, expenditure variance, volume variance, total fixed overhead variance, and
confirmation that the sub-variances reconcile.

*Working.*
`FOAR = 60,000 / 3,000 = 20.00`
`Overhead absorbed = AH × FOAR = 2,800 × 20 = 56,000`
`Expenditure variance = BFOH − AFOH = 60,000 − 64,000 = (4,000)`
`Volume variance = (AH − BH) × FOAR = (2,800 − 3,000) × 20 = (200) × 20 = (4,000)`
`Check: Expenditure variance + Volume variance = (4,000) + (4,000) = (8,000)`, and `Overhead absorbed − AFOH =
56,000 − 64,000 = (8,000)` ✓ reconciles exactly.

*Answer.* **Expenditure variance = $(4,000), adverse** (SAR (15,000)). **Volume variance = $(4,000), adverse**
(SAR (15,000)). **Total fixed overhead variance = $(8,000), adverse** (SAR (30,000)) (budgeted $60,000/SAR
225,000; actual $64,000/SAR 240,000).

*Interpretation.* Overhead itself overran its period budget by $4,000 (expenditure variance — e.g. higher
site security or temporary-facility costs than planned) *and* 200 fewer labour hours than budgeted were
worked, so $4,000 less overhead was absorbed into the value of work done than the budget intended (volume
variance). The two adverse effects happen to be numerically equal here, but arise from entirely different
causes: one from overspending on overhead itself, the other from an activity shortfall that echoes the same
schedule slippage already identified via SPI (Example 4.2.2) and the labour efficiency variance (Example
4.2.5) — a third symptom of the same underlying schedule problem, reached by a third, independent analytical
route.

**Fig 4.2.6 — Fixed overhead variance tree: total fixed overhead variance, Pipeline Segment 4B.**
Chart type: top-down variance tree diagram, structurally consistent with Figs 4.2.4–4.2.5. Root box: "Total
fixed overhead variance $(8,000) adverse," brand blue `#1D4ED8` outline. Two child boxes, both amber-outlined
(both adverse): "Expenditure (spend) variance $(4,000) adverse" and "Volume variance $(4,000) adverse."

*Underlying data:* BFOH $60,000; BH 3,000 hr; FOAR $20/hr; AH 2,800 hr; AFOH $64,000; overhead absorbed
$56,000; expenditure variance $(4,000) A; volume variance $(4,000) A; total $(8,000) A.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Choosing an absorption base that does not genuinely drive the overhead cost (e.g. absorbing on labour hours
  when the overhead is actually driven by calendar time or by equipment usage) — this is a cost-accounting
  policy judgement (cross-ref KA 5.1) that, if wrong, generates a persistent, misleading volume variance every
  period regardless of true performance.
- Treating an adverse volume variance as a "spending" problem and directing corrective action at the overhead
  budget, when its actual cause is an activity/schedule shortfall — the corrective action for a volume
  variance is schedule recovery, not overhead cost-cutting.
- Applying this fixed-overhead model to a cost that is genuinely **variable** with output — variable overheads
  use a different, output-flexed analysis, not the fixed-overhead expenditure/volume split shown here.

**AI in this topic.** A BI or ERP-integrated tool can compute overhead absorption and both sub-variances
automatically across many control accounts or cost centres, refreshed as actual hours and overhead postings
arrive (cross-reference KA 4.4, KA 13.5) — valuable at portfolio scale, where recalculating dozens of accounts
manually every period is impractical. It cannot judge whether the chosen absorption base remains appropriate,
nor distinguish a genuine overhead-spend problem from an activity-driven volume effect without the kind of
cross-reference to schedule performance shown in the interpretation above — that synthesis remains a
professional judgement.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.2.3 (the general model, applied here to a recovered rather than purchased cost); KA
5.1 (cost drivers, allocation and absorption in depth); KA 4.2.2 (the SPI/schedule-slippage link to the volume
variance's cause); KA 4.2.5 (the parallel labour efficiency finding from the same root cause).

### 4.2.7 Root-cause analysis: from variance to explanation

**Definition & purpose.** A calculated, reconciled variance (4.2.4–4.2.6) identifies *where* a cost problem
lies and roughly *how large* it is; it does not, by itself, explain *why*. Root-cause analysis is the
structured process of tracing a variance back to its underlying, correctable cause, using established
management techniques rather than guesswork.

**The underlying principle.** Two complementary techniques, both long established in quality- and
performance-management practice, are commonly applied to project-controls variances:

- **The 5 Whys** — starting from the observed variance, repeatedly asking "why?" until a root cause is
  reached that is specific and actionable (typically after three to five iterations), rather than stopping at
  the first, superficial explanation.
- **The cause-and-effect (fishbone/Ishikawa) diagram** — organising candidate causes into standard categories
  (commonly Materials, Method, Manpower, Machine, Measurement — the "6Ms," sometimes with a sixth,
  Mother Nature/environment, added) to ensure a variance is investigated systematically across all plausible
  cause categories rather than jumping to the first hypothesis that comes to mind.

Neither technique is unique to project controls — both are general management/quality-improvement tools — but
both apply directly and usefully to a cost or schedule variance once it has been isolated by the calculations
in 4.2.4–4.2.6.

**Mini-case — 5 Whys applied to the WP4 labour efficiency variance (Example 4.2.5).**

| Step | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Why was the labour efficiency variance adverse ($(900))? | 20 more welding hours were needed than the 240-hour standard allowed. |
| 2 | Why were 20 extra hours needed? | A higher-than-normal rate of welds failed non-destructive testing (NDT) and required re-welding. |
| 3 | Why did more welds fail NDT? | Weld quality declined on the extended overtime shifts. |
| 4 | Why did quality decline on overtime shifts? | Crew fatigue on shifts extended beyond the normal working day. |
| 5 | Why were extended shifts worked? | Overtime was authorised to recover schedule after the Month-5 SPI shortfall (Example 4.2.2). |

*Root cause reached:* the labour efficiency variance is a **second-order consequence of a schedule-recovery
decision**, not a first-order productivity or workmanship failure. This changes the corrective action
materially (4.2.9): rather than simply retraining the welding crew, the more effective fix addresses the
schedule root cause itself and/or the fatigue-management controls around authorised overtime (e.g. capping
consecutive extended shifts, adding a second crew instead of extending hours) — directly linking this
variance's true fix back to the schedule performance already identified via SPI in Example 4.2.2, and
avoiding a corrective action aimed at the wrong level of the causal chain.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Stopping at the first "why," which almost always identifies a symptom (rework, wastage) rather than a root
  cause (a decision, a resourcing constraint, an environmental factor).
- Investigating only the cost-element category the variance was calculated in (e.g. only "Manpower" causes for
  a labour variance) when the fishbone method's value lies precisely in checking *all* categories — the WP4
  mini-case's true root cause (a schedule-recovery decision) would not have been found by looking only at
  labour-specific causes.
- Treating root-cause analysis as a one-off exercise for a single period's variance rather than tracking
  whether the same root cause recurs across periods — a recurring root cause (e.g. a persistently
  under-resourced schedule) is a stronger signal than any single period's variance in isolation.

**AI in this topic.** A GenAI tool, prompted with a variance's category, size and available context (schedule
status, recent site notes, prior period causes), can draft a plausible 5-Whys chain or suggest candidate
fishbone-category causes by pattern-matching against historical variance write-ups (cross-reference KA 13.3,
KA 13.5) — a genuinely useful drafting aid that can save investigation time. This is also one of the areas
where AI's tendency to produce a fluent, confident-sounding but **unverified** explanation (hallucination) is
most risky: an AI-drafted root cause that sounds plausible is not evidence that it is correct, and unlike a
factual data-extraction task, root-cause conclusions here typically cannot be checked against a source system
at all — only against direct verification with the people and records involved (a site supervisor, an
inspection report, a timesheet). No AI-suggested root cause should be reported or actioned without that human
verification.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.2.4–4.2.6 (the calculated variances this technique investigates); KA 6.3.6 (the
analogous diagnostic sequence used to choose an EAC method); KA 12.2 (risk identification techniques, a
related discipline); KA 13.6 (AI governance and hallucination risk in depth).

### 4.2.8 Variance thresholds, materiality and exception reporting

**Definition & purpose.** Not every variance warrants investigation — the cost of investigating a trivial
variance can exceed the value of whatever corrective action it might trigger. A **threshold** (or materiality
rule) sets the point at which a variance is escalated for investigation under 4.2.7, implementing the
**management-by-exception** principle introduced in 4.2.1 and the general threshold concept covered in KA
4.1.

**The underlying principle.** A single threshold type is rarely sufficient. A **percentage** threshold (e.g.
"investigate if the variance exceeds 5% of standard cost") scales naturally across cost elements of very
different sizes, but can trigger false positives on small-value elements, where even a tiny absolute variance
produces a large percentage. An **absolute-value** threshold (e.g. "investigate if the variance exceeds
$1,000") avoids that distortion but can miss a genuinely significant percentage swing on a small account.
Standard practice combines both — investigate only when a variance breaches **both** a relative and an
absolute floor — and, critically, applies the test at more than one level: to each sub-variance individually,
**and** to the total variance for the cost element, because correlated sub-variances can each individually
fall under an absolute floor while their combined effect does not.

**Example 4.2.8 — applying a combined threshold rule to the Pipeline Segment 4B sub-variances already
calculated.**

*Scenario.* Applying an illustrative combined rule — **investigate if `|variance| > 5%` of standard/budgeted
cost of that element AND `|variance| > $1,000`** — to every sub-variance computed in 4.2.4–4.2.6.

*Required.* Classify each variance as flagged or not flagged, individually and at the cost-element total
level.

*Working.*

| Variance | $ | % of standard/budgeted cost | Exceeds 5%? | Exceeds $1,000? | Flagged individually? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Materials price (4.2.4) | 4,800 F | 5.3% | Yes | Yes | **Yes** |
| Materials usage (4.2.4) | (6,000) A | (6.7)% | Yes | Yes | **Yes** |
| Labour rate (4.2.5) | (780) A | (7.2)% | Yes | No | No |
| Labour efficiency (4.2.5) | (900) A | (8.3)% | Yes | No | No |
| **Labour total (4.2.5)** | **(1,680) A** | **(15.6)%** | Yes | Yes | **Yes** |
| Overhead expenditure (4.2.6) | (4,000) A | (6.7)% | Yes | Yes | **Yes** |
| Overhead volume (4.2.6) | (4,000) A | (6.7)% | Yes | Yes | **Yes** |

*Answer.* Materials price, materials usage, overhead expenditure and overhead volume all breach both floors
individually and are flagged directly. **Neither labour sub-variance breaches the $1,000 absolute floor on
its own** — yet their combined total, $(1,680), does. A threshold rule applied mechanically only to
sub-variances would miss the labour account's largest single cost-element problem entirely.

*Interpretation.* This is the specific reason a threshold policy must test totals as well as sub-variances:
correlated adverse sub-variances (here, both driven by the single overtime-authorisation root cause found in
4.2.7) can each individually stay under an absolute floor set for granular, line-by-line screening while their
combined effect is clearly material. A well-designed exception report checks both levels and would correctly
escalate the labour total even though it might, on a naïve line-by-line scan, be overlooked.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Setting only a percentage threshold, which produces excessive false-positive alerts on small cost elements
  (illustrated by overhead's 18.8% swing on a small $4,800 base in the account-level bridge of 4.2.9) unless
  paired with an absolute floor.
- Setting only an absolute threshold, which can miss a proportionately severe variance on a small account.
- Applying a single, organisation-wide threshold without regard to the reporting level — a variance immaterial
  at the total-project level (cross-ref KA 4.2.2's CV) may be highly material at the individual work-package
  level, and vice versa; thresholds should be set relative to the reporting audience (cross-ref KA 4.3).

**AI in this topic.** An automated exception-reporting engine can apply combined percentage-and-absolute
threshold rules across every cost element and every roll-up level in real time, flagging breaches the instant
new actuals are posted (cross-reference KA 4.3, KA 4.4, KA 13.5) — a strong, scalable use of automation given
the underlying rule is fully deterministic once set. The threshold values themselves, and any adjustment for
reporting level or audience, remain an organisational policy decision requiring professional judgement, not a
parameter an AI system should set unsupervised.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.1 (KPIs, targets, thresholds and tolerances in general); KA 4.2.4–4.2.6 (the
sub-variances this rule is applied to); KA 4.3 (audience-appropriate, exception-based reporting); KA 6.2.6
(TCPI as an analogous forward-looking threshold/trigger concept in EVM).

### 4.2.9 From variance to corrective action: the variance bridge (waterfall) from budget to actual

**Definition & purpose.** A **variance bridge** (also called a cost bridge or waterfall chart) is the single
exhibit that draws together every sub-variance calculated for a cost element or control account into one
visual reconciliation from the standard/budgeted cost of the output achieved to the actual cost incurred —
the practical deliverable a controls professional actually prepares for a monthly cost report or a corrective-
action review.

**The underlying principle.** Because every sub-variance in this KA has been shown, individually, to
reconcile exactly to its parent total (4.2.4–4.2.6), those totals can themselves be summed into one
project-level bridge with no residual, unexplained gap — provided the same output scope is used consistently
throughout. A bridge's value is not the arithmetic (already proven correct topic by topic) but the **ordering
and labelling**: it turns a page of variance figures into a narrative a non-technical stakeholder can follow
from left to right, and — critically — each step in the bridge should be traceable back to the root cause
(4.2.7) and threshold decision (4.2.8) that explains why it is there.

**Example 4.2.9 — the full variance bridge: a combined trench-backfill-and-welding control account, Month 5.**

*Scenario.* A single illustrative control account combines the materials scope of Example 4.2.4 and the
labour scope of Example 4.2.5, plus its share of fixed overhead absorbed at the $20.00/hour standard rate
established in Example 4.2.6, applied to this account's 240 standard welding hours. Actual overhead charged
to this account for the period was $5,700.

*Required.* The standard cost of output achieved, the actual cost incurred, each sub-variance, and a
reconciled bridge from one to the other.

*Working.*

| Cost element | Standard cost ($) | Actual cost ($) | Sub-variances ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Materials (Example 4.2.4) | 90,000 | 91,200 | Price 4,800 F; Usage (6,000) A |
| Labour (Example 4.2.5) | 10,800 | 12,480 | Rate (780) A; Efficiency (900) A |
| Overhead (240 hr × $20/hr standard; see above) | 4,800 | 5,700 | Overhead variance (900) A |
| **Total** | **105,600** | **109,380** | |

`Total variance = 105,600 − 109,380 = (3,780)`
`Check by summing sub-variances: 4,800 + (6,000) + (780) + (900) + (900) = (3,780)` ✓ reconciles exactly.

*Bridging step by step, from the standard cost of output achieved to the actual cost incurred* (each adverse
step adds cost; each favourable step subtracts cost):

`105,600 [standard cost] + 6,000 [materials usage, adverse] − 4,800 [materials price, favourable] + 780
[labour rate, adverse] + 900 [labour efficiency, adverse] + 900 [overhead, adverse] = 109,380 [actual cost]`

*Answer.* **Total variance = $(3,780), adverse** (SAR (14,175)) (standard $105,600/SAR 396,000; actual
$109,380/SAR 410,175), bridged through five labelled steps that sum, exactly, to the gap between standard and
actual.

*Interpretation.* The bridge shows at a glance that the **materials usage variance** ($(6,000)) is the single
largest adverse driver, partly offset by a favourable materials price effect; that **labour** contributes a
smaller, fully adverse $(1,680) from the schedule-recovery overtime traced in 4.2.7; and that **overhead**
adds a further $(900). Applying the 4.2.8 threshold, the materials usage and labour-total steps are the ones
that clear the combined 5%/$1,000 rule and warrant the corrective actions below; the overhead step, though
individually below the $1,000 absolute floor at this account's scale, has already been shown at the
period/project level (Example 4.2.6) to be materially adverse and rooted in the same schedule shortfall.

*Corrective actions arising, mapped to root cause:* (i) review the alternative bedding-material source for
specification compliance before continuing to use it, given the usage-variance finding (4.2.4); (ii) review
overtime-authorisation and fatigue-management practice on schedule-recovery shifts, rather than only
re-training the welding crew, given the 5-Whys finding that the true root cause is upstream of the welding
crew itself (4.2.7); (iii) re-test whether the schedule slippage driving both the labour and overhead findings
has itself been addressed (cross-ref KA 6.3's EAC methods and KA 10.3's schedule-recovery techniques) —
illustrating that a variance bridge's real output is not the chart, but the specific, traceable list of
actions it justifies.

**Fig 4.2.9 — Variance bridge (waterfall): standard cost to actual cost, combined control account, Month 5.**
Chart type: waterfall/bridge chart. Two solid anchor bars in brand blue `#1D4ED8`: "Standard cost $105,600" (
left) and "Actual cost $109,380" (right). Between them, five floating step bars in sequence, each rising or
falling from the running total of the bar before it: "Materials usage $(6,000)" (adverse — rises, grey fill,
amber outline), "Materials price $4,800" (favourable — falls, blue outline), "Labour rate $(780)" (adverse —

(adverse — rises, amber outline). A running-total connector line links the top of each step to the base of
the next, so the last step's top exactly meets the "Actual cost" anchor bar.

*Underlying data (the table above, reproduced for the chart):* Standard $105,600; Materials usage $(6,000);
Materials price $4,800; Labour rate $(780); Labour efficiency $(900); Overhead $(900); Actual $109,380.

**Animation 4.2.9 — "Building the bridge" [digital-only].** Frame 1: only the "Standard cost $105,600" anchor
bar is shown. Frames 2–6: each step bar flies in from the left in the order listed above, one at a time, with
a running-total readout ticking after each ("after materials usage: $111,600 … after materials price:
$106,800 … after labour rate: $107,580 … after labour efficiency: $108,480 … after overhead: $109,380").
Frame 7: the "Actual cost $109,380" anchor bar fades in exactly where the running total has landed, confirming
the reconciliation visually. Narration cue: "Five steps, five root causes, one number at the end that matches
the ledger exactly. That's what makes a bridge different from a list of variances — every step has to lead
somewhere, and the somewhere has to be verifiable."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Presenting a variance bridge whose steps do not sum exactly to the actual figure — any residual, unexplained
  gap ("other/unallocated") should be a rare, small, explicitly investigated item, never a routine plug used
  to force reconciliation.
- Ordering bridge steps by convenience or by which looks best first, rather than by materiality or by the
  narrative sequence of the underlying root-cause story — a well-ordered bridge tells the corrective-action
  story in the order a reader needs to understand it.
- Presenting the bridge as the end product, rather than as the launch point for the corrective-action list —
  per 4.2.1's loop, a bridge with no resulting action has not completed the variance-analysis cycle.

**AI in this topic.** A reporting-automation tool can generate exactly this bridge chart and its underlying
table automatically from a live cost-ledger feed once the standard cost card and threshold rules are
configured (cross-reference KA 4.3, KA 4.4, KA 13.5), and can auto-draft the narrative commentary connecting
each step to its likely cause. The arithmetic and charting are safe to automate; the causal narrative is not —
an AI-drafted commentary that asserts a root cause without the verification steps described in 4.2.7 can sound
authoritative while being wrong, and every AI-drafted bridge narrative must be checked against confirmed root
causes, not merely against correct arithmetic, before it is issued to a sponsor or client. **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Cross-references.** KA 4.2.4–4.2.6 (the sub-variances this bridge assembles); KA 4.2.7 (root cause, mapped
to each bridge step); KA 4.2.8 (the threshold decisions determining which steps are investigated); KA 4.3
(management reporting formats and cadence); KA 6.3.7 (the analogous multi-scenario EAC comparison exhibit in
Domain 6); KA 8.4 (integrated change control acting on the corrective actions identified here).

### Key terms

- **Variance** — the signed difference between a standard/budgeted figure and an actual result; positive =
  favourable, negative = adverse, per the convention fixed in 4.2.1.
- **Favourable (F) / adverse (A)** — a cost variance is favourable when the standard/budgeted cost exceeds the
  actual cost, and adverse when the actual cost exceeds the standard/budgeted cost; shown as "(A)" or in
  parentheses in tables.
- **Total variance** — the un-decomposed difference between standard/budgeted cost of output achieved and
  actual cost, for a cost element; always equal to the sum of its price/rate and quantity/usage/efficiency
  sub-variances.
- **Standard cost / standard cost card** — the pre-agreed standard quantity and standard price per unit of
  input for a unit of output, set before the period begins (cross-ref KA 3.2).
- **Flexed budget** — the standard cost re-scaled to the actual output achieved, the fair comparison point for
  variance analysis (as distinct from the original, fixed budget).
- **Materials price variance (MPV) / materials usage variance (MUV)** — `(SP−AP)×AQ` and `(SQ−AQ)×SP`
  respectively (4.2.4).
- **Labour rate variance (LRV) / labour efficiency variance (LEV)** — `(SR−AR)×AH` and `(SH−AH)×SR`
  respectively (4.2.5).
- **Fixed overhead expenditure (spend) variance / volume variance** — `BFOH−AFOH` and `(AH−BH)×FOAR`
  respectively (4.2.6).
- **Overhead absorption rate (FOAR)** — the standard rate at which fixed overhead is recovered into cost per
  unit of activity (e.g. per direct labour hour); `FOAR = BFOH/BH`.
- **Management by exception** — directing investigative effort only toward variances that breach a materiality
  threshold, rather than reviewing every transaction.
- **5 Whys / cause-and-effect (fishbone/Ishikawa) diagram** — structured root-cause analysis techniques
  (4.2.7).
- **Variance bridge (waterfall)** — a step-by-step visual reconciliation from a standard/budgeted cost to an
  actual cost through each sub-variance (4.2.9).

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 4.2.1 [topic 4.2.1, level: recall]**
In management-accounting variance analysis, a cost variance is classified as **favourable** when:
A. Actual cost exceeds the standard/budgeted cost of the output achieved
B. **The standard/budgeted cost of the output achieved exceeds the actual cost** ✔
C. Actual cost exactly equals budgeted cost
D. The schedule variance is also positive
*Rationale:* Favourable means the work cost less than its standard/budgeted value (`Variance = Standard −
Actual > 0`). A describes an adverse variance; C describes a nil variance; D wrongly ties a cost variance's
classification to an unrelated schedule measure.

**MCQ 4.2.2 [topic 4.2.4, level: application — numerical]**
A control account has a standard material price of $12.00 per unit. During the period, 5,000 units were
purchased and used at an actual price of $12.50 per unit. What is the materials price variance?
A. $2,500 favourable
B. **$2,500 adverse** ✔
C. $500 adverse
D. $25,000 adverse
*Rationale:* `MPV = (SP−AP)×AQ = (12.00−12.50)×5,000 = (0.50)×5,000 = (2,500)`, adverse (paid more than
standard). C mistakes the per-unit price difference ($0.50) for the total; D multiplies by the wrong quantity
figure (5,000 × 5, a units/decimal error).

**MCQ 4.2.3 [topic 4.2.4, level: application — numerical]**
A work package's standard usage is 3 kg of material per unit of output, at a standard price of $10.00/kg.
800 units were produced (standard quantity allowed = 2,400 kg), and 2,500 kg were actually used. What is the
materials usage variance?
A. $1,000 favourable
B. **$1,000 adverse** ✔
C. $100 adverse
D. $2,500 adverse
*Rationale:* `MUV = (SQ−AQ)×SP = (2,400−2,500)×10.00 = (100)×10.00 = (1,000)`, adverse (used more than
standard allowed). A reverses the sign; C omits multiplying by the standard price; D confuses the actual
quantity with the answer.

**MCQ 4.2.4 [topic 4.2.5, level: application — numerical]**
Standard labour time is 2 hours per unit at a standard rate of $30.00/hour. 400 units were produced (standard
hours allowed = 800 hours), using 850 actual hours at an actual rate of $29.00/hour. What is the labour
efficiency variance?
A. **$1,500 adverse** ✔
B. $1,500 favourable
C. $850 adverse
D. $425 favourable
*Rationale:* `LEV = (SH−AH)×SR = (800−850)×30.00 = (50)×30.00 = (1,500)`, adverse (took more hours than
standard allowed). B reverses the sign; C mistakes the hours figure for the answer; D is the (unrelated)
labour rate variance (`(30.00−29.00)×850 = 850`, favourable, not $425), a distractor confusing the two
sub-variances.

**MCQ 4.2.5 [topic 4.2.4, level: analysis]**
A materials cost report shows a favourable materials price variance together with a materially adverse
materials usage variance for the same period. Which explanation is most consistent with this combined
pattern?
A. **A cheaper, lower-specification batch of material was sourced, resulting in higher wastage/over-
   consumption on site** ✔
B. The standard cost was set too high and should simply be revised upward
C. Labour worked more efficiently than planned, unrelated to the materials figures
D. The overhead absorption rate was understated
*Rationale:* A favourable price alongside an adverse usage variance is the classic signature of a cheaper but
lower-quality or harder-to-work material (Example 4.2.4's mini-case). B, C and D each describe a real but
unrelated cause that does not explain this specific *combined* price/usage pattern.

**MCQ 4.2.6 [topic 4.2.8, level: application — numerical]**
A control account's materials price variance is $4,800 favourable and its materials usage variance is $6,000
adverse. What is the total materials variance, and is it favourable or adverse overall?
A. $10,800 favourable
B. **$1,200 adverse** ✔
C. $1,200 favourable
D. $10,800 adverse
*Rationale:* Sub-variances must be netted **with their signs**, not summed by absolute value:
`4,800 + (6,000) = (1,200)`, adverse. A and D both sum the absolute values ($4,800 + $6,000 = $10,800),
double-counting the offset between the two effects — exactly the sign-error/double-counting pitfall this KA
warns against; C gets the correct magnitude but the wrong sign.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. A material has a standard price of $15.00/kg, an actual price of $14.00/kg, a standard quantity allowed
   for actual output of 1,000 kg, and an actual quantity used of 1,050 kg. Calculate the materials price
   variance, the materials usage variance and the total materials variance, stating favourable or adverse for
   each, and confirm the two sub-variances reconcile to the total.
2. A labour activity has a standard rate of $40.00/hour, an actual rate of $42.00/hour, standard hours allowed
   of 500 hours, and 480 actual hours worked. Calculate the labour rate variance, the labour efficiency
   variance and the total labour variance, stating favourable or adverse for each.
3. Pipeline Segment 4B's Month-5 EVM figures show CV = $(101,250) and SV = $(135,000) (Example 4.2.2). Explain,
   in your own words, what a management-accounting price/quantity decomposition (as covered in 4.2.4–4.2.6)
   can add to these two EVM figures that CV and SV alone cannot provide.
4. Explain why the sub-variances of a total variance (e.g. a materials price variance and a materials usage
   variance) must always be added **algebraically, with their signs**, rather than by absolute value, to
   reconcile to the total. Illustrate briefly using numbers from this KA.

**Answers**

1. `MPV = (SP−AP)×AQ = (15.00−14.00)×1,050 = 1×1,050 = 1,050`, favourable. `MUV = (SQ−AQ)×SP =
   (1,000−1,050)×15.00 = (50)×15.00 = (750)`, adverse. `Total = 1,050 + (750) = 300`, favourable. Check:
   standard cost `= 1,000×15.00 = 15,000`; actual cost `= 1,050×14.00 = 14,700`; `15,000−14,700 = 300` ✓
   matches.
2. `LRV = (SR−AR)×AH = (40.00−42.00)×480 = (2)×480 = (960)`, adverse. `LEV = (SH−AH)×SR = (500−480)×40.00 =
   20×40.00 = 800`, favourable. `Total = (960) + 800 = (160)`, adverse. Check: standard cost `= 500×40.00 =
   20,000`; actual cost `= 480×42.00 = 20,160`; `20,000−20,160 = (160)` ✓ matches.
3. CV confirms *that* an adverse $101,250 cost variance exists on the work performed, and SV confirms *that*
   the project is materially behind its earned-value schedule — but neither states *why*: whether the cost
   overrun came from paying more than standard for materials and labour (a price/rate effect) or from
   consuming more of them than standard for the output achieved (a quantity/usage/efficiency effect), or a
   mix of both. Decomposing the relevant cost elements into price/rate and usage/efficiency variances, as
   done in 4.2.4–4.2.6, identifies which driver is responsible and points toward a different, specific
   corrective action for each — a diagnosis CV and SV alone cannot supply.
4. Favourable and adverse sub-variances can partially or fully offset one another; summing their absolute
   values ignores this and overstates the true net effect. For example, Example 4.2.4's $4,800 favourable
   materials price variance and $(6,000) adverse materials usage variance combine, with signs, to a net
   $(1,200) adverse total — summing the absolute values ($4,800 + $6,000 = $10,800) would overstate the
   variance by $9,600 and misrepresent a modest net overrun as a very large one, a double-counting error this
   KA's MCQ 4.2.6 tests directly.
