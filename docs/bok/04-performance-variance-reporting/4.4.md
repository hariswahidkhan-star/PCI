## KA 4.4 — Data visualisation & storytelling for controls

KA 4.1 established the vocabulary of performance targets, thresholds and leading/lagging indicators; KA 4.2

### 4.4.1 Visual encoding, data-ink and the discipline of a clean chart

**Definition & purpose.** Visual encoding is the mapping of a data value onto a visual property — position,
length, angle, area, colour — that the reader's eye decodes back into a number. A chart's usefulness is set
almost entirely by (a) which encoding it uses and (b) how much of its "ink" is spent showing data rather
than decoration. This topic gives the professional a working vocabulary and discipline for building charts
that are read correctly, quickly, and without conscious effort.

**The underlying principle.** Not all encodings are decoded with equal accuracy. Research into graphical
perception, most influentially by researchers such as William Cleveland and Robert McGill, ranked the
elementary tasks the eye performs when reading a chart, from most to least accurate: comparing **position
along a common, aligned scale** (as in a bar or dot chart sharing one axis) is read most precisely; comparing
**length** (bar heights, line steepness) is next; comparing **angle or slope** is read less precisely still;
and comparing **area or volume** (pie slices, bubble sizes, 3D shapes) is read least precisely and most
inconsistently. This is why a bar chart reliably outperforms a pie chart for comparing a handful of values,
and why a well-chosen chart type is doing real perceptual work, not just a stylistic preference (developed
fully in 4.4.3). A complementary discipline, popularised by Edward Tufte, is to maximise the **data-ink
ratio** — the proportion of a chart's visible ink that encodes actual data, as distinct from **chart-junk**:
gridlines, borders, backgrounds, 3D bevels, drop shadows, redundant legends and decorative icons that carry
no information and compete with the data for attention.

**Formula (illustrative).**
`Data-ink ratio = data-ink / total ink used in the graphic`
This is a conceptual, order-of-magnitude guide rather than something measured to the pixel in practice —
treat it as a discipline ("could this mark be removed without losing information?"), not a precise
engineering metric.

**Example 4.4.1 — Decluttering a Pipeline Segment 4B budget-composition chart.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's reporting team originally built a chart of the $600,000 BAC split by work package
(WP1 Design & engineering $80,000; WP2 Pipe & valve procurement $150,000; WP3 Trenching & earthworks
$200,000; WP4 Pipe-laying & welding $120,000; WP5 Site supervision $50,000 — cross-reference KA 6.1.4) as a
3D-bevelled bar chart with a heavy background gridline every $10,000, drop shadows under each bar, a
redundant legend repeating the category names already on the x-axis, and a decorative pipeline icon in the
corner.

*Required.* Identify what to remove, and estimate the qualitative change in data-ink ratio.

*Working.* Every element that does not encode one of the five WP values is a candidate for removal: the 3D
bevel and drop shadows distort the perceived bar length (an area/volume-like distortion, contradicting the
ranking above) without adding information; a gridline every $10,000 is far finer than the reporting
precision needed (five values, each a multiple of $10,000, are legible against two or three reference
lines); the legend duplicates the x-axis labels; the icon encodes nothing. Removing all four and retaining
flat 2D bars, three light horizontal gridlines (at $0, $100,000, $200,000) and a single direct data label
per bar removes roughly two-thirds of the non-data ink while leaving every data value exactly as legible —
informally, the data-ink ratio rises from a low estimate (roughly a third of visible ink is data) to a high
one (roughly four-fifths).

*Answer.* **The decluttered chart removes 3D bevels, drop shadows, the redundant legend and the decorative
icon, keeping only flat bars, minimal gridlines and direct labels** — the same five numbers, read faster and
more accurately.

*Interpretation.* Nothing about the underlying $600,000 changed; only the ink used to show it did. This is
the cheapest, highest-value edit available to a project-controls professional building any chart for a
report.

**Fig 4.4.1 — Before/after: Pipeline Segment 4B budget-by-work-package chart.**
Chart type: paired bar-chart panel, "Before" and "After," side by side. Both plot the same five categories
(WP1–WP5) on the horizontal axis and USD on the vertical axis. "Before": 3D-bevelled bars in a muted default
palette, drop shadows, a fine background grid at $10,000 intervals, a legend box duplicating the x-axis
categories, and a small decorative icon in the top corner. "After": flat 2D bars in brand blue `#1D4ED8`,
three light grey horizontal gridlines only (at $0 / $100,000 / $200,000), a direct value label centred
above each bar, no legend, no icon.

*Underlying data:* WP1 $80,000; WP2 $150,000; WP3 $200,000; WP4 $120,000; WP5 $50,000 (total $600,000, SAR
2,250,000).

**Animation 4.4.1 — "Stripping the chart-junk" [digital-only].** Frame 1: the cluttered "before" chart in
full. Frames 2–5: each non-data element fades out in turn — first the icon, then the drop shadows and 3D
bevel (bars flatten to 2D), then the dense gridlines (thinning to three), then the legend (as the categories
brighten on the x-axis instead) — while the five bars themselves never move or resize. Frame 6: the clean
"after" chart remains, with a running data-ink-ratio indicator in the corner ticking up as each element is
removed. Narration cue: "Nothing about the data changed. Everything that wasn't the data did."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Adding decoration to make a report "look designed" — a chart is a working instrument, not a decorative
  graphic; polish should come from clarity, not ornamentation.
- Removing so much that genuinely useful reference information (axis units, a baseline, a data label the
  audience needs) is lost in the pursuit of minimalism — data-ink discipline removes non-data ink, not data.
- Treating gridlines as free — every gridline is ink competing with the bars for attention; use the minimum
  that lets the reader estimate a value without a data label.

**AI in this topic.** General-purpose chart-generation and "AI in Excel/analysis assistant"-class tools can
build a first-draft chart from a pasted table in seconds — a genuine speed gain over building one manually.
Their default output, however, is usually the tool's own generic template (a default theme, gridlines, a
legend even where none is needed) rather than a decluttered, purpose-built chart, because the tool does not
know this specific chart is going into a board pack. The professional must still apply the data-ink
discipline in this topic to whatever an AI tool proposes — **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Cross-references.** KA 4.4.3 (chart-type selection, which depends on the same perceptual-accuracy

### 4.4.2 Distortion and misdirection: truncated axes, dual axes, 3D effects and other chart lies

**Definition & purpose.** Some chart choices do not just waste ink (4.4.1) — they actively misrepresent the
data, making a small difference look large, an unrelated pair of series look correlated, or a modest slice
look dominant. This topic names the most common distortions in project-controls reporting and gives a way to
quantify how misleading a chart is.

**The underlying principle.** A chart is a promise: the visual difference between two marks should represent
the same proportional difference as the underlying numbers. Every distortion in this topic breaks that
promise in a specific, well-understood way — by changing where an axis starts, by forcing two unrelated
scales to share one visual space, by adding a third dimension that has no data behind it, or by using a
colour scheme that implies an order the data does not have (4.4.4). Edward Tufte proposed a simple
diagnostic for the axis-based distortions: the **Lie Factor**, the ratio of the size of an effect as shown
in the graphic to the size of that effect in the underlying data. A Lie Factor close to 1.0 is honest; one
far from 1.0 (Tufte's rule of thumb treats roughly 0.95–1.05 as acceptable) signals a chart that is actively
misleading, whatever the underlying data or the chart-maker's intent.

**Formula.**
`Lie Factor = (size of effect shown in the graphic) / (size of effect in the data)`
where each "size of effect" is expressed as a percentage change between the two values being compared,
measured the same way (e.g. bar-height change) in both the graphic and the data.

**Example 4.4.2 — Quantifying a truncated-axis distortion, Pipeline Segment 4B Month 3.**

*Scenario.* A draft slide compares EV ($330,000) and AC ($280,000) at the Month-3 data date (the same CV of
+$50,000 established in KA 6.2.1) using a bar chart whose vertical axis starts at $260,000 and ends at
$340,000, rather than at $0.

*Required.* The Lie Factor of the truncated chart, and the corrected version.

*Working.*
Effect in the data: `(EV − AC) / AC = (330,000 − 280,000) / 280,000 = 17.9%`.
Effect shown in the graphic: bar heights are proportional to (value − 260,000), so the EV bar's visual
height is `330,000 − 260,000 = 70,000` and the AC bar's is `280,000 − 260,000 = 20,000`; the depicted
increase is `(70,000 − 20,000) / 20,000 = 250%`.
`Lie Factor = 250% / 17.9% ≈ 14.0`

*Answer.* **The truncated chart has a Lie Factor of approximately 14** — the EV bar is made to look roughly
fourteen times more dramatically larger than AC than the underlying 17.9% cost variance actually is. A
chart drawn on a full, zero-based axis has a Lie Factor of 1.0 by construction.

*Interpretation.* A genuinely favourable, but modest, cost variance has been redrawn to look like a dramatic
outperformance. Whether intentional or not, this is exactly the kind of chart that should never leave a
controls function without correction — a reviewer who trusts the visual impression rather than reading the
axis and data labels will draw a materially wrong conclusion about how large the variance really is.

**Fig 4.4.2 — Before/after: the same Month-3 EV/AC comparison, truncated versus zero-based axis.**
Chart type: paired bar-chart panel. "Before": vertical axis from $260,000–$340,000, two bars (EV $330,000,
AC $280,000) that visually differ by roughly 3.5×. "After": vertical axis from $0–$350,000, the same two
bars, now visually differing by the true ~18%, with a data label on each bar and the CV (+$50,000) annotated
between them in brand blue `#1D4ED8`.

*Underlying data:* EV $330,000; AC $280,000; CV +$50,000 (17.9% of AC).

**Other distortions to eliminate before publishing.**
- **Dual axes.** Plotting two series with independently scaled left and right vertical axes (e.g. cost in
  USD on one axis, CPI as a ratio on the other) invites the reader to compare the *positions* of two lines
  that are not on the same scale — an apparent crossover or gap can be manufactured simply by choosing where
  each axis starts and ends, with no change to the underlying data. Where two series must be compared,
  prefer a shared scale, a small-multiples pair of separate charts (4.4.3), or an explicit annotation of the
  relationship rather than a dual-axis overlay.
- **3D effects (bars, pies, "exploded" wedges).** A third dimension with no data behind it distorts the
  area/volume-based perceptual comparisons that are already the least accurate in the encoding hierarchy
  (4.4.1) — a 3D pie's front-facing wedges appear systematically larger than equally-sized wedges further
  from the viewer, purely because of the rendering angle.
- **Rainbow / unordered-hue palettes on ordered or continuous data.** A rainbow colour scale (red→orange→
  yellow→green→blue→violet) has no perceptual ordering — the eye cannot read "more red" as consistently
  "higher" the way it reads "further along a blue gradient" as higher, and rainbow scales introduce false
  visual boundaries where none exist in the data (developed fully in 4.4.4).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Truncating an axis "to make the chart fit the slide" without checking the resulting Lie Factor — a
  legitimate space constraint is not a legitimate reason to distort the message.
- Assuming a dual axis is safe because both series are genuinely related (e.g. cost and CPI) — the risk is
  not that they are unrelated, but that the reader cannot tell *how much* one changed relative to the other
  without reading both axes carefully, which most report readers will not do.
- Using 3D or rainbow styling because a charting tool's default theme offers it — a distortion is a
  distortion whether or not the software makes it one click away.

**AI in this topic.** A natural-language chart-generation tool, left to its own defaults, has been observed
to choose a non-zero axis or a dual-axis layout purely because it produces a chart that "looks more
dramatic" or fits a requested aspect ratio on the sample data — reproducing exactly the distortions in this
topic without any intent to mislead. A fast, polished, AI-generated chart is not automatically an honest
one; the professional must check the axis, the scale and the Lie Factor of any chart before it is published,
AI-generated or not — **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Cross-references.** KA 6.2.1 (the CV this example is drawn from); KA 4.4.1 (the perceptual-accuracy
ranking that explains why 3D/area distortions are especially damaging); KA 4.4.4 (colour ordering and
rainbow palettes in full); KA 4.3 (management reporting, where a distorted chart can misdirect an entire
steering-committee discussion).

### 4.4.3 Choosing the right chart for the message: a decision framework

**Definition & purpose.** Every chart makes an implicit claim about what kind of comparison the reader
should make: a trend over time, a comparison between categories, how a whole breaks into parts, how a set of
values is distributed, how two variables relate, or where a single value sits against a target. Choosing a
chart type that does not match the intended message is one of the most common and avoidable errors in
project-controls reporting.

**The underlying principle.** Six message types recur constantly in controls reporting, and each has a
chart family best suited to it, directly because of the perceptual-accuracy ranking established in 4.4.1
(position and length read most accurately; area and angle read least accurately):

| Message | Best-suited chart | Why | Typical controls example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trend over time | Line chart / cumulative S-curve | Position along a shared time axis, read continuously | PV/EV/AC S-curve (KA 6.1, 4.4.6a) |
| Comparison between categories | Bar chart (grouped/paired) | Length comparison on a common baseline, the most accurate non-positional task | Budget by work package; CPI by control account |
| Composition (parts of a whole) | Stacked bar / 100%-stacked bar / waterfall | Shows both the parts and, cumulatively, the whole | BAC broken into WP1–WP5; a variance bridge (4.4.6c) |
| Distribution | Histogram / box plot | Shows the shape, spread and outliers of a set of values, not just its average | Spread of daily trenching production rates (4.4.6d) |
| Relationship between two variables | Scatter chart | Position on two axes simultaneously, the only chart type built for this | CPI vs SPI health quadrant (KA 6.2.5) |
| Status against a target | RAG indicator / bullet chart | A single position or marker read against a fixed reference, not a full series | CPI/SPI/TCPI scorecard (4.4.6b) |

A pie or donut chart is conspicuously absent from the "best-suited" column: because angle and area are read
less accurately than length (4.4.1), a pie chart is a weaker choice than a bar chart for the same
composition data whenever more than two or three slices are involved, and it should be treated as a last
resort, not a default.

**Example 4.4.3 — The same Pipeline Segment 4B data, three chart types, three different readabilities.**

*Scenario.* The $600,000 BAC split by work package (WP1 $80,000; WP2 $150,000; WP3 $200,000; WP4 $120,000;
WP5 $50,000) needs to answer two different questions in two different reports: "which work package carries
the most budget?" and "what share of the total does trenching (WP3) represent?"

*Required.* The most accurate chart for each question, and what happens if a 3D pie is used for both
instead.

*Working.* For "which carries the most budget," a sorted horizontal bar chart lets the reader compare five
bar lengths against one shared baseline — WP3 ($200,000) is visibly the longest, WP2 ($150,000) second, and
the ranking is unambiguous. For "what share of the total," a 100%-stacked bar (or a simple annotated donut,
used sparingly) showing each WP's percentage of $600,000 (WP1 13.3%; WP2 25.0%; WP3 33.3%; WP4 20.0%; WP5
8.3%) directly answers a share-of-whole question. A 3D pie built from the same five values distorts both
answers at once: the wedge nearest the viewer appears larger than an equally-sized wedge further away, so
the *apparent* ranking of WP3 versus WP2 can shift depending purely on rotation angle, with no change to the
underlying $200,000 and $150,000.

*Answer.* **A sorted bar chart for the ranking question; a 100%-stacked bar (or restrained donut) for the
share-of-whole question; a 3D pie is the wrong choice for either.**

*Interpretation.* There is no single "best" chart for a dataset in isolation — the right choice depends on
the specific question the chart must answer, which is exactly why this decision framework is organised by
*message*, not by data type alone.

**Fig 4.4.3 — Small multiples: the same WP1–WP5 budget data, three chart types compared.**
Chart type: a three-panel small-multiples figure, sharing the same five-category, $600,000-total dataset.
Panel 1 ("comparison"): horizontal bar chart, sorted descending, brand blue `#1D4ED8` bars, WP3 longest.
Panel 2 ("composition"): a single 100%-stacked horizontal bar, five segments shaded in a consistent
categorical palette (4.4.4) summing to 100%, each segment labelled with its percentage. Panel 3 ("avoid"): a
3D pie of the identical data, visually flagged with a warning border, with a callout arrow showing how the
front-facing WP3 wedge and the further-back WP2 wedge are harder to compare accurately than the two
equivalent bars in Panel 1.

*Underlying data:* WP1 $80,000 (13.3%); WP2 $150,000 (25.0%); WP3 $200,000 (33.3%); WP4 $120,000 (20.0%);
WP5 $50,000 (8.3%); total $600,000 (100.0%).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Defaulting to whatever chart type a spreadsheet tool inserts first (often a 3D column or pie) rather than
  deliberately matching chart to message.
- Using a line chart for categorical comparison data with no meaningful order (e.g. work packages) — a line
  implies a trend or trajectory between points that do not actually connect.
- Cramming every available metric onto one combination chart instead of using **small multiples** — several
  simple charts, each answering one question clearly — a technique that, as Panel 1–2 above illustrate, is
  often more readable than one crowded chart trying to do everything.

**AI in this topic.** A chart-recommendation or "copilot"-style BI feature can propose a chart type
automatically from a dataset's shape (number of categories, whether a field is a date, a measure or a
category) — a genuinely useful starting suggestion, especially for someone building their first chart from a
new export. It does not know *which question* the chart is meant to answer for a specific audience, so its
suggestion should be checked against the message-driven framework above, not accepted as the final choice —
**AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable.**

**Cross-references.** KA 4.4.1 (the perceptual-accuracy ranking this framework is built on); KA 4.4.6 (fully
worked examples of each chart family); KA 6.2.5 (the CPI/SPI scatter/quadrant chart as the relationship

### 4.4.4 Colour and accessibility: colour-blind-safe palettes and the brand system

**Definition & purpose.** Colour is one of the least accurately decoded encodings (4.4.1) and is also the
encoding most likely to fail entirely for a meaningful share of readers: colour vision deficiency (commonly,
though imprecisely, called "colour blindness") affects a material proportion of readers — most
significantly red–green deficiencies, which affect roughly 1 in 12 men (and a much smaller proportion of
women) of Northern European descent, with other populations and deficiency types varying. A chart that only
works for readers with typical colour vision is not a finished chart.

**The underlying principle.** Colour should be used with three disciplines, in order of importance: (1)
never encode a critical distinction using colour alone — pair it with position, a label, a pattern or a
shape so the information survives greyscale printing and colour-vision deficiency; (2) choose hues that
remain distinguishable under the common forms of colour-vision deficiency, favouring blue/orange or
blue/amber contrasts over red/green; and (3) reserve the strongest, most saturated colour for what matters
most (in this book's visual system, the brand blue `#1D4ED8`) and use lighter, greyer tones for context and
comparison series, so emphasis is read through *intensity*, not just hue. Established, real reference
palettes for this work include Cynthia Brewer's ColorBrewer sequential/diverging/qualitative palette sets
and the Okabe–Ito eight-colour qualitative palette, both developed specifically to remain distinguishable
under common colour-vision deficiencies; either is a reasonable starting point for a controls function
building its own chart palette.

**Example 4.4.4 — An illustrative categorical palette anchored on the brand blue.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's reporting team needs a five-colour categorical palette for the WP1–WP5 chart (4.4.1,
4.4.3) that (a) keeps the brand blue `#1D4ED8` as the primary/emphasis colour, (b) remains distinguishable
for readers with red–green colour-vision deficiency, and (c) still separates on a greyscale printout.

*Required.* A candidate palette and the design logic behind it.

*Working.* Starting from the brand blue as the anchor for the single most important series (or, in a
categorical chart, the category the story is about), the remaining four hues are chosen to differ in both
**hue** and **lightness**, avoiding a red/green pairing: an amber/orange (high hue-contrast to blue, and
commonly distinguishable under both protanopia and deuteranopia), a neutral grey (for baseline/reference
series, separated purely by lightness so it survives greyscale), a darker teal (blue-green, kept visibly
darker/less saturated than the brand blue so the two do not collapse together under deficiency simulation),
and a muted violet as a fifth category, chosen for a distinct lightness step from the other four.

*Answer.* **Illustrative palette:** brand blue `#1D4ED8` (primary), amber `#F59E0B`, neutral grey
`#6B7280`, teal `#0F766E`, violet `#7C3AED`.

*Interpretation.* This is a design starting point, not a certified accessible palette — before it is
adopted for production reporting, it must be checked against a colour-vision-deficiency simulator (a
genuine verification step, not a formality) and against the organisation's actual print and screen
environments; this is flagged explicitly for design/SME sign-off at the end of this KA.

**Fig 4.4.4 — The WP1–WP5 chart rendered under typical colour vision and simulated colour-vision
deficiency.**
Chart type: a three-panel comparison of the same bar chart (WP1–WP5, 4.4.1). Panel 1: rendered in the
illustrative five-colour palette above, as a reader with typical colour vision would see it. Panel 2: the
same chart simulated under deuteranopia (red–green deficiency), showing the brand blue and violet remaining
distinguishable while a poorly chosen red/green pair (shown crossed out, not used in the actual palette)
would have collapsed to a similar tone. Panel 3: the same chart converted to greyscale, showing that the
five bars still separate by lightness alone because colour was never the only encoding (each bar is also
separated by position and a direct data label).

*Underlying data:* WP1–WP5 values as in Fig 4.4.1; palette as in Example 4.4.4.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using a **rainbow palette** on ordered or continuous data (e.g. shading a heat-map by raw hue across the
  spectrum) — rainbow scales have no consistent perceptual ordering and create false visual boundaries where
  the underlying data changes smoothly (cross-reference 4.4.2).
- Relying on a red/green RAG (Red-Amber-Green) status scheme with colour as the *only* cue — a genuinely
  accessible RAG indicator also uses a symbol or label (e.g. a filled circle for "red," a triangle for
  "amber," a check for "green," or the word itself) so status is legible without colour.
- Assuming a palette that looks fine to the person who built it is fine for everyone — colour-vision
  deficiency is common enough, and invisible enough to a person who does not have it, that verification
  against a simulator should be routine, not exceptional.

**AI in this topic.** General-purpose AI chart or design tools can generate a colour palette that looks
polished on request, but do not reliably verify colour-blind-safety or brand/contrast compliance unless
explicitly instructed and given the constraint — an unreviewed AI-suggested palette can look attractive on
the designer's own screen while still failing for a material share of readers. The professional must verify
any AI-proposed palette against a colour-vision-deficiency simulator and the organisation's brand and
accessibility standards before it is used in production reporting.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.4.1 (colour's place in the encoding-accuracy hierarchy); KA 4.4.2 (rainbow
palettes as a distortion); KA 4.4.6b (the RAG/bullet-chart application of these rules); KA 4.3
(audience-appropriate reporting, including accessibility as an audience requirement).

### 4.4.5 Dashboard design and layout hierarchy

**Definition & purpose.** A dashboard is not a single chart but a page (or screen) of several, and the
*arrangement* of those charts communicates as much as any individual chart does. This topic covers how to
lay out a project-controls dashboard so the most important information is seen first, understood fastest,
and supported — not buried — by the detail beneath it.

**The underlying principle.** Two ideas govern dashboard layout. First, **visual hierarchy**: readers scan a
page in a broadly predictable pattern (commonly described as a "Z" or an "F" shape in Western, left-to-right
reading contexts — entering top-left, scanning right, dropping down, scanning again), so the position that
is seen first and most reliably is the top-left, and position should be assigned by importance, not by
build order or alphabetical habit. Second, the **Gestalt principle of proximity**: elements placed close
together are read by the eye as related, and elements placed apart are read as unrelated, regardless of
whether they actually are — so grouping (e.g. all cost-related tiles together, all schedule-related tiles
together) should follow the *meaning* of the metrics, not just available space. A useful summary discipline
borrowed from journalism is the **inverted pyramid**: put the single most important fact first (the headline
KPI or RAG status), supporting detail next (the trend charts), and full granularity last (the detail table)
— because most readers will stop reading before the bottom of the pyramid, and the pyramid ensures they
still leave with the most important fact.

**Example 4.4.5 — Laying out a Month-3 Pipeline Segment 4B status dashboard.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's controls team must fit five things onto one dashboard page for a steering committee
that has roughly five seconds of attention before moving to discussion: overall cost/schedule status, the
S-curve trend, the CPI/SPI/TCPI scorecard, a risk summary, and the WP1–WP5 detail table.

*Required.* An ordered layout following the inverted pyramid and proximity principles.

*Working.* Top band (seen first, highest importance): a row of RAG-coded KPI tiles giving the headline
answer in under a second — CV +$50,000 (green), SV +$60,000 (green), CPI 1.18 (green), SPI 1.22 (green),
TCPI 0.84 (green) — grouped together by proximity because they are all "how are we doing" metrics. Middle
band (supporting detail, seen second): the S-curve (trend, 4.4.6a) and the CPI/SPI bullet scorecard (status,
4.4.6b) side by side, because a reader who wants "why" naturally looks here next. Bottom band (full detail,
seen last, by readers who choose to drill in): the WP1–WP5 table with budget, EV, method and % complete for
each package, plus a compact risk-register summary (R1–R4, cross-reference KA 12.2).

*Answer.* **Top band = KPI tiles (headline); middle band = S-curve + scorecard (trend/status); bottom band
= WP detail table + risk summary (drill-down)** — three bands, ordered by the inverted pyramid, each band
internally grouped by proximity.

*Interpretation.* A reader who looks at the dashboard for five seconds and no longer still leaves with the
correct headline conclusion (favourable cost and schedule position); a reader who stays for the full
steering-committee discussion has everything needed to interrogate that conclusion, without the two
audiences' needs competing for the same space.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Sizing tiles and charts by how much space is left over rather than by importance — the largest element on
  the page is read as the most important one, whether or not that was intended.
- Scattering related metrics (e.g. all five EVM indices) across different parts of the page instead of
  grouping them by proximity, forcing the reader to hunt and reassemble the story themselves.
- Building a dashboard "bottom-up" from whatever data happens to be easiest to extract, rather than top-down
  from the one question the primary audience actually needs answered first.

**AI in this topic.** BI-copilot-class features can auto-arrange tiles and suggest a layout directly from a

capability. Such a tool has no knowledge of which specific committee this dashboard serves or what they care
about most this month; the choice of what occupies the top band is an audience judgement that the
professional must set deliberately, not delegate to a default arrangement (cross-reference KA 4.3's
treatment of audience-appropriate reporting).

### 4.4.6 The standard project-controls visual library

**Definition & purpose.** A small number of chart types recur so often in project-controls reporting that
fluency in building and reading them correctly is a core professional skill in its own right. This topic
works through six of them, each grounded in Meridian Rail Contracting LLC data already established
elsewhere in this book, so the reader sees the same numbers rendered in the chart type built specifically
for their message (4.4.3).

**Fig 4.4.6a — The S-curve: cumulative trend.**
Chart type: cumulative line chart, USD on the vertical axis, Month 1–6 on the horizontal axis, plotting PV
(full baseline), and EV/AC (known only to the current data date) — the standard project-controls trend
chart, fully specified and animated at KA 6.1's Fig 6.1.1, which this topic cross-references rather than
repeating in full. Message: trend over time (4.4.3). Underlying data: PV cumulative — M1 $60,000, M2
$150,000, M3 $270,000, M4 $420,000, M5 $540,000, M6 $600,000; Month-3 EV $330,000, AC $280,000.

**Fig 4.4.6b — The EVM performance/status chart: a bullet-chart scorecard.**
Chart type: a **bullet chart** (a compact status format developed by Stephen Few as a data-dense alternative
to a gauge or speedometer chart). Message: status against a target (4.4.3). For each of CPI, SPI and TCPI, a
single horizontal bar shows three background bands (red <0.90, amber 0.90–0.99, green ≥1.00), a solid
marker bar for the actual value, and a thin vertical tick for a comparative reference. Render-ready
description: three stacked bullet rows, labelled "CPI," "SPI," "TCPI," each scaled 0.60–1.40; CPI's marker
sits at 1.18 (in the green band); SPI's marker sits at 1.22 (green); TCPI's marker sits at 0.84, with its
comparative tick at CPI's 1.18, visually showing TCPI comfortably below CPI (favourable, per KA 6.2.6).
Underlying data: CPI 1.18, SPI 1.22, TCPI 0.84 (KA 6.2).

**Fig 4.4.6c — The variance/exposure waterfall: composition/bridge.**
Chart type: waterfall chart. Message: composition/bridge (4.4.3). This bridges Pipeline Segment 4B's BAC
through its four quantitatively assessed risks (KA 12.2, R1–R4) to a **risk-adjusted cost exposure** — a
genuinely different question from the tornado at Fig 4.4.6f: the waterfall asks "what is the cumulative exposure
if every quantified risk materialises," while the tornado asks "which single risk matters most."

*Working.* `BAC 600,000 → +R1 (ground conditions) 70,000 = 670,000 → +R2 (utility clash) 60,000 = 730,000 →
+R3 (price escalation) 40,000 = 770,000 → +R4 (weather delay) 20,000 = 790,000`.

*Underlying data:* BAC $600,000; R1 +$70,000; R2 +$60,000; R3 +$40,000; R4 +$20,000; risk-adjusted exposure
$790,000 (SAR 2,962,500).

Render-ready description: a floating-bar waterfall, starting column "BAC $600,000" (solid, grey, full
height from $0), four intermediate floating columns for R1–R4 (each in a warning amber, floating between its
running-total start and end value, labelled with its risk ID and $ amount), and a final solid column
"Risk-adjusted exposure $790,000" in brand blue `#1D4ED8`, with thin connector lines linking the top of each
column to the base of the next.

**Fig 4.4.6d — The histogram: distribution.**
Chart type: histogram. Message: distribution (4.4.3). Meridian's productivity team logs a 20-day sample of
the trenching crew's daily production (metres/day) to understand the *spread*, not just the average, of
productivity feeding WP3 (cross-reference KA 6.1.4, KA 3.2 productivity data): 38, 42, 45, 47, 48, 50, 50,
51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 55, 56, 58, 60, 62, 65, 68, 72 (n = 20; sum 1,081 m; mean 54.1 m/day).

*Working (bin selection).* Using **Sturges' rule** — a standard, widely used rule of thumb for choosing an
approximate bin count, `k = 1 + log₂(n)` — gives `k = 1 + log₂(20) = 1 + 4.32 = 5.32`, rounded to **5
bins**. With a data range of `72 − 38 = 34`, a bin width of `34 / 5 = 6.8`, rounded to a convenient **7
m/day**, gives bins of [38–44], [45–51], [52–58], [59–65], [66–72].

*Answer.* Frequencies: [38–44] = 2; [45–51] = 6; [52–58] = 7; [59–65] = 3; [66–72] = 2 (total
2+6+7+3+2 = 20 ✓).

*Interpretation.* The distribution is roughly unimodal and centred close to the 54.1 m/day mean, with a
long-ish upper tail (one reading at 72 m/day) — useful evidence, beyond a single average, for setting a
realistic productivity assumption and range in a forecast or estimate (cross-reference KA 3.2, KA 3.4),
rather than assuming every day performs at the mean.

Render-ready description: a five-bar histogram, bin ranges on the horizontal axis, frequency (count of
days) on the vertical axis, bars in brand blue `#1D4ED8`, a dashed vertical reference line at the mean (54.1
m/day).

**Fig 4.4.6e — The milestone/Gantt chart: schedule.**
Chart type: Gantt bar chart with milestone markers. Message: schedule status, a specialised comparison-over-
time view. Reuses the WP schedule windows established at KA 6.4's Fig 6.4.1 (WP1 Months 1–2; WP2 Months
1–3; WP3 Months 2–5; WP4 Months 3–6; WP5 Months 1–6, LOE), adding three milestone diamonds: "WP1 design
complete" at the end of Month 2; "Month-3 data date / status review" at Month 3; and "Planned mechanical
completion (BAC fully earned)" at Month 6. Underlying data: the WP schedule windows above and the three
milestone dates. Render-ready description: five horizontal bars (one per WP) against a Month 1–6 axis,
brand-blue `#1D4ED8` bars for in-progress/complete work, grey outline for not-yet-started, three diamond
markers at the dates above, each with a short text label.

**Fig 4.4.6f — The tornado diagram: sensitivity/relative ranking.**
Chart type: tornado diagram — a horizontal bar chart ranking input variables by the size of their individual
effect on an output, sorted with the largest effect at the top so the bars taper like a funnel. Message: a
specialised comparison, answering "which single driver matters most," distinct from Fig 4.4.6c's cumulative
bridge.

*Working.* For each of Pipeline Segment 4B's four quantified risks (R1–R4, KA 12.2), the effect on total
project cost if that risk occurs at its full estimated impact, holding all others at the BAC base case: R1
$70,000; R2 $60,000; R3 $40,000; R4 $20,000. Sorted descending, R1 is already the widest bar and R4 the
narrowest — no re-ranking needed, and this ordering matches R1–R4's existing High-to-Medium prioritisation in
KA 12.2.

*Answer.* **R1 (adverse ground conditions) is the single largest individual cost-sensitivity driver at
$70,000, more than three times R4's $20,000.**

Render-ready description: four horizontal bars, one per risk, each running from a vertical reference line at
BAC ($600,000, labelled "base case") to the right by its $ impact, sorted R1 (top, longest) to R4 (bottom,
shortest), all bars in brand blue `#1D4ED8`, each labelled with its risk ID and $ amount.

**Animation 4.4.6f — "Building the tornado" [digital-only].** Frame 1: a vertical reference line at the BAC
base case, no bars yet. Frames 2–5: R1–R4's bars grow outward from the base case one at a time, in scoring
order from KA 12.2 (R1 first), each pausing briefly with its $ value called out. Frame 6: the four bars
re-sort, sliding into the final widest-at-top order, visibly forming the tornado's tapered shape. Narration
cue: "Same four risks as the register. Ranked, they tell you which one to act on first."

**Common pitfalls (all six visuals).**
- Treating the waterfall's risk-adjusted exposure ($790,000, "if every quantified risk occurs") as a
  forecast or an EAC — it is a stress-test bridge, not a probability-weighted forecast (cross-reference KA
  6.3, KA 12.2's expected-value treatment).
- Building a tornado diagram from ranked scores (KA 12.2's probability × impact ordinal score) rather than
  from actual dollar sensitivity — the two rankings can differ, and a tornado should be built on the
  dollar/output-sensitivity basis shown here.
- Reusing a chart type out of habit (e.g. always reaching for a Gantt chart) rather than checking, via the
  4.4.3 framework, that it is still the right chart for this particular message.

**AI in this topic.** A natural-language "build me an S-curve/tornado/waterfall from this data" request to a
BI copilot or GenAI analysis tool can genuinely assemble any of these six chart types from a data export in
seconds — a strong, current, mechanical time-saver (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). It has no way to
judge whether the *method* behind the chart is sound — for instance, whether a tornado varies one risk at a
time correctly, or whether a waterfall's step order tells a causally honest story rather than one arranged
to look most favourable — that methodological check remains the professional's responsibility before any of
these standard visuals is published.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.1.1, 6.1.4, 6.4.1 (S-curve and Gantt source data); KA 6.2 (CPI/SPI/TCPI); KA 12.2
(risk register, R1–R6); KA 3.2 (productivity/estimating data); KA 4.4.3 (the decision framework these six
visuals implement); KA 4.3 (where these visuals are assembled into a report).

### 4.4.7 From data to decision: the narrative arc and the one-slide story

**Definition & purpose.** A correct chart is necessary but not sufficient: a busy sponsor needs to be told,
explicitly, what the data means and what to do about it. This topic gives a repeatable narrative structure —
**situation → complication → insight → recommendation** — for turning a dataset into a decision-focused
story, and applies it to a full Meridian Rail Contracting LLC mini-case.

**The underlying principle.** Each stage of the arc does a distinct job: **situation** states the baseline
fact the audience already broadly expects (often the headline KPI); **complication** introduces the specific
tension or risk that makes the situation worth a decision, rather than just a status update; **insight**
connects the situation and complication into the one conclusion that matters — the "so what"; and
**recommendation** states the specific action, owner and timeframe the story is leading to. A chart or
dashboard (4.4.1–4.4.6) supplies the *evidence*; the arc supplies the *argument* — a report with excellent
charts but no argument leaves the reader to construct their own conclusion, which is exactly the failure
mode a management report exists to prevent (cross-reference KA 4.3).

**Mini-case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC, Pipeline Segment 4B: the Month-3 one-slide story.**

*Situation.* At the Month-3 data date, Pipeline Segment 4B looks healthy on every headline EVM measure: CV
+$50,000, SV +$60,000, CPI 1.18, SPI 1.22 — comfortably in the "healthy" quadrant (KA 6.2.5), and TCPI
(0.84) is well below the CPI already being delivered, so BAC remains readily achievable (KA 6.2.6).

*Complication.* That headline masks two things sitting directly ahead of the still-incomplete trenching work
package (WP3, only 45% earned by Month 3): the two largest quantified risks on the register, R1 (adverse
ground conditions, $70,000, 30% probability) and R2 (utility clash rework, $60,000, 20% probability) —
together $130,000, roughly 22% of BAC (KA 12.2) — have not yet been triggered or retired, and WP2 (pipe &
valve procurement, $150,000) still carries only start credit under the 50/50 method, with the ordered
materials not yet delivered to site.

*Insight.* A favourable Month-3 snapshot is not the same as a favourable outcome: the project's strongest
risk exposure sits precisely where its schedule is least complete, so the current CPI/SPI position could
reverse within one or two reporting periods if either R1 or R2 crystallises before WP3 finishes — the same
completion-point caution that applies to SPI late in a project (KA 6.2.7) applies here in reverse, early in
a project, to risk timing.

*Recommendation.* Commission the geotechnical/utility survey already evaluated as a positive-expected-value
decision in KA 12.2's Fig 12.2.5 decision tree before committing further trenching spend, and set an
explicit CPI/SPI re-forecast trigger for the Month-4 status date rather than waiting for the normal
quarterly cycle — owner: the Pipeline Segment 4B project controls lead; due: before Month-4 trenching
mobilisation.

**Fig 4.4.7 — The Meridian Pipeline Segment 4B one-slide story.**
Chart type: a single-slide layout, top-to-bottom in three bands. Top band (roughly 20% of slide height): one
headline sentence in large type — "Healthy today, exposed tomorrow: $130,000 of top-two risk sits directly
ahead of unfinished trenching" — this is the *insight*, stated first, not buried. Middle band (roughly 55%):
the supporting evidence in one compact combination visual — the CPI/SPI bullet scorecard (Fig 4.4.6b) on the
left, and a simplified schedule strip on the right showing WP3's Month 2–5 window with R1 and R2 flagged as
risk markers sitting on the still-unexecuted portion of that bar. Bottom band (roughly 25%): a boxed
recommendation in brand blue `#1D4ED8` — the survey decision, the re-forecast trigger, the owner and the due
date — set apart visually from the evidence above it so the "what to do" is never mistaken for more
evidence.

*Underlying data:* CPI 1.18, SPI 1.22 (KA 6.2); R1 $70,000/30%, R2 $60,000/20% (KA 12.2); WP2 $150,000
(50/50, start credit only), WP3 $200,000 (45% complete) (KA 6.1.4).

**Animation 4.4.7 — "Building the argument, not just the chart" [digital-only].** Frame 1: a blank slide.
Frame 2: the middle-band evidence (scorecard and schedule strip) fades in first, alone — an accurate but
argument-free chart. Frame 3: the top-band headline sentence types itself in above the evidence, turning the
same chart into a claim. Frame 4: the bottom-band recommendation box drops into place, completing situation
→ complication → insight → recommendation as one continuous read. Narration cue: "The chart never changed.
What changed is that it now has to lead somewhere."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Leading with the recommendation or the raw numbers and leaving the audience to infer the insight
  themselves — the arc exists precisely so the "so what" is stated, not assumed.
- Writing an "insight" that merely restates the situation in different words (e.g. "CPI is 1.18, which is
  good") rather than genuinely connecting situation and complication into a forward-looking conclusion.
- Building a technically accurate one-slide story around a complication that is not, in fact, material
  (cross-reference KA 4.1's thresholds/materiality) — the arc is persuasive by design, which makes an
  unjustified complication actively misleading, not just weak.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI drafting tools are genuinely capable, today, of turning a data table and a short
prompt into a fluent situation–complication–insight–recommendation narrative (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA
13.5) — a real and valuable acceleration of the drafting step. The tool has no accountability for whether
the "insight" it drafts is actually correct: asked to summarise Pipeline Segment 4B's Month-3 position from
the EVM figures alone, a GenAI tool with no visibility of the risk register could easily draft a confidently
favourable story and miss the R1/R2 exposure that is the entire point of this mini-case. The professional
must verify that an AI-drafted narrative's insight is built on the *complete*, correctly cross-referenced
dataset — not just the most readily available numbers — before it reaches a steering committee. This is the
clearest instance in this KA of **"AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable."**

**Cross-references.** KA 4.3 (management reporting, narrative vs numbers); KA 6.2.5, 6.2.6 (the
health-quadrant and TCPI figures used in the situation); KA 12.2 (R1–R2 and the geotechnical-survey decision
tree); KA 4.1 (materiality/thresholds, relevant to judging whether a complication belongs on the slide).

### Key terms

- **Visual encoding** — mapping a data value onto a visual property (position, length, angle, area, colour)
  that the eye decodes back into a number.
- **Graphical-perception hierarchy** — the ranking of how accurately different encodings are read: position
  along a common scale, then length, then angle/slope, then area/volume, least accurately.
- **Data-ink ratio** — the proportion of a chart's visible ink that encodes actual data, as opposed to
  decoration.
- **Chart-junk** — gridlines, borders, 3D bevels, shadows, redundant legends and decorative elements that
  carry no data.
- **Lie Factor** — `(size of effect shown in the graphic) / (size of effect in the data)`; a diagnostic for
  axis-based and other visual distortion.
- **Small multiples** — several simple charts, each answering one question clearly, used instead of one
  overloaded combination chart.
- **Colour-vision deficiency** — reduced ability to distinguish certain hues (most commonly red–green);
  addressed by pairing colour with position/label/shape and choosing colour-blind-safe palettes.
- **RAG (Red-Amber-Green)** — a three-state status colour convention, made accessible by pairing colour with
  a symbol or label.
- **Bullet chart** — a compact status format (Stephen Few) showing an actual value, background performance
  bands and a comparative marker on one horizontal bar.
- **Inverted pyramid** — a layout discipline placing the most important fact first, supporting detail next,
  full granularity last.
- **Gestalt principle of proximity** — elements placed close together are perceived as related.
- **Variance/exposure waterfall** — a chart bridging a starting value through named positive/negative steps
  to an ending value.
- **Tornado diagram** — a horizontal bar chart ranking input variables by the size of their individual effect
  on an output, sorted largest to smallest.
- **Sturges' rule** — `k = 1 + log₂(n)`, a standard rule of thumb for choosing the approximate number of
  histogram bins.
- **Situation–complication–insight–recommendation arc** — the four-stage narrative structure for turning
  data into a decision-focused story.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 4.4.1 [topic 4.4.1, level: recall]**
What does the "data-ink ratio" measure?
A. The proportion of a chart's colours that are brand-approved
B. **The proportion of a chart's visible ink that encodes actual data, as opposed to decoration** ✔
C. The ratio of chart height to chart width
D. The number of data points a chart can display before becoming cluttered
*Rationale:* B is Tufte's data-ink ratio definition. A confuses it with brand compliance; C describes aspect
ratio; D describes data density/cardinality, a different concept.

**MCQ 4.4.2 [topic 4.4.2, level: application — numerical]**
A bar chart shows two values, 120 and 100 (a true 20% difference). Its vertical axis is truncated to run
from 90 to 130, so the bars' visual heights are 30 and 10 respectively (a 200% depicted difference). What is
the chart's Lie Factor?
A. 0.10
B. 2.00
C. **10.0** ✔
D. 20.0
*Rationale:* Lie Factor = depicted effect / actual effect = 200% / 20% = 10.0. A inverts the ratio; B
mistakenly treats the depicted percentage as if divided by 100 rather than by the actual effect; D confuses
the answer with the actual data's raw percentage doubled.

**MCQ 4.4.3 [topic 4.4.3, level: application]**
A controls professional needs to show how a $600,000 project budget is divided among five work packages.
Following the message-driven chart-selection framework, which chart is the strongest choice?
A. A line chart connecting the five work packages in sequence
B. **A sorted bar chart, or a 100%-stacked bar for share-of-whole** ✔
C. A 3D pie chart, for visual interest
D. A scatter chart plotting each work package's budget against its name
*Rationale:* This is a comparison/composition message, best served by a bar or stacked-bar chart
(length-based comparison). A wrongly implies a trend between unrelated categories; C uses the least accurate
encoding (area/angle in 3D) for this message; D misapplies a relationship chart to categorical, not
two-variable numeric, data.

**MCQ 4.4.4 [topic 4.4.4, level: recall]**
Which practice best protects a RAG (Red-Amber-Green) status chart from becoming unreadable for a
colour-vision-deficient reader?
A. Using the most saturated red, amber and green available
B. Adding a rainbow gradient between the three states for smoother transitions
C. **Pairing each colour with a distinct symbol or label, so status does not depend on colour alone** ✔
D. Printing the chart only in colour, never in greyscale
*Rationale:* C follows the "never encode critical information in colour alone" discipline. A does not
address colour-vision deficiency (saturation alone does not fix hue confusion); B introduces a rainbow
palette, itself a distortion risk (4.4.2); D is the opposite of good practice — a chart should remain
legible in greyscale.

**MCQ 4.4.5 [topic 4.4.6, level: analysis]**
A project team builds two charts from the same four quantified risks: a waterfall bridging the budget
through each risk's impact to a combined exposure figure, and a tornado diagram ranking the same four risks
by individual dollar impact. Why might a stakeholder reasonably want to see both?
A. They always show identical information, so either is sufficient
B. **The waterfall answers "what is the cumulative exposure if every risk occurs," while the tornado
   answers "which single risk matters most" — different, complementary questions** ✔
C. The tornado is simply a rotated version of the waterfall
D. Only the waterfall is methodologically valid; the tornado should never be used with risk-register data
*Rationale:* B captures the genuine, complementary difference in the question each chart answers. A and C
wrongly treat them as equivalent/interchangeable; D is incorrect — a tornado is a standard, valid technique
for ranking risk drivers by individual sensitivity.

**MCQ 4.4.6 [topic 4.4.6, level: application — numerical]**
Using Sturges' rule, `k = 1 + log₂(n)`, approximately how many histogram bins are suggested for a
productivity sample of n = 40 daily readings?
A. 4
B. **6** ✔
C. 10
D. 40
*Rationale:* `k = 1 + log₂(40) = 1 + 5.32 = 6.32`, rounded to 6 bins. A underestimates by using too coarse a
rule; C overstates the count; D wrongly treats every data point as its own bin.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using Pipeline Segment 4B's Month-3 EV ($330,000) and AC ($280,000), calculate the Lie Factor of a chart
   whose vertical axis runs from $260,000 to $340,000. What Lie Factor would a properly zero-based chart
   have, and why does that matter for a chart used in a steering-committee pack?
2. A controls professional is choosing between a bar chart and a pie chart to show the same five-work-package
   budget composition used throughout this KA. Using the graphical-perception hierarchy from 4.4.1, explain
   why the bar chart is the stronger choice once more than two or three categories are involved.
3. Explain the difference in purpose between the variance/exposure waterfall (4.4.6c) and the tornado diagram
   (4.4.6f), even though both can be built from the same R1–R4 risk data.
4. Using the situation–complication–insight–recommendation arc, identify which stage of the Meridian
   one-slide story (4.4.7) is missing if a slide states only "CPI is 1.18 and SPI is 1.22" with no further
   text. What is lost by stopping there?

**Answers**

1. Effect in the data = `(330,000 − 280,000) / 280,000 = 17.9%`. Depicted effect on the truncated axis: bar
   heights are proportional to (value − 260,000) → EV height = 70,000, AC height = 20,000 → depicted effect
   = `(70,000 − 20,000) / 20,000 = 250%`. `Lie Factor = 250% / 17.9% ≈ 14.0`. A zero-based chart has a Lie
   Factor of 1.0 by construction, because the depicted and actual percentage differences are then identical.
   This matters because a steering committee reading the truncated chart would perceive an outperformance
   roughly fourteen times more dramatic than the genuine 17.9% variance, risking a decision based on a
   distorted impression rather than the real position.
2. The graphical-perception hierarchy ranks position/length as more accurately read than angle/area. A bar
   chart encodes each work package's budget as a length compared against a shared baseline — an accurately
   read comparison even across five categories. A pie chart encodes the same values as angles/areas of
   wedges, which are read less accurately and less consistently, especially once there are more than two or
   three slices to compare, making ranking (e.g. "is WP2 or WP3 larger?") harder to judge correctly by eye.
3. The waterfall answers a cumulative, "worst case if everything happens" question: it bridges the BAC
   through each risk's impact, in sequence, to a combined risk-adjusted exposure ($790,000). The tornado
   answers a comparative, "which single risk matters most" question: it ranks the same four risks
   independently by individual dollar impact, without summing them. Both are legitimate, complementary uses
   of the same underlying risk data, answering different management questions.
4. The slide as stated gives only the situation (the headline EVM position) — it omits the complication (the
