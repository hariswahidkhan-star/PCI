# Domain 4 — Performance Management, Variance Analysis & Management Reporting

## KA 4.3 — Management reporting

KA 4.1 defined the KPIs, targets and tolerance thresholds a project controls function tracks, and KA 4.2
turned raw budget-versus-actual data into formal cost, price, quantity and efficiency variances. Neither of
those is worth anything until it reaches the person who can act on it, in a form they can actually use,
before it is too late to matter. That is the job of **management reporting**: taking the variances, the
Earned Value Management (EVM) measures of Domain 6, the forecasts of Domain 3 and the risk register of
Domain 12, and packaging them — at the right level of detail, on the right cycle, for the right audience —
into a report someone will actually read and act on. PMI's PMBOK Guide frames this as the progression from
raw **work performance data** (unprocessed observations — hours logged, invoices posted) through analysed
**work performance information** (a variance, an index, a trend) to a compiled **work performance report**
(the physical or digital document circulated for decisions); AACE International's Total Cost Management
(TCM) Framework treats performance reporting as a core deliverable of its project performance measurement
and forecasting process area. Get the report wrong — too dense, too late, addressed to the wrong audience,
built on unreconciled data — and a correctly calculated variance never reaches the decision it should have
triggered. This KA covers the reporting hierarchy and its audiences, the standard suite of report types, the
anatomy of a report, RAG status, cadence, data quality and the assurance trail, audience tailoring,
forecasting narrative, and — in two fully worked mini-cases — builds an actual one-page monthly report and
an actual exception report for the recurring **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC** / **Pipeline Segment 4B**
illustrative contract (BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1, contract price
$750,000; KA 6.1–6.3, KA 11.1). It deliberately does not repeat KA 2.5's accounting-standards contrast
between management and statutory reporting, or KA 4.4's treatment of chart design and visual storytelling —
both are cross-referenced, not duplicated, throughout.

### 4.3.1 The reporting hierarchy and its audiences

**Definition & purpose.** A project generates one stream of performance data, but that data is consumed at
five progressively broader levels of an organisation: **project**, **programme** (a group of related
projects managed together for benefits neither could achieve alone), **portfolio** (an organisation's whole
collection of projects and programmes, prioritised against strategy), **executive/board**, and **client**
(an external party with a contractual right to be informed). Each level needs the same underlying truth, but
at a different altitude.

**The underlying principle.** As data moves up the hierarchy, *volume must fall and abstraction must rise*,
or the report becomes unusable at that level. A board member reviewing forty programmes cannot absorb
control-account-level detail for any one of them; a control account manager cannot act on a single portfolio
RAG dot. The professional's job is not to write one report and photocopy it upward — it is to **aggregate
and re-narrate the same source data** at each level, a discipline formalised in KA 4.3.7's "one truth, many
views" principle.

|---|---|---|---|---|

**Common pitfalls.**
- Sending the project-level cost report unedited to the board — either it is ignored (too dense) or, worse,
  a board member fixates on one line-item variance that is immaterial at their level.
- Reporting different, unreconciled figures at each level because each was rebuilt from a different source
  rather than aggregated from one (KA 4.3.6).
- Treating "client" as just another internal audience — the client relationship is contractual, and what is
  reported (and when) is often fixed by the contract terms, not by internal reporting preference.

**AI in this topic.** A reporting-automation platform can generate the project, programme, portfolio and
board views from a single underlying data model simultaneously, guaranteeing they reconcile by construction
(cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — a genuine efficiency and consistency gain over five separately
maintained reports. It cannot decide what a board actually needs to see this month versus routine noise; that
editorial judgement about materiality at each altitude remains the professional's.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.1 (KPIs and thresholds that populate each level's headline); KA 4.3.2 (report
types by audience); KA 4.3.7 (tailoring detail without changing the underlying truth); KA 8.2 (programme/
portfolio structures in planning).

### 4.3.2 Report types across the controls reporting suite

**Definition & purpose.** "A report" is not one thing. A controls function typically maintains six distinct
report types, each with a different purpose, trigger and shelf life. Confusing them — most commonly, treating
an exception report as just an unscheduled cost report — undermines the very urgency they are designed to
convey.

**The underlying principle.** Each report type answers a different question, on a different trigger:

| Report type | Question it answers | Trigger | Typical length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Progress report | "What work has been done, and what's next?" | Scheduled cadence | 1–3 pages, narrative-led |
| Cost report | "Where does spend stand against budget?" | Scheduled cadence | 1–2 pages, table-led |
| Dashboard | "What is the live status, right now?" | Continuous (pull, not push) | 1 screen/page, visual |
| Monthly report | "What is the full cost/schedule/risk position this period?" | Monthly cadence | 1–4 pages, combines the above |
| Exception report | "Which specific metric breached its threshold, and why?" | A defined tolerance breach (KA 4.3.4) | 1 page, targeted |
| Flash report | "What just happened that can't wait for the next cycle?" | A significant event (not necessarily a numeric threshold) | Half a page, headline-only |

The distinction between the last two matters most in practice. An **exception report** is triggered
*specifically* by a measured KPI crossing a pre-agreed tolerance band (e.g. CPI or SPI falling below 0.90 —
worked in full in KA 4.3.10); it is data-driven and quantified. A **flash report** is triggered by a
*significant event* that may have no numeric threshold at all — a safety incident, a sudden client
instruction to stop work, a key subcontractor's insolvency — and its purpose is speed of notification, not
completeness of analysis; a fuller report follows once the position is understood.

**Example 4.3.2 — choosing the right report type.**

*Scenario.* Three situations arise on Pipeline Segment 4B in the same week: (1) the routine Month-3 data
date arrives; (2) a site incident closes one work face for two days; (3) at Month 5, CPI falls to 0.80 and
SPI to 0.75, both below Meridian's agreed 0.90 tolerance band.

*Required.* Identify the correct report type for each.

*Working/Answer.* (1) is the **monthly report** — scheduled, comprehensive (KA 4.3.9). (2) is a **flash
report** — an unscheduled, significant event with no numeric trigger, issued within hours, narrative-only,
to be followed by fuller detail in the next scheduled report. (3) is an **exception report** — a defined
tolerance breach on named KPIs, issued outside the monthly cycle with quantified detail (KA 4.3.10).

*Interpretation.* All three could, in principle, be forced into "an email to the client" — but naming and
structuring them differently signals urgency and content correctly, and lets recipients build a mental filing
system (e.g. "an exception report always has an EAC range attached; a flash report never does").

**Fig 4.3.2 — Report type comparison matrix.** *(the table above, rendered as a landscape comparison grid,
brand blue `#1D4ED8` header row, one row per report type, a fifth column added for "primary audience level"
mapped to Fig 4.3.1's pyramid.)*

**Animation 4.3.2 — "Same week, three report types" [digital-only].** Frame 1: a calendar week shows one
scheduled dot (the monthly report). Frame 2: an unscheduled flash icon fires over "site incident," producing
a half-page "Flash report — issued within hours" card. Frame 3: a CPI/SPI gauge drops through 0.90, firing a
full-page "Exception report — with EAC range" card. Narration cue: "The calendar only tells you about one of
these three reports in advance."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using "exception report" loosely for any bad-news update, diluting the specific, threshold-triggered
  meaning that makes it useful as an early-warning mechanism.
- Building a dashboard that is really just last month's cost report reformatted — a genuine dashboard is
  pulled/refreshed continuously, not pushed once a month (KA 4.4 develops dashboard design further).
- Skipping the flash report for an urgent event because "the monthly report is only three days away" — three
  days is a long time for an unbriefed client or board to hear about a significant event from someone else.

**AI in this topic.** Monitoring tools can watch incoming data feeds and classify an event as exception- or
flash-worthy against pre-set rules, drafting the initial notification (cross-reference KA 13.5). The
classification rule itself, and the decision to actually issue an external-facing report, remain the
professional's — a false-positive exception report to a client damages credibility, and a suppressed true
positive is worse.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.1 (the tolerance thresholds that trigger an exception report); KA 4.3.4 (RAG
status, closely related to the exception trigger); KA 4.4 (dashboard design in depth); KA 4.3.10 (the worked
exception report).

### 4.3.3 The anatomy of a project controls report

**Definition & purpose.** Whatever the report type, a competent project controls report shares a common
skeleton, built precisely to move a reader from PMBOK's "work performance data" to "work performance
information" without forcing them to do that translation themselves. This topic defines the seven standard
blocks used throughout this KA, most fully assembled in KA 4.3.9's worked monthly report.

**The underlying principle.** A report fails when it is either a **data dump** (raw figures with no
interpretation, forcing every reader to do their own analysis) or **narrative with no numbers** (a confident
story unsupported by traceable figures). The anatomy below exists specifically to force both elements — data
and interpretation — into every report, in a fixed, learnable order so a regular reader can navigate quickly
to the section they need.

**Structure — the seven-block anatomy.**

| Block | Content | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Header | Project, contract reference, period, data date, preparer, RAG summary | "What am I looking at, and as of when?" |
| 2. RAG dashboard | Cost, schedule, risk, safety/quality status at a glance (KA 4.3.4) | "Is this project healthy?" |
| 3. Cost summary | BAC, AC, EV, CV, CPI, EAC, VAC | "Are we on budget, and what will it finish at?" |
| 4. Schedule summary | PV, EV, SV, SPI, key milestone status | "Are we on time?" |
| 5. Narrative commentary | Driver, trend, action for each adverse item (KA 4.3.8) | "Why, and what is being done?" |
| 6. Risk & change | Top risks, open change/variation items | "What could still move this?" |
| 7. Actions & sign-off | Owner, due date, preparer/reviewer sign-off | "Who does what next, and who stands behind this report?" |

**Example 4.3.3 — auditing a draft report against the anatomy.**

*Scenario.* A draft Pipeline Segment 4B report contains blocks 1, 3 and 4 only — a header and two numeric
tables — with no RAG summary, no commentary and no named actions.

*Required.* Assess the draft against the seven-block anatomy and state the risk of issuing it as-is.

*Working.* Present: header, cost summary, schedule summary (blocks 1, 3, 4). Missing: RAG dashboard (2),
narrative commentary (5), risk & change (6), actions & sign-off (7).

*Answer.* **The draft is a data dump: four of seven blocks are missing, all of them the interpretive ones.**

*Interpretation.* A reader can extract CPI and SPI themselves, but the report does nothing to tell them
*why* those figures moved, *what* it means for the forecast, or *who* is doing something about it — exactly
the gap that turns a technically accurate report into a useless one (KA 4.3.8 develops the fix).

**Fig 4.3.3 — The seven-block report anatomy, as a one-page layout.**
Chart type: a rectangular page mock-up divided into seven labelled zones matching the table above, header
band across the top in brand blue `#1D4ED8`, RAG dashboard as three small traffic-light icons beneath it,
cost and schedule summaries as two side-by-side tables, commentary as a text block, risk/change as a small
table, actions/sign-off as a footer strip.

*Underlying data:* the seven-block table above (block order and content only; the fully populated version
appears in Fig 4.3.9).

**Animation 4.3.3 — "Filling the anatomy, block by block" [digital-only].** Frame 1: a blank seven-zone page
outline. Frame 2: header and RAG dots (turning green) fill in. Frame 3: cost and schedule tables populate.
Frame 4: a commentary paragraph types in, followed by a risk line and a signature strip. Narration cue:
"Every block answers a question a reader would otherwise have to ask separately."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating the anatomy as decoration rather than structure — a report with all seven headings but empty or
  boilerplate content under commentary and actions is still, functionally, a data dump.
- Reordering the blocks project-by-project so regular readers cannot navigate a report on autopilot.
- Omitting block 7 (sign-off) — an unattributed report has no accountable owner if a figure later proves
  wrong (KA 4.3.6 develops this into the full assurance trail).

**AI in this topic.** A report-generation tool can auto-populate blocks 1, 3 and 4 from source systems (KA
13.4, KA 13.5) — the mechanical, data-assembly part of the job. Blocks 5–7 require judgement — commentary
needs to know *why*, and sign-off needs a named accountable human; a report with AI-drafted commentary
substituted without review is still a data dump wearing a narrative's clothes.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.3.4 (RAG, block 2); KA 4.3.8 (commentary, block 5); KA 4.3.6 (the assurance trail
behind block 7's sign-off); KA 4.3.9 (the fully worked, populated example).

### 4.3.4 RAG status: setting and applying thresholds

**Definition & purpose.** RAG status (Red/Amber/Green) is a three-state traffic-light summary that lets a
reader assess health in under a second, before reading a single figure. It is the single most consumed part
of any project controls report at the programme, portfolio and board levels (Fig 4.3.1), which is exactly
why it must rest on a defined, defensible rule rather than a preparer's gut feel.

**The underlying principle.** RAG is only meaningful if the bands are set **in advance**, against agreed
tolerance thresholds (KA 4.1), and applied **consistently** every period — a RAG rating chosen after the
fact to make a report look better is not a control, it is a cosmetic exercise. This KA uses the following
illustrative cost/schedule tolerance bands, applied to cumulative CPI and SPI (KA 6.2); organisations vary
this convention, and it is not a fixed standard — see the "For SME verification" note.

**Structure — illustrative RAG tolerance bands.**

| Status | CPI or SPI range | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Green | ≥ 0.95 | On or ahead of target; routine monitoring |
| Amber | 0.90 to < 0.95 | Early warning; watch, no formal escalation yet |
| Red | < 0.90 | Tolerance breach; mandatory exception report (KA 4.3.10) |

**Aggregation rule.** Where a project tracks both CPI and SPI (and, commonly, additional risk/safety/quality
indicators), the overall RAG status is set by the **worst-of** rule: the project's headline status is the
worst individual status among its tracked indicators, never an average. A project with CPI Green and SPI Red
is reported Red overall.

**Example 4.3.4 — applying the RAG rule to Pipeline Segment 4B at two data dates.**

*Scenario.* Month 3: CPI 1.18, SPI 1.22 (KA 6.2.3, 6.2.4). Month 5: CPI 0.80, SPI 0.75 (KA 6.3.2).

*Required.* RAG status at each data date, applying the bands above and the worst-of rule.

*Working.* Month 3: CPI 1.18 ≥ 0.95 → Green; SPI 1.22 ≥ 0.95 → Green; worst-of → **Green**.
Month 5: CPI 0.80 < 0.90 → Red; SPI 0.75 < 0.90 → Red; worst-of → **Red**.

*Answer.* **Month 3: Green/Green (overall Green). Month 5: Red/Red (overall Red) — a mandatory exception
report is triggered (KA 4.3.10).**

*Interpretation.* The same tolerance rule, applied without judgement at two different data dates on the same
project, produces two entirely different reporting actions — that consistency is what gives RAG its
authority; a reader trusts a Red rating precisely because they know it wasn't a judgement call.

**Fig 4.3.4 — RAG threshold bands applied to CPI/SPI, Month 3 vs Month 5.**
Chart type: horizontal gauge/bullet chart, two gauges (CPI, SPI) each scaled 0.60–1.30, with green/amber/red
background bands per the table above. Two markers per gauge in brand blue `#1D4ED8`: one for Month 3, one for
Month 5.

*Underlying data:*

| Indicator | Month 3 | Month 5 | Green band | Amber band | Red band |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CPI | 1.18 | 0.80 | ≥ 0.95 | 0.90–0.95 | < 0.90 |
| SPI | 1.22 | 0.75 | ≥ 0.95 | 0.90–0.95 | < 0.90 |

**Animation 4.3.4 — "Watching a marker cross into Red" [digital-only].** Frame 1: both gauges show Month-3
markers sitting in the green band. Frame 2: the markers slide left through amber into red by Month 5, pulsing
with a callout: "Threshold breached — exception report triggered." Narration cue: "RAG isn't a colour someone
picks — it's where the marker lands against a line drawn in advance."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Setting the bands loosely enough that almost nothing is ever Red — this defeats RAG's purpose as an
  early-warning mechanism and, over time, trains readers to ignore Amber too.
- Averaging CPI and SPI into a single blended score before applying RAG — this is the same error KA 6.2.5
  warns against for the underlying indices, and it hides a project that is badly behind on one dimension
  while merely fine on the other.
- Re-basing the thresholds mid-project to avoid reporting Red — any change to the tolerance bands themselves
  must be a documented, approved governance decision, not a quiet adjustment.

**AI in this topic.** A dashboard tool can apply the RAG rule automatically the instant CPI/SPI refresh,
removing any temptation to round a 0.897 CPI up to "basically Amber" (cross-reference KA 4.4, KA 13.5). It
cannot decide whether the bands themselves suit this contract's risk appetite, nor override the worst-of rule
with a persuasive "but the trend is improving" — that governance judgement belongs to the professional.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.1 (KPI thresholds and tolerances, in depth); KA 6.2 (CPI, SPI, the health
quadrant); KA 4.3.10 (the Red status that triggers the worked exception report); KA 4.3.9 (RAG as block 2 of
the report anatomy).

### 4.3.5 Reporting cadence and the reporting calendar

**Definition & purpose.** **Cadence** is the fixed rhythm at which each report type is produced; the
**reporting calendar** is the documented schedule that turns that rhythm into specific dates so every
contributor (site, commercial, finance, scheduling) knows when their input is due and every recipient knows
when to expect their report.

**The underlying principle.** Cadence should match the **decision cycle** it serves, not be set arbitrarily.
Site-level cost data changes daily but is only useful in aggregate weekly; a client contractually expects a
monthly formal position; a board reviewing forty programmes cannot absorb more than a quarterly roll-up
without exception reporting filling the gaps in between. Cadence that is too frequent for the audience wastes
preparation effort on reports nobody has time to read; cadence that is too infrequent leaves a threshold
breach undiscovered for weeks (which is precisely why exception reporting exists *outside* the fixed
calendar, KA 4.3.2).

**Example 4.3.5 — building Meridian's reporting calendar for Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's project controls function sets the following cadence for the contract, aligned to a
six-month, monthly-data-date programme (KA 6.1's Month 1–6 structure).

*Required.* A reporting calendar for one quarter (Months 1–3), showing report type, audience and typical
turnaround from data date to issue.

*Working/Answer.*

| Report type | Cadence | Audience | Turnaround (data date → issue) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site progress update | Weekly (every Monday) | Internal project team | Same day |
| Cost report (internal) | Monthly, aligned to the data date | Meridian project/commercial management | 3 working days |
| Monthly report (client-facing) | Monthly, aligned to the data date | Client capital-programme office | 5 working days (allows internal review first) |
| Programme dashboard | Continuous (refreshed weekly, reviewed monthly) | Programme director | N/A (pull) |
| Board report | Quarterly (Months 3, 6, …) | Meridian board | 10 working days (rolls up Months 1–3) |
| Exception/flash report | Ad hoc, on trigger | Escalation chain per KA 4.3.2/4.3.4 | Within 2 working days of the breach |

*Interpretation.* Note the deliberate 2-working-day gap between the internal cost report and the client-
facing monthly report — internal review and sign-off (KA 4.3.6) always precedes external release; and the
exception report's own turnaround target (2 working days) is far tighter than the routine monthly cycle,
because its entire purpose is to beat that cycle.

**Fig 4.3.5 — Meridian's reporting calendar, one quarter.**
Chart type: horizontal calendar/Gantt strip, weeks 1–13 along the top, one row per report type, coloured
blocks in brand blue `#1D4ED8` marking each issue date; the board report row shows a single block at week 13

**Animation 4.3.5 — "A quarter of reporting, compressed" [digital-only].** Frame 1: an empty 13-week calendar
strip fills with weekly site-update dots. Frame 2: three larger monthly blocks appear at weeks 4, 8 and 12.
Frame 3: a wide board-report bracket appears at week 13, absorbing the monthly blocks beneath it. Frame 4: a

**Common pitfalls.**
- Publishing a client-facing report before the internal review/sign-off step has actually happened, under
  calendar pressure — the calendar sets a target, not licence to skip the assurance trail (KA 4.3.6).
- Letting cadence drift (a "monthly" report that in practice slips to six weeks) without renegotiating the
  commitment — this quietly erodes the client's or board's trust in the calendar itself.
- Assuming exception reporting can be scheduled — by definition it is triggered, not calendared; a reporting
  calendar with no explicit ad hoc/exception row implicitly assumes nothing will ever breach.

**AI in this topic.** Workflow-automation tools can track the calendar, chase overdue inputs, and
auto-generate a report's routine sections the moment source data is available (cross-reference KA 13.5),
tightening the turnarounds above. They cannot judge whether a report is fit to leave the organisation just
because the calendar says it is due — the turnaround target is a service standard, not permission to skip
block 7's sign-off.

### 4.3.6 Data quality, the single source of truth and the report assurance trail

**Definition & purpose.** A **single source of truth (SSOT)** is the principle that every figure appearing
in any report, at any level of the hierarchy, is traceable back to one authoritative source system (the
ERP/cost ledger for AC, the scheduling tool for PV, the progress-measurement system for EV) rather than being
independently re-keyed or re-derived by each report's preparer. The **assurance trail** is the documented
record of who prepared, reconciled and approved a report before it was issued.

**The underlying principle.** A report is only as trustworthy as its worst unreconciled input. KA 2.5.3
already showed the mechanics of tying a project's actual-cost-to-date figure back to its ledger control
account (labour + materials + equipment + subcontract); that reconciliation step is not optional detail — it
is what allows block 7's sign-off (KA 4.3.3) to mean something. Two reports quoting different AC figures for
the same project on the same date is not a rounding quirk; it is a control failure, because it means at least
one of them was not built from the single source of truth.

**Structure — the report assurance trail.**

| Step | Actor | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Source systems | Ledger (AC), schedule tool (PV), progress system (EV) hold the authoritative data |
| 2 | Preparer | Extracts data, reconciles it to source (e.g. AC ties to the ledger control account) |
| 3 | Preparer | Calculates variances/indices/EAC, drafts narrative commentary |
| 4 | Reviewer | Independently checks figures and narrative against source data |
| 5 | Approver | Signs off the report as fit to issue (named, dated) |
| 6 | Recipient | Receives the report with a visible preparer/reviewer/approver trail |

**Example 4.3.6 — a reconciliation failure caught before issue.**

*Scenario.* The Month-5 Pipeline Segment 4B draft report shows AC of $506,250 (KA 6.3.2's accrual-adjusted
figure). During review, the reviewer finds the underlying ledger extract totals only $481,250 — a $25,000
gap, because a subcontractor accrual for work performed but not yet invoiced had not been posted at the time
of the ledger pull.

*Required.* What should happen before the report is issued?

*Working.* `Gap = 506,250 − 481,250 = 25,000`. This is exactly the accrual-adjustment issue flagged
generally in KA 6.1.3/6.2.1: an AC figure that excludes a known, unbilled accrual materially understates cost
and, downstream, overstates CPI.

*Answer.* **The report is held. The $25,000 accrual is confirmed and posted (or the AC figure is corrected
to include it) before the report is reissued for sign-off — not released on the unreconciled $481,250
figure.**

*Interpretation.* This is precisely what step 4 of the assurance trail exists to catch; a report that skips
independent review to save a day risks issuing a materially wrong CPI to a client or board, which is far
costlier to correct after the fact than the delay would have been.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating "the numbers came from the system" as automatic proof of correctness — a system extract is only as
  good as what has been posted to it as of the pull date (as the accrual gap above shows).
- Skipping independent review under deadline pressure, especially for a report that "looks the same as last
  month" — a stable-looking report can still contain a new, uncaught error.
- No visible, named sign-off trail — if a figure is later challenged, an unattributed report gives no way to
  establish who checked what.

**AI in this topic.** Reconciliation tools can compare a report's figures against source-system extracts and
flag gaps like the $25,000 accrual example the moment they appear, faster and more consistently than a manual
tie-out (cross-reference KA 5.2, KA 13.5). They cannot determine *why* a gap exists, or authorise the report
to proceed once it is explained — that diagnostic and sign-off judgement remains with the named reviewer and
approver.

**Cross-references.** KA 2.5.3 (the ledger reconciliation mechanics this topic builds on, not repeated here);
KA 5.2 (the cost control cycle and ledger extraction generally); KA 6.1.3 (the accrual-adjustment issue in

sign-off, as part of the anatomy).

### 4.3.7 Tailoring detail to the audience: one truth, many views

**Definition & purpose.** Building on KA 4.3.1's hierarchy, this topic gives the operating rule for producing
different reports at different altitudes **without ever changing the underlying figures** — only their level
of aggregation, framing and narrative depth change.

**The underlying principle.** "Tailoring" means choosing what to *omit and aggregate*, never what to
*change*. A CV of +$50,000 at project level is the same $50,000 whether it appears as a single line in a
board report or is decomposed into ten control-account variances in a project-level report; if the board
figure and the project figure do not reconcile to the same underlying $50,000, that is not tailoring, it is
an error (KA 4.3.6).

**Example 4.3.7 — the same Month-3 CV, tailored to three audiences.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's Month-3 CV is +$50,000 (favourable; EV $330,000, AC $280,000; KA 6.2.1),
built up from three control accounts: earthworks +$30,000, materials +$15,000, subcontract +$5,000.

*Required.* Show how the same $50,000 is presented at project, programme and board level.

*Working/Answer.*
- **Project level (internal cost report):** all three control-account lines shown individually, each with
  its own driver commentary — "earthworks favourable due to better-than-planned ground conditions,"
  "materials favourable due to a bulk-purchase discount," "subcontract broadly on plan."
- **Programme level (dashboard):** a single Pipeline Segment 4B row reading "CV +$50,000 (favourable),
  Green" alongside equivalent rows for sibling projects in the programme — no control-account breakdown.
- **Board level (board report):** Pipeline Segment 4B appears only if it is the programme's most material
  item or an exception; otherwise it is absorbed into a single "rail-signalling programme: aggregate CV
  +$X" figure covering all segments together.

*Interpretation.* All three views trace back to the identical $50,000 and the identical three control-account
figures — nothing was recalculated, only re-aggregated and re-narrated for the reader's decision-making
altitude.

**Fig 4.3.7 — "One truth, many views": the Month-3 CV traced through three audiences.**
Chart type: a funnel/tree diagram, three control-account boxes (earthworks +$30,000; materials +$15,000;
subcontract +$5,000) converging into a single project-level box (+$50,000, brand blue `#1D4ED8`), which then

**Common pitfalls.**
- Independently recalculating a "summary" figure for a higher audience rather than genuinely aggregating the
  lower-level figures — the two should be mathematically identical, never merely similar.
- Over-aggregating early — hiding a materially adverse control account inside a favourable overall project
  total, so the board never learns a specific risk exists until it grows large enough to move the whole
  project total.
- Under-aggregating for a board audience — presenting control-account-level detail "just in case," which
  defeats the purpose of tailoring and re-creates the data-dump problem at the wrong altitude.

**AI in this topic.** A well-built reporting data model can generate every audience's view from the same
underlying figures automatically, guaranteeing the "one truth" property by construction (cross-reference KA
13.5) — one of the lowest-risk automation cases in this KA, since aggregation arithmetic is fully
deterministic. Deciding *where* to draw the aggregation line for a given audience remains the professional's,
informed by KA 4.3.4's thresholds and KA 4.1's materiality guidance.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.3.1 (the hierarchy this topic applies to); KA 6.2.1 (the Month-3 CV and its
control-account build-up); KA 4.1 (materiality and thresholds); KA 4.3.6 (the single-source-of-truth
discipline that makes tailoring safe).

### 4.3.8 Forecasting and narrative commentary

**Definition & purpose.** Narrative commentary is the prose that converts a variance, index or forecast into
something a reader can act on: not just *what* the number is, but *why* it moved and *what happens next*. The
IASB's (non-mandatory) **IFRS Practice Statement 1 Management Commentary**, though written for statutory
narrative reporting (KA 2.5.2), states the same principle project controls reporting relies on: numbers alone
do not tell the story of performance; the narrative that links them to causes and future expectations is what
makes them useful to a reader.

**The underlying principle.** Good project controls commentary follows a fixed three-part pattern for every
material item: **driver** (what specifically caused the movement — never a vague restatement of the number
itself), **trend** (is it isolated or continuing — directly informing the EAC method choice of KA 6.3.6), and
**action** (what is being done, by whom, by when). Commentary that restates the number ("CPI is 0.80, which
is below target") without any of the three adds nothing a reader could not get from the table itself.

**Example 4.3.8 — from a bare number to usable commentary, with the AI-drafting step made visible.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5: CPI 0.80, SPI 0.75 (Red, KA 4.3.4). A GenAI drafting tool is given
the raw EVM table and asked to "write commentary."

*Required.* (a) The AI's first-draft commentary; (b) the professional's verification and final version.

*Working.*
(a) **AI first draft:** "Cost and schedule performance are below target this period, with CPI at 0.80 and
SPI at 0.75. Management should monitor the situation closely." — This restates the numbers and adds a
generic instruction; it names no driver, does not distinguish isolated from trending, and assigns no owner or
date. Left unverified, it would satisfy the letter of block 5 (KA 4.3.3) while failing its purpose entirely.

(b) **Professional's verified, final commentary:** "CPI (0.80) and SPI (0.75) have deteriorated from a
favourable Month-3 position (CPI 1.18, SPI 1.22) due to a confirmed equipment failure on the critical-path
earthworks package, now resolved, plus a resulting schedule slip requiring paid weekend shifts to protect the
Month-6 finish. This is assessed as a *trending*, not isolated, cause (three-period CPI trend 0.80–0.82, KA
6.3.3) — EAC Method 3 ($831,250, KA 6.3.7) is the recommended reporting basis. Action: site manager to confirm
acceleration cost by [date]; commercial director to review contingency drawdown by [date]."

*Answer.* **The AI draft is a starting point only; the verified version adds the specific driver, the
trend/isolated diagnosis, the linked EAC method and two named, dated actions — none of which the AI tool
could supply from the raw table alone.**

*Interpretation.* This is the sharpest, most concrete illustration in this KA of "AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable": the AI draft is fast and grammatically fine, but it is not *wrong* so much as
*empty* — it cannot know the equipment-failure cause, cannot make the one-off-versus-trend judgement KA
6.3.6 requires, and cannot assign accountable actions. A reader given only the AI draft would be no better
informed than reading the table directly.

**Fig 4.3.8 — Bare number → AI first draft → verified commentary: three stages compared.**
Chart type: three-column comparison card, brand blue `#1D4ED8` header per column ("Raw data," "AI first
draft," "Verified commentary"), each populated with the corresponding text from Example 4.3.8.

*Underlying data:* the (a) and (b) text blocks above, plus the raw CPI 0.80/SPI 0.75 figures as column one.

**Animation 4.3.8 — "What the AI draft is missing" [digital-only].** Frame 1: the raw CPI/SPI table sits
alone. Frame 2: a generic AI-drafted paragraph appears beside it. Frame 3: three callouts light up over the

**Common pitfalls.**
- Issuing AI-drafted commentary unreviewed because it reads fluently — fluency is not the same as
  accountability or completeness, as Example 4.3.8 shows directly.
- Writing commentary that restates the number rather than explaining the driver — a common failure mode even
  in human-drafted reports, not unique to AI.
- Omitting the trend/isolated judgement from commentary on an adverse metric — this is the same diagnostic
  step KA 6.3.6 requires before choosing an EAC method, and the report's narrative should say explicitly
  which the team has concluded, not leave the reader to guess.

**AI in this topic.** Generative drafting tools speed up the first pass of turning a data table into prose,
and can consistently apply a house driver/trend/action template across many accounts at once (cross-reference
KA 13.3, KA 13.5). As Example 4.3.8 shows concretely, such a tool cannot supply the site-specific driver,
cannot make the one-off-versus-trend causal judgement, and cannot assign an accountable owner and date — every
AI-drafted commentary must be reviewed and completed by someone with direct project knowledge before it
reaches block 5; an unreviewed AI paragraph is a governance gap, not a finished deliverable.

**Cross-references.** KA 2.5.2 (IFRS Practice Statement 1 Management Commentary, the statutory-narrative
analogue); KA 6.3.6 (the one-off-versus-trend diagnosis this commentary must reflect); KA 4.3.3 (commentary
as block 5 of the report anatomy); KA 13.3 (prompting and verifying GenAI drafts, in depth); KA 13.6 (AI
governance and sign-off).

### 4.3.10 Exception reporting: a threshold breach on Pipeline Segment 4B

**Definition & purpose.** This closing topic works the scenario KA 4.3.2 and KA 4.3.4 have been building
toward: a real tolerance breach, and the exception report it triggers outside the normal monthly cycle.

**Example 4.3.10 — the Month-5 exception report.**

*Scenario.* At the Month-5 data date, Pipeline Segment 4B's position (KA 6.3.2–6.3.7) is: BAC $600,000; PV
$540,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250; CPI 0.80; SPI 0.75. Applying KA 4.3.4's rule, both indices are below the
0.90 Red threshold. Three EAC methods have been calculated (KA 6.3.7): Method 1 $701,250 (VAC adverse
$101,250), Method 2 $750,000 (VAC adverse $150,000), Method 3 $831,250 (VAC adverse $231,250) — the
best-evidenced figure this period, given the confirmed critical-path schedule slip requiring paid
acceleration (KA 6.3.4, KA 6.3.6).

*Required.* Compute the commercial impact against the $750,000 contract price and populate an exception
report.

*Working.* Forecast margin under each EAC method: `Forecast margin = Contract price − EAC`.
Method 1: `750,000 − 701,250 = 48,750` (6.5% of contract price).
Method 2: `750,000 − 750,000 = 0` (0.0% — exact breakeven).
Method 3: `750,000 − 831,250 = (81,250)` (−10.8% — a forecast **loss**).

*Answer.* **Depending on the EAC method adopted, forecast margin ranges from $48,750 favourable down to an
$81,250 loss — a $130,000 swing entirely explained by which assumption (KA 6.3.6) is applied to the same
underlying data.** On the best-evidenced Method 3 basis, the contract has moved from a budgeted 20.0% margin
to a forecast loss.

*Interpretation.* A forecast loss on a fixed-price contract is not merely a cost-control concern — under IAS
37 (KA 1.4, KA 2.4) it is the trigger for testing whether an **onerous contract provision** is now required in
the statutory accounts, recognising the *entire* expected loss immediately rather than spreading it over the
remaining percentage of completion (the mechanism worked in full in KA 2.5.4's mini-case). This is precisely
why the exception report escalates to the commercial director and finance, not only the project manager: the
Month-5 breach is simultaneously a project controls event and a potential statutory recognition event.

### Key terms

- **Reporting hierarchy** — the project → programme → portfolio → executive/board → client levels at which
  performance data is consumed, each requiring less detail and more abstraction than the last.
- **Work performance data / information / reports** — PMBOK's progression from raw observations, through
  analysed variances and indices, to the compiled document circulated for decisions.
- **Progress report, cost report, dashboard, monthly report, exception report, flash report** — the six
  standard report types, distinguished by trigger, content and turnaround (KA 4.3.2).
- **Seven-block report anatomy** — header; RAG dashboard; cost summary; schedule summary; narrative
  commentary; risk & change; actions & sign-off.
- **RAG status (Red/Amber/Green)** — a three-state health summary set against pre-agreed tolerance bands and
  aggregated by the worst-of rule, never averaged.
- **Reporting cadence / reporting calendar** — the fixed rhythm and documented schedule for each report type,
  matched to the decision cycle it serves.
- **Single source of truth (SSOT)** — the principle that every reported figure traces to one authoritative
  source system, never independently re-derived per report.
- **Report assurance trail** — the documented preparer/reviewer/approver chain that gives a report an
  accountable, traceable sign-off.
- **"One truth, many views"** — tailoring a report to its audience by aggregation and narration only, never
  by changing the underlying figures.
- **Driver / trend / action commentary pattern** — the three-part structure (what caused it; is it isolated or
  continuing; what is being done, by whom, by when) that turns a number into usable narrative.
- **Onerous contract provision** — the IAS 37 requirement (cross-ref KA 1.4, KA 2.4, KA 2.5.4) to recognise a
  contract's entire forecast loss immediately once identified — a statutory trigger an exception report on a
  deteriorating fixed-price contract may surface.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 4.3.1 [topic 4.3.1, level: recall]**
Which statement best describes how a report should change as it moves from project level to board level?
A. The underlying figures should be independently recalculated at each level for accuracy
B. **Volume should fall and abstraction should rise, while the underlying figures stay traceable to the same source** ✔
C. Only favourable variances should be carried up to the board
D. The report format should be identical at every level to ensure consistency
*Rationale:* B captures the "one truth, many views" principle (KA 4.3.1, 4.3.7). A describes a control
failure, not tailoring; C describes suppressing adverse information, a serious reporting integrity breach;
D ignores that different audiences need different levels of detail.

**MCQ 4.3.2 [topic 4.3.2, level: application]**
A subcontractor's sudden insolvency is discovered on site with no defined numeric KPI breach involved. Which
report type is most appropriate for the immediate notification?
A. Exception report
B. **Flash report** ✔
C. Dashboard
D. Board report
*Rationale:* A flash report is triggered by a significant event, not a measured threshold breach (KA 4.3.2) —
exactly this scenario. A is incorrect because no defined KPI tolerance has been breached; C is a continuous,
pulled view, not an urgent push notification; D is too infrequent for an urgent event.

**MCQ 4.3.3 [topic 4.3.4, level: application — numerical]**
Using the illustrative RAG bands (Green ≥ 0.95; Amber 0.90 to <0.95; Red <0.90) and the worst-of aggregation
rule, what is the overall RAG status for a project reporting CPI = 0.93 and SPI = 0.97?
A. Green, because SPI is favourable
B. **Amber** ✔
C. Red
D. The average of Green and Amber
*Rationale:* CPI 0.93 falls in the Amber band (0.90 to <0.95); SPI 0.97 falls in Green. The worst-of rule
takes the lower status, Amber (KA 4.3.4). A ignores the CPI reading; C misreads 0.93 as below 0.90; D applies
an averaging approach the KA explicitly rejects.

**MCQ 4.3.4 [topic 4.3.10, level: application — numerical]**
Pipeline Segment 4B has a $750,000 contract price. Under a given EAC method, the forecast total cost is
$831,250. What is the forecast margin, and what does it indicate?
A. +$81,250, a favourable margin
B. **−$81,250, a forecast loss** ✔
C. $0, breakeven
D. −$831,250, a total loss of the contract value
*Rationale:* `Forecast margin = Contract price − EAC = 750,000 − 831,250 = −81,250` — a forecast loss (KA
4.3.10), which is also the figure that triggers the IAS 37 onerous-contract test cross-referenced in that
topic. A reverses the sign; C is the Method 2 result on this same data set, not Method 3; D confuses the EAC
itself with the margin calculation.

**MCQ 4.3.5 [topic 4.3.6, level: analysis]**
A reviewer finds that a draft report's actual-cost figure does not match the ledger extract for the same
period. What does the single-source-of-truth principle require before the report is issued?
A. Issue the report as planned; minor reconciliation differences are normal
B. Use whichever of the two figures is more favourable to the project
C. **Hold the report, investigate and resolve the gap against the authoritative source, then reissue for
   sign-off** ✔
D. Remove the actual-cost figure from the report entirely to avoid the discrepancy
*Rationale:* C reflects the assurance-trail discipline in KA 4.3.6 (illustrated by the $25,000 accrual gap in
Example 4.3.6) — a report is only trustworthy once its figures reconcile to source. A and B both release an
unreconciled report; D removes required information rather than fixing the underlying data quality issue.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Explain, in your own words, why the "worst-of" rule (not an average) is used to aggregate CPI and SPI into
   an overall RAG status, referencing the health-quadrant idea from KA 6.2.5.
2. A project's Month-4 CPI is 0.92 and SPI is 0.96. Using the illustrative bands in KA 4.3.4, what is the
   overall RAG status, and would an exception report be triggered?
3. A colleague argues that once a reporting tool can auto-generate the cost and schedule tables, blocks 5–7
   of the seven-block anatomy (commentary, risk, sign-off) are no longer necessary. Explain why this is
   incorrect, using the AI-drafted commentary example in KA 4.3.8.
4. Using Pipeline Segment 4B's Month-5 data (contract price $750,000; EAC Method 2 = $750,000), calculate the
   forecast margin and state what it indicates about the contract's commercial position under that specific
   method.

**Answers**

1. CV/CPI and SV/SPI measure independent dimensions (cost and schedule); averaging them into one score, as
   KA 6.2.5's health quadrant shows, can mask a project that is badly behind on one dimension while adequate
   on the other (e.g. a Q3 "efficient but slow" project would average out to look healthier than it is). The
   worst-of rule ensures the more urgent of the two signals always drives the reported status, consistent with
   RAG's purpose as an early-warning mechanism, not a blended performance score.
2. CPI 0.92 falls in the Amber band (0.90 to <0.95); SPI 0.96 falls in Green. Worst-of → overall **Amber**.
   An Amber status is an early warning, not a defined tolerance breach (which requires a reading below 0.90),
   so a formal exception report is **not** triggered at this stage — routine monitoring continues, though the
   trend should be watched closely ahead of the next reporting cycle.
3. Blocks 1–4 (header, RAG, cost table, schedule table) are largely mechanical and can be reliably automated
   from reconciled source data. Blocks 5–7 require judgement an automation tool cannot supply on its own: block
   5 needs the specific, site-known driver behind a variance and the one-off-versus-trend diagnosis (KA
   4.3.8's AI-first-draft example showed a fluent but empty commentary lacking exactly this); block 6 requires
   knowing which risks are currently live; block 7 requires a named, accountable human to stand behind the
   report before it is issued. Removing these blocks would return the report to being a data dump.
4. `Forecast margin = Contract price − EAC = 750,000 − 750,000 = 0`. Under EAC Method 2, Pipeline Segment 4B
   is forecast to exactly break even — the entire originally budgeted $150,000 (20.0%) margin would be
   eroded, though (unlike Method 3's −$81,250 outcome) the contract would not yet be forecast as loss-making,
   so the IAS 37 onerous-contract test would not yet be triggered on this specific method's figures alone.
