# Domain 13 — AI for Project Controls & PM: Concepts, Tools & Practice

## KA 13.1 — AI foundations for professionals

Domain 13 is the flagship AI domain of the PCL‑AI Body of Knowledge (20% of the exam blueprint) and the
largest single domain in the book. Every other domain already carries an "AI in this domain / AI in this
topic" thread showing how AI touches accounting, budgeting, EVM, contracts, scheduling, agile delivery and
risk; Domain 13 is where that thread is taught properly, in depth, tool by tool and workflow by workflow.
This opening Knowledge Area, KA 13.1, lays the conceptual foundation everything else in the domain builds
on: what AI and machine learning actually are (and are not), how a large language model produces text and
why that mechanism causes hallucination, what "training" and "inference" mean, and — running through every
topic — an honest line between what today's AI is genuinely good at and where it remains unreliable for a
project‑controls professional. KA 13.2 then covers data as the fuel AI runs on; KA 13.3 covers prompting and
verification practice; KA 13.4 surveys tool categories by name; KA 13.5 applies AI across the full
project‑controls lifecycle; KA 13.6 covers governance, ethics and assurance in depth; KA 13.7 covers building
organisational capability. This KA does not repeat any of that later material — it gives the vocabulary and
mental model that makes it possible to read it critically.

Two commitments run through this KA and the whole domain. First, **honesty over hype**: AI is described here
as it actually behaves in 2026, including its structural limits (hallucination, non‑reproducibility, data
dependency, bias), not as marketing material presents it. Second, the governing principle that already
threads every other domain applies here in its strongest, most literal form: **"AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable."** A model, however capable, produces a proposal — a prediction, a probability, a
draft, a summary. A named, accountable professional decides what happens next. Nothing in this KA should be
read as licence to skip that step; the opposite is true — understanding *why* AI behaves as it does is what
makes verifying it possible.

### 13.1.1 What artificial intelligence is — and is not: rules, learning, and the narrow‑AI reality behind the hype

**Definition & purpose.** Artificial intelligence (AI) is the broad field of building systems that perform
tasks — recognising a pattern, generating language, making a prediction, supporting a decision — that would
require intelligence if a person did them. That single label covers two fundamentally different engineering
approaches, and confusing them is the single most common source of miscommunication between a project‑controls
professional and an AI vendor or IT function:

1. **Rules‑based (symbolic) systems.** A person writes explicit if‑then logic (e.g. "if the invoice amount
   differs from the purchase order by more than 2%, hold the payment for review"). The system is fully
   deterministic and fully explainable — it does exactly, and only, what it was told to do — but it is only
   as good as the rules a human anticipated in advance.
2. **Machine learning (ML) systems.** The software infers a mapping from data rather than being told the
   rule explicitly (13.1.2–13.1.3). It can find patterns a person did not think to write a rule for, but it
   is probabilistic, data‑dependent, and — unlike a rule — not fully explainable in the same direct sense.

**The underlying principle.** "AI," "machine learning," "deep learning" and "generative AI" are frequently
used interchangeably in vendor marketing. They are **not** synonyms. They are nested and overlapping
categories (Fig 13.1.1) with materially different capabilities, failure modes and — critically for a
governed profession — different audit and verification obligations. Precision in this vocabulary is not
pedantry: whether a tool is a deterministic rule, a narrow statistical predictor, or a generative language
model changes what can go wrong with it and how a professional should check its output (cross‑reference
13.6).

**Narrow AI vs. general AI.** Every AI system in commercial use today, including the most capable large
language models, is **narrow AI** (sometimes "weak AI"): it performs a bounded set of tasks it was designed
or trained for and has no general, human‑like understanding, no autonomous goals, and no common sense that
reliably transfers outside its trained or prompted task. **Artificial general intelligence (AGI)** — a
hypothetical system with human‑level, cross‑domain reasoning and autonomous learning — does not exist as of
this writing (2026), and there is no expert consensus on whether or when it might, with serious researchers'
public estimates ranging from within a decade to "not with current methods at all." This book treats AGI and
"superintelligence" claims as genuinely speculative and out of scope for a certification grounded in current
professional practice: a project‑controls professional's job is to govern the narrow AI tools available and
in use today, not to plan around a hypothetical future system. This is explicitly flagged as an area where
the field's consensus could shift — any specific capability claim in this domain should be re‑checked against
the current state of tools before being relied upon (cross‑reference 13.4, 13.7).

### 13.1.2 The three learning paradigms: supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning

**Definition & purpose.** "Learning," in the machine‑learning sense, means a system improves its performance
on a task by processing data, rather than by being explicitly programmed with the task's logic. Three
paradigms differ by what kind of data and feedback drive that improvement:

1. **Supervised learning.** The model learns from **labelled** examples — input–output pairs where the
   correct answer is already known — to predict a label for new, unseen inputs. Two sub‑types: classification
   (a discrete label, e.g. "will breach / will not breach") and regression (a continuous number, e.g. a
   predicted final cost). The great majority of ML currently deployed in business and project‑controls
   settings is supervised.
2. **Unsupervised learning.** The model learns structure from **unlabelled** data — there is no "right
   answer" given at all. Typical tasks: clustering (grouping similar control accounts by cost behaviour
   without being told the groups in advance) and anomaly detection (flagging cost postings that do not fit
   the pattern the model has learned to call "normal").
3. **Reinforcement learning (RL).** An agent learns by taking actions in an environment and receiving
   rewards or penalties, refining its behaviour ("policy") through repeated trial and error to maximise
   cumulative reward. RL has limited direct, everyday production use in project controls as of 2026 compared
   with supervised/unsupervised ML and generative AI; its most relevant link for this book's reader is
   indirect — a technique called **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)** is one of the methods
   used to train modern LLM assistants to follow instructions and produce more helpful, better‑behaved
   responses (bridging into 13.1.5).

**The underlying principle.** The right paradigm follows from the question being asked, not from habit or
fashion: "predict this known kind of outcome from historical examples" points to supervised learning;
"find groups or outliers I did not know to look for" points to unsupervised learning; "learn a sequence of
actions to optimise an outcome through trial and error" points to reinforcement learning.

**Example 13.1.2 — a supervised‑learning cost‑overrun classifier, end to end.**

*Scenario.* Meridian Rail Contracting LLC (KA 1.1) is itself a newly formed project company and does not yet
hold years of closed‑out history of its own. Its project controls function instead draws on the historical
portfolio of its wider contracting group: 240 closed‑out control accounts from comparable rail and
infrastructure contracts over the preceding three years, each with a known final outcome. The function wants
to build a supervised classifier that flags, early, which control accounts are at elevated risk of closing
with an adverse cost variance worse than −10% of budget — so review effort can be targeted before close‑out,
not after. The trained model will then be applied prospectively to Meridian's own live accounts, including
those on Pipeline Segment 4B (BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1; KA 6.1–6.3).

*Required.* Build and evaluate the classifier, and state what its output is (and is not) fit to be used for.

*Working — the six steps of a supervised‑learning build.*

1. **Define the label (y).** For each of the 240 historical accounts: `Breach = 1` if the final cost variance
   was worse than −10% of budget at close‑out, `Breach = 0` otherwise. This is what makes the exercise
   "supervised" — every historical example already carries a known, correct answer, established
   retrospectively from actual closed accounts.
2. **Define the features (X).** Recorded at each account's 20%‑complete milestone (deliberately early, so the
   model is useful as a *warning*, not hindsight): CPI and SPI at 20% complete (KA 6.2); number of open
   variations; number of subcontractors; original AACE estimate class (KA 3.2); work type.
3. **Split the data.** 180 accounts (75%) for **training**; 60 accounts (25%) held back for **testing**. The
   model must be judged on examples it has never seen — otherwise it could simply memorise the training data
   (a failure mode called **overfitting**) rather than learn a pattern that generalises to new accounts.
4. **Train.** A classical ML method suited to structured, tabular data of this size — e.g. logistic regression
   or a gradient‑boosted decision tree (deep learning would be an inappropriate, over‑complex choice here;
   see 13.1.4) — processes the 180 training examples and adjusts its internal **parameters** to minimise the
   gap between its predictions and the known labels. This step is **training**.
5. **Evaluate.** The trained model is run on the 60 held‑out test accounts' features only (not their labels)
   and its predictions are compared against the known actual outcomes. This step — applying an already‑trained
   model to new input — is **inference**.
6. **Read the result via a confusion matrix.** Of the 60 test accounts, 18 actually breached and 42 did not:

   | | Predicted breach | Predicted no breach | Total actual |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Actual breach | 14 (True Positive) | 4 (False Negative) | 18 |
   | Actual no breach | 6 (False Positive) | 36 (True Negative) | 42 |
   | Total predicted | 20 | 40 | 60 |

   `Accuracy = (TP + TN) / n = (14 + 36) / 60 = 50/60 = 83.3%`
   `Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 14 / 20 = 70.0%`
   `Recall = TP / (TP + FN) = 14 / 18 = 77.8%`

*Answer.* The classifier achieves **83.3% accuracy, 70.0% precision and 77.8% recall** on unseen data.

*Interpretation.* Precision of 70.0% means three in ten accounts the model flags as "at risk" turn out fine —
false alarms a reviewer will need to screen out. Recall of 77.8% means the model misses roughly one in five
accounts that do go on to breach (the four false negatives) — a residual risk that ordinary variance analysis
(KA 4.2) and EAC review (KA 6.3) must still catch regardless of what the model says. Note, too, that a trivial
model that always predicted "no breach" would score 42/60 = 70.0% accuracy while catching **zero** real
breaches — a reminder that accuracy alone can be a misleading headline number for an imbalanced outcome like
this one (18 breaches vs. 42 non‑breaches), which is exactly why precision and recall are reported alongside
it. Once validated, the trained model is deployed for monthly **inference** against Meridian's live control
accounts — producing a probability score to prioritise review, never a verdict in itself.

**Common misconceptions.**
- "Unsupervised" meaning "not supervised by any human" — it refers only to the *training data* lacking
  labels; humans still design, monitor and govern the whole pipeline.
- Assuming a single, newly formed project or entity has enough history of its own to train a reliable model —
  in practice, meaningful supervised ML of this kind needs portfolio‑scale historical data, which is exactly
  why group‑level data governance (KA 13.2) matters even for a brand‑new project company like Meridian.
- Treating accuracy as the only, or the best, headline metric — as shown above, it can look reassuring while
  the model is actually useless for the minority outcome that matters most (the breaches).
- Confusing classification (a category) with regression (a number) — both are supervised learning, but they
  answer different kinds of question and use different evaluation metrics.

**Capabilities & limits.** Supervised ML is genuinely effective at consistent, scalable pattern recognition
over structured historical data, at a volume no reviewer could manually re‑check every month. It is,
however, only as good as its label definition and its historical data (cross‑reference 13.2); it degrades
when the future stops resembling the past (a new contract type, a new client base, a market shift); and its
output is a probability score whose "flag" threshold is itself a policy choice someone must own, not a
neutral fact the model discovers (cross‑reference 13.6).

**Cross‑references.** KA 6.2 (CPI/SPI as model features); KA 3.2 (estimate class as a feature); KA 13.2 (the
data‑quality dependency this model illustrates); KA 13.5 (the deployed lifecycle application); KA 13.6

### 13.1.3 Model vs. data, training vs. inference: the mechanics common to every ML system

**Definition & purpose.** A **model** is a mathematical structure with adjustable internal **parameters**
that maps inputs to outputs. **Data** serves two distinct roles: the historical examples used to set those
parameters during training, and the new inputs later fed to an already‑trained model. **Parameters** are the
internal numeric values a model adjusts during training — from a handful in a simple regression equation to
many billions in a large language model. **Features** are the input variables a model consumes (CPI, SPI,
number of variations, in Example 13.1.2); **labels** are the known outcomes a supervised model is trained to
predict (Breach/No breach).

**The underlying principle.** **Training** happens once, or periodically when the model is deliberately
retrained on new data, and is computationally expensive. **Inference** happens every time the trained model
is used to produce an output for a new input, and is typically fast and cheap by comparison. This distinction
has a direct practical consequence: an organisation almost never trains a large model from scratch (it is
extremely resource‑intensive); instead it either trains small, purpose‑built models on its own structured
data (as in Example 13.1.2) or performs inference against a large, pre‑trained model built by someone else
(as with the GenAI/LLM assistants covered from 13.1.5 onward).

**Common misconceptions.**
- Assuming a deployed AI tool is "learning" from every use in real time by default. Most deployed models are

model is a snapshot of the patterns present at the point it was trained — it has no automatic awareness of a
change in the business, a new contract clause, or this week's site conditions unless it is retrained or given
that information at the point of inference (e.g. via retrieval‑augmented generation, 13.1.9).

### 13.1.4 Classical machine learning vs. deep learning: what changed, and why it matters for reliability

**Definition & purpose.** **Classical machine learning** covers statistical/algorithmic methods — linear and
logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting, support‑vector machines, k‑means
clustering — that work well on structured, tabular data with a moderate, well‑defined set of features: exactly
the kind of data a project‑controls function already holds in an ERP or cost ledger. **Deep learning (DL)**
is a subset of ML using artificial neural networks with many ("deep") layers of interconnected nodes, capable
of automatically learning relevant features from raw, unstructured data — images, audio, free text, video —
without a person hand‑engineering those features first. That automatic feature‑learning ability is deep
learning's central advantage over classical ML.

**The underlying principle.** The choice between them is task‑ and data‑dependent, not a matter of "newer is
always better." For the tabular, moderate‑sized, well‑understood datasets typical of project controls (cost
ledgers, schedules, control accounts — Example 13.1.2's 240 rows and roughly half a dozen features), classical
ML methods are often more accurate on that scale of data, faster to train, cheaper to run, and — critically in
a governed environment — easier to explain and audit than deep learning (cross‑reference 13.6). Deep learning
earns its complexity where the data is unstructured and available at scale: free‑text contract documents,
meeting transcripts, drawings and images, and — importantly — the large language models covered from 13.1.5
onward, which are built on a specific deep‑learning architecture (the Transformer, from 2017‑onward research)
trained at very large scale.

**Failure modes.** Classical ML struggles with unstructured data (raw text, images) without heavy manual
feature engineering, and can underfit genuinely complex, non‑linear patterns. Deep learning needs large
volumes of training data and significant computing power; is harder to explain — a specific prediction's
"why" is not directly readable from the model's parameters the way a regression coefficient is (the
"black‑box" problem, an active but not fully solved area of interpretability research as of 2026); and is
prone to learning spurious correlations present in the training data that do not hold up in the real world,
including well‑documented forms of bias amplification when trained on biased historical data (cross‑reference
13.2, 13.6).

**Worked illustration — fit‑for‑purpose reasoning.** Contrast two tasks Meridian's controls function might
reasonably face. Example 13.1.2's cost‑overrun classifier — tabular data, roughly half a dozen features, 240
rows — is deliberately a classical‑ML problem: a deep neural network would be unnecessary complexity here and
would likely **overfit** on so few rows, learning noise rather than signal. Contrast that with a genuinely
different task: automatically extracting the contract value, retention percentage and defects‑liability
period from scanned, free‑text subcontract PDFs. That is an unstructured‑document task, and deep‑learning‑based
document‑AI (cross‑reference KA 13.4) is the appropriate category — a classical regression model has no
natural way to "read" a scanned PDF's free text at all.

**Common misconceptions.**
- Treating "deep learning" and "AI" as synonyms.
- Assuming deep learning is always more accurate than classical ML — on small, structured datasets it
  frequently is not, and it is harder to audit.
- Assuming more layers or more parameters automatically produces a better model — performance depends on fit
  between the method, the task and the available data, not on raw model size.

**Capabilities & limits.** Deep learning has enabled genuine, current capability jumps in unstructured‑data
tasks — document extraction, transcription, image and drawing analysis, and the generative/language
capabilities covered from 13.1.5 — this is real, not hype. The "black‑box" explainability limitation is
equally real and, in general, unresolved as of 2026 (interpretability research is active and improving, not
finished). That is precisely why a high‑stakes project‑controls output built on deep learning — an EAC input,
a claim valuation, a payment‑certification figure — should be treated as an input to professional judgement,
never as a self‑justifying final answer (cross‑reference 13.6).

**Cross‑references.** 13.1.2 (the classical‑ML worked example); 13.1.5 (deep learning underpinning LLMs); 13.4
(document/RAG tools using deep learning); 13.6 (explainability and auditability governance).

### 13.1.5 Generative AI and large language models: producing new content, not just a label or a number

**Definition & purpose.** **Generative AI (GenAI)** is a category of AI models that produce **new content** —
text, images, audio, code — resembling the patterns in their training data, rather than predicting a fixed
label or number for a given input, as the classifiers of 13.1.2–13.1.4 do. **Large language models (LLMs)**
are the specific, currently dominant form of GenAI: models trained on vast quantities of text to model and
generate natural language, built on the Transformer deep‑learning architecture and trained via
**next‑token prediction** (13.1.6) at very large scale.

**The underlying principle.** An LLM is, fundamentally, a highly sophisticated pattern‑completion engine over
language — not a database of verified facts, not a calculator, and not a reasoning engine in the human sense.
Its scale nonetheless gives it genuinely useful capabilities that were never explicitly programmed into it:
drafting, summarising, rephrasing, translating, explaining and following complex natural‑language instructions
across an enormous range of topics, including project‑controls‑adjacent tasks such as commentary drafting
(cross‑reference KA 13.3).

**Contrast with a narrow classifier.** Example 13.1.2's classifier answers one fixed, narrow question ("will
this account breach?") with a probability and nothing else. An LLM assistant can be asked an open‑ended range
of questions and will attempt a fluent answer to nearly all of them — which is both its power and, as 13.1.7
shows, the root of its central risk. Asked in natural language about a control account's likely cost‑overrun
causes, an LLM can produce a full paragraph of plausible‑sounding reasoning, drawing on general patterns of
construction cost overruns present in its training text — genuinely useful as a first‑pass drafting aid, but
not because it consulted Meridian's actual ledger, unless it was specifically given that data (cross‑reference
13.1.9, KA 13.3).

**Failure modes distinct to GenAI/LLMs.** Hallucination (13.1.7); sensitivity to exactly how a question is
phrased ("prompt sensitivity," cross‑reference KA 13.3); inconsistency across repeated runs of the same
question (13.1.8); a fixed **knowledge cut‑off** — a model only "knows" what was present in its training data
up to a fixed date, and has no inherent awareness of anything after that, or of an organisation's private,
current records, unless specifically connected to them (cross‑reference 13.1.9); and a confidently fluent
**tone that carries no automatic correlation with factual correctness** — a critical governance point
developed fully in 13.1.7 and KA 13.6.

**Common misconceptions.**
- Assuming a general‑purpose assistant is always "searching the internet" or a database for its answer. By
  default, a base LLM generates from learned patterns; it is not searching anything live unless it has been
  specifically equipped with a retrieval or browsing tool (cross‑reference 13.1.9, KA 13.4).
- Assuming a bigger or newer model "won't hallucinate." Scale and technique genuinely reduce the *frequency*
  of hallucination; they do not eliminate the underlying mechanism (13.1.7).
- Treating "generative AI" as a single, uniform thing — the term spans multiple content types (text, image,
  audio, code); this KA, and this domain generally, focuses on text/LLMs as most relevant to project controls.

**Capabilities & limits.** LLMs are genuinely strong at language tasks: drafting, summarising, rephrasing,
structuring, explaining, translating, and, increasingly, tool‑assisted tasks such as retrieving an
organisation's own documents (13.1.9) or the coding/automation uses surveyed in KA 13.4. They are genuinely
weak and unreliable at stating specific facts about a private entity's own records without being explicitly
grounded in them, and at guaranteed‑correct arithmetic or multi‑step numeric reasoning without external tool
support (13.1.8, 13.1.10 develop this further). Representative tool **categories** — named honestly, without
asserting specific unverified features — include general‑purpose LLM assistants such as Claude, ChatGPT,
Gemini and Copilot‑class tools built on this kind of model; exact capabilities differ by vendor and change
frequently, and should be checked against current documentation rather than assumed from this text
(cross‑reference KA 13.4, KA 13.7).

**Cross‑references.** 13.1.4 (the deep‑learning/Transformer foundation); 13.1.6–13.1.8 (mechanics, hallucination,
determinism); KA 13.3 (prompting practice); KA 13.4 (tool categories); KA 13.6 (governance).

### 13.1.6 Inside an LLM: tokens, embeddings, context window and next‑token prediction

**Definition & purpose.** Four mechanical concepts explain how an LLM actually produces a response.

- **Token.** The basic unit of text an LLM processes — typically a word, a piece of a word, or a punctuation
  mark (e.g. "reconciliation" might be split into "recon" and "ciliation"), not always a whole word. Text is
  converted into a sequence of tokens before an LLM can process it, and a response is generated one token at a
  time.
- **Embedding.** A numerical representation — a vector of numbers — that captures a token's or a passage's
  meaning in a form a model can process mathematically, such that texts with related meaning end up
  numerically "close" together in this space. This is the mechanism underlying an LLM's apparent grasp of
  meaning, and it is also the basis of the retrieval step in retrieval‑augmented generation (13.1.9).
- **Context window.** The maximum amount of text, measured in tokens, an LLM can take into account at once —
  the prompt, the conversation history and any retrieved documents, together. Anything outside the context
  window is simply not seen by the model when it generates its response. Context windows vary by model and
  have grown substantially over successive generations of models, but remain finite in every case — a
  concrete, checkable limit, not a hypothetical one (the current size for any specific tool should be verified
  against that vendor's documentation at the time of use — cross‑reference KA 13.4, KA 13.7 — rather than
  assumed from this text, which deliberately does not quote a specific figure that would go stale).
- **Temperature.** A setting controlling how narrowly or how widely the model samples from its computed
  probability distribution over possible next tokens (below). A low temperature favours the highest‑probability
  token almost every time, producing more consistent, more conservative output; a higher temperature allows
  lower‑probability tokens to be chosen more often, producing more varied output that is also more prone to
  drift and error. Exact behaviour and default settings vary by vendor and product.

**The underlying principle — next‑token prediction.** An LLM generates text by repeatedly computing a
probability distribution over every candidate "next token," given everything so far (the prompt plus whatever
has already been generated, within the context window), then selecting one token according to that
distribution, appending it to the sequence, and repeating — one token at a time. The model does not compose a
whole answer in advance the way a person drafts an essay; it has no separate, pre‑formed answer it is
retrieving and reciting. This is the single most important mechanical fact in this Knowledge Area, because it
is the direct cause of hallucination (13.1.7).

**Common misconceptions.**
- Believing the model "looks up" an answer in a database or "remembers" a past conversation with the same
  rigour a database would. By default, a new conversation typically starts fresh unless a product has a
  specific, disclosed memory feature — that is a deliberate, named capability, not a universal default
  (13.1.8 returns to this).
- Assuming the context window is effectively unlimited. It is not; a professional feeding a large contract or
  a long cost‑ledger extract into an assistant needs to know whether it actually fits within that session's
  window, or risk the model silently working from an incomplete view of the document (cross‑reference 13.2,
  KA 13.3).
- Assuming temperature only affects "creativity" and is irrelevant to factual work. It affects factual and
  numeric reliability too — professional, factual tasks should generally default to the lowest available
  temperature/most deterministic setting a tool offers (cross‑reference KA 13.3).

**Capabilities & limits.** This mechanism is genuinely what allows LLMs to produce fluent, well‑structured,
contextually appropriate text across an enormous range of topics without being explicitly programmed for each
one — a real and current capability. It is equally, structurally, the reason an LLM cannot be trusted by
default to know or verify a specific private fact — that limit follows directly from the mechanism, not from
today's models being merely "not good enough yet" (13.1.7).

**Cross‑references.** 13.1.5 (LLMs generally); 13.1.7 (the hallucination mechanism this topic sets up); 13.1.9

### 13.1.7 Why LLMs hallucinate — and why it is a structural property, not a simple bug

**Definition & purpose.** A **hallucination** is an AI‑generated output that is fluent, confident and
plausible‑sounding, but factually incorrect, unsupported by any source material actually provided, or
entirely fabricated — an invented citation, clause number, statistic or fact presented with the same tone as
a correct one.

**The underlying principle.** Hallucination follows directly from 13.1.6's mechanism. Because an LLM generates
its next token by statistical plausibility learned from training patterns — not by retrieving and verifying a
specific fact — a request for a specific, private, or simply rare fact it was never reliably trained on (or
was not given at the point of answering) does not reliably produce "I don't know." Instead, the model tends
to produce the most statistically plausible‑sounding continuation available to it, which can be a fabrication
carrying the correct grammatical and structural shape of a genuine answer — a real‑looking clause number, a
real‑looking dollar figure, a real‑looking citation — without being tied to any actual source at all. This is
why hallucination is not simply a defect that better engineering will fully eliminate: it is an emergent
consequence of how a probabilistic, pattern‑completing generator works. It can be substantially **mitigated**
— grounding the model in real source documents (retrieval‑augmented generation, 13.1.9), using tools that can
verify facts rather than generate them, choosing a lower temperature, giving explicit instructions to cite a
source or say "I don't know," and, always, independent human verification — but it should never be assumed
**solved** (cross‑reference 13.1.9, KA 13.3, KA 13.6).

**Mini‑case 13.1.7 — a hallucinated retention clause.** An analyst, working in a general‑purpose LLM assistant
with **no connection to Meridian's actual contract documents**, asks: "What is the retention percentage and
the Month‑4 retention release amount under Pipeline Segment 4B's contract?" A plausible ungrounded response
might confidently state something like *"Under Clause 14.9 of the contract, retention is held at 10% of
certified value, with $24,000 released against the Month‑4 certificate"* — a fluent, specific‑sounding,
professionally worded answer. **This is an illustrative example of what a hallucination looks like — it is
not a real quotation from any actual standard, and not a real Meridian record.** Meridian's actual contract
terms are the entity's own private business record; they are not general public knowledge any LLM could have
been reliably trained on, so the only honest, reliable ungrounded answer is "I don't have access to that
document," not a specific invented clause number and figure.

*The mitigated version.* The same question, asked of an assistant actually given Segment 4B's real signed
contract text as grounding context (retrieval‑augmented generation, 13.1.9), can quote the genuine clause and
figure — and even then, the professional must check the quoted passage against the source document before
relying on it, because grounding reduces, but does not reduce to zero, the risk of a subtly misquoted or
misattributed passage.

**Common misconceptions.**
- Hallucination only happens with "bad" or "cheap" AI tools. It is a structural property of the generative
  mechanism itself, present in principle in every LLM, including the most capable ones; better models and
  grounding techniques reduce its *frequency*, not its *possibility*.
- A confident tone is evidence of accuracy. It is not — tone and correctness are generated somewhat
  independently by the same next‑token mechanism.
- Hallucinations are always obvious. The most dangerous ones are not the wildly implausible ones a reviewer
  catches instantly, but subtly wrong, plausible‑sounding details — a real‑looking but incorrect clause
  number, embedded in an otherwise‑accurate paragraph.

**Capabilities & limits.** This is the single most important governance fact in this Knowledge Area, stated
directly: no output from a general‑purpose LLM that asserts a specific fact about a private entity's own
records — a contract term, a specific historical cost, a specific decision — should ever be relied upon
without independent verification against the actual source document or system of record. This is not a
temporary limitation to be waited out; it is a standing verification obligation, consistent with "AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable" (cross‑reference 13.1.11, KA 13.6).

**Cross‑references.** 13.1.6 (the generation mechanism causing this); 13.1.9 (RAG as a mitigation, not a
cure); KA 13.2 (data quality and grounding); KA 13.3 (verification‑first prompting practice); KA 13.6 (the
assurance/verification checklist for AI outputs).

### 13.1.8 Determinism, probabilism and reproducibility: why the same question can get two different answers

**Definition & purpose.** A **deterministic** system always produces exactly the same output from exactly the
same input — a spreadsheet formula, a rules‑based validation check, and, in practice under fixed conditions, a
classical ML model's inference step (13.1.2–13.1.3), which applies fixed, already‑trained parameters to an
input. A **probabilistic** system can produce different outputs from the same input, by design, because it
samples from a probability distribution rather than always selecting one fixed answer — 13.1.6's next‑token
sampling, at any temperature above the strictest deterministic setting, is probabilistic by construction.

**The underlying principle.** Most GenAI/LLM tools, in their normal default configuration, are probabilistic:
asking the same question twice, even seconds apart, can produce two differently worded — and occasionally
substantively different — answers. This has direct, practical consequences for a project‑controls
professional. **Reproducibility** (can the same output be regenerated later to check it, or to show an
auditor how a figure was produced?) is not automatically guaranteed with GenAI the way it is with a
spreadsheet formula or a fixed classical‑ML inference. **Version drift** compounds this further: the
underlying model itself may be updated by its vendor over time, changing its behaviour on the same prompt
between one use and the next (cross‑reference KA 13.4, KA 13.7).

**Worked illustration — reproducibility side by side.**

| Property | Example 13.1.2 classifier (classical ML) | LLM assistant drafting a commentary |
|---|---|---|
| Same input twice → same output? | Yes — deterministic inference | Not guaranteed — probabilistic by default |
| Underlying mechanism | Fixed, already‑learned parameters applied to input | Token‑by‑token sampling from a probability distribution |
| Reproducibility for audit | High — re‑running later reproduces the same figure | Requires the actual output to be saved/logged at the time it was produced, since a later re‑run may not reproduce it exactly |

**Common misconceptions.**
- Assuming "AI" as a category is either always deterministic or always random. It depends on the specific
  type of model and its configuration, not on "AI" as a whole.
- Assuming a low or zero temperature setting makes an LLM's output guaranteed‑identical every time, in every
  product. Even at a low or "zero" temperature setting, some implementations retain minor sources of
  variability; treat "more consistent" as the safe claim, "guaranteed identical" as one to verify against the
  specific tool in use.
- Assuming this reproducibility gap means GenAI outputs cannot be used professionally at all. They can,
  provided the actual output relied upon is captured and logged at the time it informed a decision, exactly as
  any other piece of evidence would be (cross‑reference KA 13.6).

**Capabilities & limits.** This is a genuine, practical governance implication, not a theoretical curiosity.
Any AI‑generated output that informs a professional judgement — a drafted commentary, a suggested narrative
around an EAC — should be saved exactly as it was actually produced and reviewed, precisely because
regenerating it later for an audit trail may not reproduce the same words (cross‑reference KA 13.6's
auditability treatment).

**Cross‑references.** 13.1.6 (temperature and sampling, the mechanism behind this); 13.1.2–13.1.3 (deterministic
ML contrast); KA 13.6 (auditability and logging of AI outputs); KA 13.4 (tool and model‑version management).

### 13.1.9 Adapting and grounding a model: fine‑tuning and retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG)

**Definition & purpose.** Beyond initial training (13.1.3), two further, distinct techniques adapt an
already‑trained model to a specific need:

- **Fine‑tuning.** Taking an already‑trained (often large, general‑purpose) model and continuing to train it
  further on a smaller, more specific dataset, adjusting its parameters so it performs better on a narrower
  task or in a particular style or domain — for example, a model fine‑tuned on a contracting group's own
  historical variance commentaries to better match its house style and terminology. Fine‑tuning changes the
  model's underlying parameters, is comparatively resource‑intensive, and produces a new, distinct version of
  the model.
- **Retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG).** A technique that does **not** change the model's parameters at
  all. At the moment of answering a query, the system first retrieves relevant documents or passages from an
  approved knowledge source — using the embeddings/semantic‑search mechanism from 13.1.6 — and inserts them
  into the model's context window alongside the question, so the model generates its answer grounded in that
  specific retrieved content rather than solely from its general training patterns. RAG is the standard
  mechanism behind "chat with your documents" and enterprise knowledge‑assistant tools (cross‑reference KA
  13.4).

**The underlying principle.** These are not competing techniques but different tools for different problems:
fine‑tuning changes *how a model behaves or writes in general*; RAG changes *what specific facts a model has
available for a given answer*. RAG is the more direct, and generally more auditable, mitigation for the
hallucination risk described in 13.1.7 when the goal is "answer accurately about our own documents," because
the actual retrieved source passages can be shown alongside the answer for verification. Fine‑tuning does not,
by itself, solve hallucination, and can in some cases make outputs sound more confidently authoritative
without improving their factual grounding — a governance‑relevant nuance (cross‑reference KA 13.6).

**Worked illustration — grounding Mini‑case 13.1.7.** Suppose Meridian's controls function connects its
document/knowledge assistant to its actual repository of signed contracts, an approved, access‑controlled
corpus. When the analyst asks about Segment 4B's retention clause, the system retrieves the actual contract
passage and supplies it to the model as grounding context; the model's answer both states the figure and
shows the retrieved source passage for the analyst to check against the original document. This meaningfully
reduces — though, per 13.1.7, never eliminates to zero — the hallucination risk, and, crucially, it makes the
claim independently checkable, which an ungrounded answer is not.

**Common misconceptions.**
- RAG makes an answer 100% guaranteed accurate. It does not — the retrieval step itself can retrieve the wrong
  or an outdated passage, and the model can still misquote or misinterpret even a correctly retrieved passage;
  verification remains necessary.
- Fine‑tuning is how "the AI learns about our company automatically over time." It is a deliberate, resourced
  technical exercise undertaken by whoever operates the model, not a background process ordinary subscription
  use triggers.
- RAG content and a model's original training data are the same thing. They are not: RAG content is supplied
  fresh at query time and is never baked into the model's underlying parameters.

**Capabilities & limits.** RAG is a genuinely important, current, widely deployed, governance‑friendly pattern
for enterprise AI use. A project‑controls function evaluating an AI document or knowledge tool (KA 13.4)
should specifically ask whether, and how, it grounds answers in the organisation's own approved documents
rather than relying on the base model's general training alone, and whether it surfaces the retrieved source
for verification. Fine‑tuning is a more specialised, typically vendor‑ or platform‑level undertaking most
project‑controls functions will consume as a finished product, if at all, rather than perform themselves.

**Cross‑references.** 13.1.6 (embeddings, the retrieval mechanism); 13.1.7 (hallucination and its mitigation);
KA 13.2 (the organisation's own data as the RAG source, and its governance); KA 13.3 (prompting practice with
grounded tools); KA 13.4 (the RAG/document‑tool category); KA 13.6 (verification and auditability).

### 13.1.10 The capability boundary: what today's AI is genuinely good at, and genuinely unreliable at, in project controls

**Definition & purpose.** This topic draws 13.1.1–13.1.9 together into a practical, honest boundary a
professional can apply day to day — and delivers directly on the question every reader of this domain will
eventually ask: for a given project‑controls task, which category of tool, if any, actually fits?

**The underlying principle.** The right category for a task depends on three questions: (i) is the logic
fully known and stable enough to write as an explicit rule? (ii) is there enough good‑quality historical
labelled data to learn a narrow, bounded prediction? (iii) is the task fundamentally about generating or
transforming language or content, rather than computing a fixed answer? A fourth, and often decisive,
question sits above all three: is this actually a *decision*, with financial or contractual consequence, that
must remain a human, accountable act regardless of what analysis supports it?

**Mini‑case 13.1.10 — classifying Meridian's controls tasks.**

| Task | Best‑fit category | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Reject a purchase order that exceeds a named approver's delegated authority limit | Rules‑based automation | The logic is fully known, fixed and auditable — no learning is needed or appropriate |
| Flag control accounts at elevated risk of a cost‑variance breach, from historical patterns | Supervised ML (Example 13.1.2) | Enough historical labelled outcomes exist; the question is a bounded prediction, not open‑ended generation |
| Group control accounts into behavioural clusters to surface cost patterns no one has hypothesised yet | Unsupervised ML | No pre‑defined label — the point is discovering structure, not predicting a known outcome |
| Draft a first‑pass narrative variance commentary from this month's cost figures, for a reviewer to edit | Generative AI / LLM | The task is language generation from given data, not classification or prediction |
| Answer "what does our signed subcontract actually say about liquidated damages?" | GenAI **grounded** via RAG — never an ungrounded general assistant | Requires an accurate, source‑specific fact plus fluent presentation; grounding is mandatory (13.1.7, 13.1.9) |
| Decide whether to release contingency against an adverse VAC this month | **No AI** — professional judgement, informed by AI‑assisted analysis | A funding and accountability decision, not a prediction or drafting task — 13.1.11 |

*Interpretation.* The final row is deliberate: not every task on this list should be handed to any AI
category at all. The decision to commit funds, certify a payment, or sign a report is, and should remain, a
human act — however good the analysis that supported it (bridging directly into 13.1.11).

**Genuinely good at (stated honestly, as of 2026).** Summarising and structuring large volumes of text
quickly; drafting first‑pass narrative content from clearly given data; classifying or predicting from
abundant, clean, labelled historical structured data; finding patterns and outliers across large datasets
faster than manual review; answering general, non‑entity‑specific questions (e.g. "explain what a contract
asset is") reasonably reliably, because that kind of knowledge is genuinely common and well represented in
training data.

**Genuinely unreliable at (stated honestly, as of 2026).** Stating specific facts about an organisation's own
private records without being explicitly grounded in them (13.1.7); guaranteed‑correct arithmetic and
multi‑step numeric reasoning without external calculation or tool support — LLMs can and do make arithmetic
errors, particularly on larger or multi‑step calculations, precisely because they generate a plausible‑looking
answer token by token rather than executing a calculation the way a calculator does, so any AI‑assisted
arithmetic must be independently verified; fully autonomous, unsupervised decision‑making with financial or
contractual consequence; guaranteed‑reproducible outputs (13.1.8); and knowing anything about events after
its training cut‑off, or private and internal events at all, unless specifically told or connected to a live
source.

**Common misconceptions.**
- Assuming newer or larger models have "solved" these limits. Several have genuinely, measurably improved
  over time; none is eliminated as a matter of principle. Re‑verify current tool capability rather than
  assuming permanence (cross‑reference KA 13.7).
- Assuming a single AI tool covers the whole table above. In practice, different task categories typically
  need different, purpose‑fit tools (cross‑reference KA 13.4).
- Treating "AI can't do X yet" as a permanent statement. This is a fast‑moving field; the honest position is
  that this boundary should be expected to shift, and KA 13.7 addresses the outlook without hype.

**Capabilities & limits.** This topic *is* the concentrated capabilities‑and‑limits treatment for the whole
Knowledge Area; carry its lens — good at generation, prediction and pattern‑finding from good data; unreliable
at entity‑specific fact assertion, guaranteed arithmetic, autonomous decisions and guaranteed reproducibility
— forward into every subsequent Domain 13 Knowledge Area.

**Cross‑references.** 13.1.1 (rules vs. learning categories); 13.1.2 (the supervised classifier); 13.1.7
(hallucination as the core reliability limit for facts); KA 13.4 (tool categories in depth); KA 13.5
(lifecycle applications); KA 13.6 (governance); KA 13.7 (maturity and outlook).

### 13.1.11 The professional‑accountability frame: "AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable"

**Definition & purpose.** This topic states, at foundation level, the principle that governs every AI
reference in this book (full treatment: KA 13.6): whatever category of AI tool is used — rules engine,
classical ML, deep learning, or GenAI/LLM — its output is an **input to a professional's judgement**, never a
substitute for the named individual's accountability for the estimate, forecast, commitment, certification or
decision that follows.

**The underlying principle.** This is not a slogan; it is a specific allocation of responsibility that follows
directly from everything else in this Knowledge Area. Narrow AI systems have no general understanding of
context (13.1.1). Supervised models are bounded by their training data and by a threshold choice someone must
own (13.1.2–13.1.3). GenAI/LLMs generate plausible language, not verified fact, by mechanism (13.1.6–13.1.7).
Outputs may not be reproducible (13.1.8). Even grounded tools require verification (13.1.9). None of this
means AI assistance should be avoided — 13.1.10 was equally clear about genuine, current strengths — it means
every AI output that feeds a professional deliverable carries an explicit human verification and sign‑off
step, proportionate to the stakes of the decision it supports: a low‑stakes drafting aid needs lighter review
than a figure feeding a payment certification or a client‑facing forecast.

**Mini‑case 13.1.11 — from an AI flag to a signed‑off decision.** Example 13.1.2's classifier flags a Segment
4B control account at an 82% predicted probability of breach. The professional does **not** report "the AI
says this account will breach" to the project sponsor. Instead: (1) the flag triggers a prioritised manual
review of that account's underlying CPI, SPI, open variations and site status (KA 5.4, KA 6.2); (2) the
reviewing cost engineer either substantiates or discounts the flag against real evidence; (3) if substantiated,
a bottom‑up ETC re‑estimate and a chosen EAC method (KA 6.3) are prepared and signed off by the named
professional; (4) the AI flag and the human reviewer's conclusion are both retained in the account's file, so
the record shows what the model proposed and what the professional decided, and why (cross‑reference KA 13.6's
auditability treatment). The model's 82% never appears in the client report as if it were the forecast itself
— the reported figure is the professional's EAC, arrived at through the ordinary discipline of KA 6.3, with
the AI flag recorded as one input among several that triggered the review.

**Common misconceptions.**
- Assuming this principle means light‑touch, box‑ticking review is sufficient regardless of stakes. It is
  not — review depth should scale with the consequence of being wrong (cross‑reference KA 13.6).
- Assuming the principle is a temporary caution that will lapse once AI tools "get good enough." Accountability
  for professional certifications, estimates and commitments is a standing feature of the profession itself,
  not a technology gap that closes as models improve — even a hypothetically flawless prediction still
  requires a named, accountable decision‑maker in a regulated or commercial context.
- Assuming the principle only applies to GenAI. It applies to every category in this Knowledge Area, including
  rules‑based automation and classical ML: an automated rule can still be mis‑specified, and a classifier's
  flag threshold is still a policy choice someone must own.

**Capabilities & limits.** Restated as this Knowledge Area's closing synthesis rather than a new claim: the
professional's accountability is not diminished by any AI capability described in 13.1.1–13.1.10, however
genuinely useful — if anything it is sharpened, because competently verifying AI output is now part of the
professional's own skill set (cross‑reference KA 13.7's capability‑building treatment).

**Cross‑references.** KA 13.6 (full governance, ethics, risk and assurance treatment — this topic is its
foundation); KA 13.5 (lifecycle applications, each carrying this same frame); KA 13.7 (building organisational
capability to apply this frame at scale); KA 6.3 (the EAC judgement this mini‑case's account feeds); KA 4.3
(reporting to a sponsor).

### Key terms

- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** — the broad field of engineering systems to perform tasks that would
  require intelligence if a person did them; includes both rules‑based and machine‑learning approaches.
- **Machine learning (ML)** — AI systems that infer a mapping from data rather than being given explicit
  rules.
- **Deep learning (DL)** — a subset of ML using multi‑layer neural networks; learns features directly from
  raw, unstructured data.
- **Generative AI (GenAI)** — AI that produces new content (text, images, audio, code) resembling patterns in
  its training data, rather than a fixed label or number.
- **Large language model (LLM)** — the dominant current form of text‑generating GenAI, trained via
  next‑token prediction on large volumes of text.
- **Narrow AI** — an AI system bounded to the tasks it was designed or trained for; all AI in commercial use
  today, including LLMs.
- **Artificial general intelligence (AGI)** — a hypothetical system with human‑level, cross‑domain reasoning;
  does not exist as of 2026; timeline and feasibility are genuinely contested.
- **Supervised learning** — learning a mapping from labelled input–output examples to predict a label
  (classification) or number (regression) for new inputs.
- **Unsupervised learning** — learning structure (clusters, anomalies) from unlabelled data.
- **Reinforcement learning (RL)** — learning a policy of actions through trial‑and‑error reward/penalty
  feedback; limited direct production use in project controls, but the training method behind LLM
  instruction‑following (RLHF).
- **Training** — the process of adjusting a model's parameters from data.
- **Inference** — applying an already‑trained model to a new input to produce an output.
- **Parameters** — the internal, adjustable numeric values a model learns during training.
- **Features** — the input variables a model consumes.
- **Labels** — the known outcomes a supervised model is trained to predict.
- **Overfitting** — a model that has memorised its training data rather than learned a pattern that
  generalises to new data.
- **Token** — the basic unit (word, part‑word or punctuation) an LLM processes and generates.
- **Embedding** — a numerical vector representing a token's or passage's meaning, enabling semantic
  comparison and retrieval.
- **Context window** — the finite amount of text an LLM can consider at once.
- **Temperature** — a setting controlling how narrowly or widely an LLM samples its next‑token probability
  distribution.
- **Next‑token prediction** — the mechanism by which an LLM generates text, one statistically plausible token
  at a time.
- **Hallucination** — a fluent, confident, but factually incorrect or fabricated AI output.
- **Fine‑tuning** — continuing to train an already‑trained model on a smaller, specific dataset to change its
  general behaviour or style.
- **Retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG)** — supplying a model with retrieved source passages at query time
  to ground its answer, without changing its parameters.
- **Determinism / probabilism** — whether the same input reliably produces the same output (deterministic) or
  may not (probabilistic).
- **"AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable"** — the governing principle that AI output is an input to
  professional judgement, never a substitute for the accountable professional's decision.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 13.1.1 [topic 13.1.1, level: recall]**
Which statement about artificial general intelligence (AGI) is most accurate, consistent with this book's
treatment?
A. AGI already exists in current large language models
B. AGI is expected within the next twelve months, per expert consensus
C. **AGI does not exist as of this writing, and there is no expert consensus on if or when it might** ✔
D. AGI is a synonym for a large language model with a very large context window
*Rationale:* Every AI system in commercial use today, including LLMs, is narrow AI. AGI is a hypothetical
future capability with no expert consensus on timeline or feasibility. A confuses narrow AI with general
intelligence; B invents a false precision this book explicitly avoids; D confuses two unrelated concepts
(context window is a mechanical limit, not a measure of generality).

**MCQ 13.1.2 [topic 13.1.2, level: application — numerical]**
A supervised cost‑overrun classifier is evaluated on a 60‑account test set. Its confusion matrix shows 14
true positives, 4 false negatives, 6 false positives and 36 true negatives. What is the model's **precision**?
A. 83.3%
B. **70.0%** ✔
C. 77.8%
D. 60.0%
*Rationale:* `Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 14 / (14 + 6) = 14/20 = 70.0%`. A is the model's accuracy
`(TP+TN)/n = 50/60`; C is its recall `TP/(TP+FN) = 14/18`; D is not a value implied by this confusion matrix.

**MCQ 13.1.3 [topic 13.1.4, level: application]**
Meridian's controls function has 240 rows of structured, tabular historical control‑account data (CPI, SPI,
number of variations, etc.) and wants to predict cost‑overrun risk. Which is the more appropriate method, and
why?
A. Deep learning, because it is always more accurate than classical ML
B. **Classical ML (e.g. logistic regression or gradient boosting), because the data is structured, tabular
   and modest in size, and the method is easier to audit** ✔
C. Reinforcement learning, because the model needs to learn through trial and error
D. A large language model, because LLMs can process any kind of data
*Rationale:* Structured, tabular, moderate‑sized data is classical ML's strength; deep learning would add

**MCQ 13.1.5 [topic 13.1.7, level: analysis]**
An analyst receives a fluent, specific‑sounding answer from an ungrounded general‑purpose LLM assistant

A. Accept the answer, because the model's confident tone indicates it is checking a real source
B. Reject all future use of LLM assistants for any task, since this incident proves they cannot be trusted

### Self‑check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. In your own words, explain why next‑token prediction (13.1.6) makes hallucination (13.1.7) a structural
   property of LLMs rather than a bug that can simply be patched out.
2. Using Example 13.1.2's confusion matrix (TP = 14, FN = 4, FP = 6, TN = 36), calculate the model's **false
   positive rate** — the proportion of actual "no breach" accounts the model incorrectly flagged as at risk.
3. A colleague argues that retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) is strictly better than fine‑tuning and should
   always be used instead of it. Using 13.1.9, explain why this is not quite right — what different problem
   does each technique solve?
4. A colleague says: "Our new AI tool is deterministic because we set its temperature to zero, so we don't
   need to log its outputs for an audit trail." Using 13.1.8, explain what is wrong with this reasoning.

**Answers**

1. An LLM does not retrieve or verify facts; at every step it selects the statistically most plausible next
   token given its training patterns and the current context. When asked about something it was never
   reliably trained on, or was not given as grounding, it has no reliable internal signal to fall back to "I
   don't know" — it will instead generate the most plausible‑sounding continuation available, which can be a
   fluent fabrication. Because this is how the generation mechanism itself works, hallucination cannot be
   fully "patched out" without changing what the model is fundamentally doing (e.g. adding grounding via RAG,
   13.1.9) — it can be mitigated, never assumed solved.
2. `False positive rate = FP / (FP + TN) = 6 / (6 + 36) = 6/42 = 14.3%`. This means 14.3% of the accounts that
   were actually fine were nonetheless flagged by the model as at risk — the "false alarm" rate a reviewer
   experiences.
3. RAG changes *what facts* a model has available at the moment it answers a specific query, by retrieving and
   inserting real source content into its context window — it is the more direct, more auditable fix for
   "does this answer reflect our actual current documents?" Fine‑tuning changes *how the model behaves or
   writes in general* by further training it on a specific dataset — useful for style, terminology or domain
   fluency, but it does not, by itself, ground any individual answer in a specific, current, verifiable
   source, and can even make an ungrounded answer sound more confidently authoritative. The two solve
   different problems and are not simple substitutes for one another.
4. Setting temperature to zero (or its lowest setting) makes an LLM's output *more consistent*, but most
   implementations do not formally guarantee bit‑for‑bit identical output on every re‑run, and — separately —
   the underlying model itself can be updated by the vendor over time (version drift), changing its behaviour
   on the same prompt between one use and a later one. Reproducibility for audit purposes therefore cannot be
   assumed; the actual output that informed a decision should be captured and logged at the time it was
   produced, exactly as any other piece of evidence would be.
