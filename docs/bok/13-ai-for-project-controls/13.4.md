# Domain 13 — AI for Project Controls & Project Management: Concepts, Tools & Practice

## KA 13.4 — AI tool categories for project controls & PM

KA 13.1 explained what AI, machine learning and generative AI actually are; KA 13.2 established that a
tool is only as useful as the data feeding it; KA 13.3 taught how to work effectively and safely with a
general-purpose GenAI assistant through prompting. This KA turns from concepts and technique to the
**market**: the recognisable categories of AI-enabled tooling a project controls professional will
actually encounter, procure, or be handed by their organisation's IT function, and what each one can and
cannot honestly be expected to do. The approach throughout is **practical and vendor-neutral**. Each
category is treated the same way: what it does and why it exists; how it genuinely helps a project
controls professional; its real limits and the governance a professional must apply around it;
**representative types of tool**, illustrated with a few well-known product or vendor names as examples of
the category — never as an endorsement, and never with invented features, benchmarks or pricing attached
to them; a use case grounded in this book's running illustration, **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**'s
**Pipeline Segment 4B** contract (BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1,
familiar from Domains 3, 6, 7 and 12) and its prospective next phase, **Pipeline Segment 5A** (introduced in
KA 7.1); and the pitfalls a professional should watch for. The software market referenced here moves fast —
products are renamed, features are added and withdrawn, and vendors merge or disappear. What this KA
teaches — the **category**, the underlying job-to-be-done, and the governance judgement each category
demands — is built to outlast any specific product name in it, which is precisely why every topic closes
with the same honest caution: verify current capability directly before relying on it. This KA closes with
three cross-cutting decisions every professional eventually faces regardless of category — build versus
buy, integrating a tool with the organisation's data layer (KA 13.2), and a repeatable rubric for evaluating
and selecting a tool — worked through Meridian's own tool-selection decision. KA 13.5 then shows these same
categories in action across the full project-controls lifecycle; KA 13.6 covers the governance, ethics and
assurance framework that must sit around every category named here; KA 13.7 addresses building the
capability to use them well over time.

### 13.4.1 Orientation: mapping the AI tool landscape for project controls

**Definition & purpose.** Before examining categories one at a time, it helps to place them on a common
map. Two questions distinguish one category of AI tool from another more usefully than any list of vendor
names: **how general or specific is it to project controls** (a chat assistant that can discuss anything,
versus a tool purpose-built to read a Bill of Quantities), and **how much of the task does it take on
before a human must intervene** (a tool that only assists a step the professional still performs, versus
one that completes a multi-step task and presents a finished output for review). Neither axis says
anything about quality — a highly specific tool can still be immature, and a highly general assistant can
still be extremely capable at a narrow task if prompted well (KA 13.3).

**The underlying principle.** Every category in this KA sits somewhere on both axes, and where it sits
predicts, fairly reliably, what governance a professional needs to apply. Tools that are general-purpose
and low-autonomy (a chat assistant drafting a paragraph a human reviews) carry mainly a *content-accuracy*
risk — check what it says before it goes out. Tools that are project-controls-specific and higher-autonomy
(a scheduling assistant that resequences a network, or an automation bot that posts a reconciled figure
into a report without a human touching it first) carry a compounded risk: a wrong output can enter the
record before anyone reviews it, and a domain-specific tool's errors are often less obviously wrong to a
non-expert reader than a generic chatbot's are. This is why KA 13.6's governance framework applies with
more force, not less, as a tool moves toward the specific/high-autonomy corner of the map — the opposite of
the instinct that "a specialised tool built for this exact job must need less oversight."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Judging a tool's trustworthiness by how project-controls-specific its marketing sounds, rather than by

consolidation are routine, and a category's typical maturity level can shift materially within a year.
Nothing in this KA should be read as a permanent ranking — treat every category's positioning as a starting
orientation to be re-checked against the live market when a real procurement decision is made.

### 13.4.2 General-purpose GenAI assistants

**Definition & purpose.** A general-purpose generative AI (GenAI) assistant is a conversational,
large-language-model-based tool — accessed through a chat interface, an API, or increasingly embedded
directly into office and productivity software — capable of drafting, summarising, explaining, translating
and reasoning over natural-language and, increasingly, tabular or document input, across essentially any
subject matter the user brings to it. It is "general-purpose" precisely because it was not built only for
project controls; its usefulness to a project controls professional comes entirely from how it is directed
(KA 13.3's prompting technique) and what it is given to work with.

**How it helps.** For a project controls professional, the single most common genuine use is *language
work*: drafting a first-pass variance commentary from a set of figures the professional supplies, tightening
a stakeholder email, summarising a long meeting transcript or a lengthy contract clause into plain language,
explaining an unfamiliar standard or technique, or acting as a fast, always-available thinking partner for
structuring an analysis before the professional does the analysis itself. Because these assistants reason
over whatever is placed in the prompt window, they are also useful for quick, bounded numerical or logical
checks — recomputing a formula, spotting an inconsistency in a small data extract pasted in — though this is
a secondary use, not their core strength (13.4.6 and 13.4.11 cover tools purpose-built for that).

**Limits & governance.** A general-purpose assistant has no independent connection to Meridian's live cost
ledger, schedule or contract register unless one is deliberately and securely built (13.4.14) — left on its
own, it only knows what is typed or pasted into it, and it will produce a fluent, confident-sounding answer
whether or not that input was complete, current or correct. It can also **hallucinate**: generate plausible
but false information, including invented figures, invented clause references, or an invented citation —
a risk KA 13.1 introduces and KA 13.6 covers in full. Most seriously for a commercial environment, pasting
confidential cost, contract or client data into a *consumer-grade, ungoverned* instance of such a tool can
constitute a data-confidentiality and, depending on the contract, a data-residency or IP breach; an
enterprise-governed, access-controlled deployment (with a clear data-handling and retention policy) is a
different and much safer proposition, and the distinction between the two must never be assumed by a user
who has not confirmed it.

**Representative tool types.** The category includes consumer- and enterprise-tier conversational
assistants such as Claude (Anthropic), ChatGPT (OpenAI), Gemini (Google) and Microsoft Copilot, along with
the growing number of office-suite integrations that embed the same underlying model family directly into
word processors, spreadsheets and email. Enterprise deployments of the same underlying assistants typically
add access controls, audit logging and contractual data-handling commitments not present in a free
consumer tier — a distinction covered further in KA 13.6.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** Meridian's project controls lead uses an enterprise-governed
GenAI assistant to draft the narrative section of the Pipeline Segment 4B Month-5 report, supplying it with
the already-computed EVM figures (BAC $600,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250; CPI 0.80; SPI 0.75 — Domain 6)
and a short instruction on tone and audience. The draft is fluent and structurally sound within seconds; the
lead then checks every figure against the source EVM table, corrects a sentence that overstated the
schedule position, and issues the edited version under her own name — exactly the workflow KA 6.3's
"multi-scenario" cross-reference and KA 13.3's worked prompting example both anticipate.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a fluent draft as a verified one — fluency and correctness are independent properties of a
  GenAI output, and a wrong number can read exactly as confidently as a right one.
- Pasting unredacted commercial, cost or personal data into a consumer-grade, ungoverned instance without
  first confirming the organisation's policy permits it.
- Using the assistant for a task it is structurally weak at (precise multi-step arithmetic on a large data
  set, or acting as a system of record) instead of the tasks it is genuinely strong at (drafting,
  summarising, explaining, structuring).

**Honest AI note.** General-purpose GenAI assistants are genuinely capable, fast-improving tools for
language and reasoning tasks, and their capability has grown substantially in a short period. They are not
or, at least, not yet in dependable general form — calculators, databases, or systems of record; they do
not "know" Meridian's data unless it is given to them, and every factual or numerical claim they produce
about a real project must be checked against the source before it is relied upon or repeated.

**Cross-references.** KA 13.1 (what a large language model is and how it generates output); KA 13.3
(prompting technique, in full); KA 13.6 (confidentiality, hallucination and governance in depth); 13.4.12
(the same underlying assistants increasingly embedded inside PM/ERP tools rather than used stand-alone).

### 13.4.3 Document intelligence, OCR & contract-analytics tools

**Definition & purpose.** This category converts unstructured or semi-structured **documents** — scanned
paper, PDFs, photographs of a site sign-off sheet, a signed contract — into structured, usable data.
**Optical Character Recognition (OCR)** is the foundational technology: recognising printed or handwritten
characters in an image and converting them to machine-readable text. **Intelligent Document Processing
(IDP)** builds on OCR with machine learning to also classify the document type and extract specific fields
(a date, a party name, an amount) into a structured record. **Contract-analytics** and **Contract Lifecycle
Management (CLM)** tools apply the same extraction approach specifically to contracts and their
amendments — identifying clauses, obligations, key dates and defined terms across a contract portfolio.

**How it helps.** Manually re-keying data from paper invoices, delivery tickets, signed variation orders or
a lengthy contract into a project's cost or commercial systems is slow and error-prone. IDP and
contract-analytics tools can extract that data automatically at far greater speed than manual entry,
flag key dates (a notice deadline, a defects-liability expiry) across a large contract portfolio that no
individual could realistically track by memory, and support a first-pass comparison between two versions of
a document (a redline) far faster than a manual read-through.

**Limits & governance.** Extraction accuracy depends heavily on document quality (a poor scan, an
unusual layout, dense handwriting) and on how well the underlying model has been trained or configured for
the specific document type; a materially wrong extracted figure or date is possible and, unlike a human
transcription error, may not look obviously wrong once it lands in a downstream system. These tools also
do not *interpret* a clause's commercial or legal meaning the way a contracts professional does — they
locate and extract text, they do not exercise judgement on entitlement, enforceability or risk allocation
(cross-reference KA 7.1, KA 7.2). Extracted output from a contract, invoice or correspondence document
should be spot-checked against the source, especially where it will feed a financial figure or a
contractual deadline.

**Representative tool types.** General-purpose IDP/OCR platforms (offered by major cloud providers and
specialist document-AI vendors) handle broad document extraction tasks; dedicated CLM and
contract-analytics platforms (a category that includes vendors such as Icertis, DocuSign (including its
CLM offering), Ironclad, Evisort and several construction-sector-specific contract-analytics tools) focus
specifically on the contract lifecycle — repository, clause extraction, obligation tracking and renewal/
key-date alerting.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** Ahead of finalising the commercial structure for Pipeline
Segment 5A (KA 7.1), Meridian's commercial team runs the client's 40-page draft contract through a
contract-analytics tool, which extracts a structured summary of key dates, payment terms, liquidated-
damages provisions and notice requirements within minutes — work that would otherwise take a contracts
professional the better part of a day to compile manually. The commercial lead then reviews the extracted
summary against the source clauses before it is relied on in negotiation, catching one instance where the
tool mis-classified a "sectional completion" clause as a standard practical-completion clause — a
distinction with real commercial consequence that only a professional read of the actual clause resolves.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Relying on an extracted field (a date, an amount, a party name) without spot-checking it against the
  source document, particularly for a document with unusual formatting.
- Assuming clause *extraction* is the same as clause *interpretation* — a tool can correctly locate a
  liquidated-damages clause and still not tell the professional whether it is enforceable or favourable.
- Feeding a contract-analytics tool documents outside the type or language it was configured for and
  trusting the output at the same confidence level as documents inside its trained scope.

**Honest AI note.** IDP and contract-analytics tools have matured into genuinely reliable extraction aids
for well-structured, machine-readable documents, and materially speed up first-pass review of a large
document set or portfolio. They remain extraction and pattern-matching tools, not legal or commercial
judgement — every extracted figure that will inform a decision, and certainly every extracted obligation
or deadline that will be relied upon contractually, is checked against the source document by the
accountable professional before use.

**Cross-references.** KA 7.1, 7.2 (contract types and administration — the substance these tools extract
from); KA 1.5 (cost coding, a natural downstream destination for extracted invoice data); 13.4.4 (NLP for
correspondence, the related but distinct category covering unstructured communications rather than formal
documents); KA 13.6 (verification governance for AI-extracted data used in a decision).

### 13.4.4 NLP for correspondence & claims analytics

**Definition & purpose.** Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the broader field of techniques for
analysing and deriving meaning from text; this category applies NLP specifically to the high volume,
free-form **correspondence** a project generates — emails, site diaries, meeting minutes, letters and
formal notices — to surface patterns relevant to claims, disputes and delay entitlement that a manual
reading of the full correspondence trail would take far longer to find. It is distinct from 13.4.3's
document-intelligence category: that category extracts structured fields from a defined document (a
contract, an invoice); this one mines large volumes of *unstructured, conversational* text for signal —
sentiment shifts, recurring themes, entitlement-relevant language, or a specific correspondence thread that
supports or undermines a claim.

**How it helps.** On a project generating thousands of emails and daily reports, a claims or commercial team
manually searching for every mention relevant to a specific delay event faces a genuine needle-in-a-haystack
problem. NLP tools can search and cluster correspondence by theme far faster than manual review, flag
language patterns associated with a forming dispute (repeated reservation-of-rights language, an escalating
tone, a recurring unresolved issue), and assemble a first-pass, time-stamped chronology of a delay event
from scattered source documents — a task that materially speeds up the early stages of building or
defending a claim.

**Limits & governance.** These tools identify *patterns and candidate material* in text; they do not
determine entitlement, causation or the legal merit of a claim, which remains an assessment made by a
suitably qualified commercial or legal professional against the contract terms (KA 7.1, KA 7.2). A
theme-clustering or sentiment-flagging tool can also miss context a human reader would catch immediately
(sarcasm, an internal joke misread as a genuine complaint, a a quoted excerpt from someone else's email
misattributed to its sender) — a lower-consequence but real accuracy risk. Because correspondence often
contains commercially and legally sensitive material, the same confidentiality and data-handling governance
that applies to any AI tool processing project data (13.4.2, KA 13.6) applies here with particular force,
given how often correspondence includes personal data about named individuals.

**Representative tool types.** The category is narrower and more specialised than general document
intelligence, spanning purpose-built construction claims-analytics platforms, e-discovery/legal-review
software adapted to a construction dispute context, and NLP-based correspondence-search features
increasingly built into project-management and document-control platforms. It is a smaller, faster-changing
niche than the mainstream categories in this KA, and product maturity varies widely.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** KA 12.2.9's mini-case recorded that R6 (permit/right-of-way
delay) was promoted from the qualitative watchlist to the quantified risk register after the permitting
authority confirmed a six-week backlog. That confirmation first surfaced because an NLP-based correspondence
tool, configured to flag delay-related language in Meridian's inbound correspondence with the permitting
authority, tagged three consecutive emails using increasingly firm "unable to confirm a turnaround date"
language weeks before the formal confirmation letter arrived — giving Meridian's risk owner an earlier
prompt to raise the issue for review than the monthly correspondence read-through would otherwise have
produced. The tool did not decide that R6 should be re-scored; it surfaced the signal that led the risk
owner to make that decision.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating an NLP-flagged pattern as proof of entitlement or fault, rather than as a lead requiring
  professional review against the facts and the contract.
- Over-tuning the tool to flag so much correspondence as "relevant" that the genuine early-warning signal is
  buried in noise — the opposite failure of not searching at all.
- Applying the tool to correspondence containing personal data about named individuals without the data-
  handling and privacy safeguards KA 13.6 requires.

**Honest AI note.** NLP correspondence tools are a genuine speed advantage for surfacing candidate material
in a large, unstructured communication trail, and can catch an early signal a periodic manual review would
otherwise only find later — as in Meridian's R6 example above. They cannot themselves establish contractual
entitlement, causation or fault; every flagged item that will support a claim, a risk re-score or a dispute
position is reviewed and interpreted by the accountable commercial or legal professional before being
relied upon, consistent with **"AI proposes; the professional disposes."**

**Cross-references.** KA 12.2.2, 12.2.9 (risk identification and monitoring — the R6 example developed
further here); KA 7.2 (claims and notices under the contract); 13.4.3 (structured document extraction, the
related but distinct category); KA 13.6 (confidentiality and personal-data governance).

### 13.4.5 Schedule-analytics & AI scheduling assistants

**Definition & purpose.** This category covers tools that assist with building, checking, optimising or
forecasting a project **schedule** — the network of activities, dependencies and durations developed in
Domain 10 — using AI/ML techniques layered on top of, or alongside, traditional Critical Path Method (CPM)
scheduling software.

**How it helps.** Three genuinely useful applications recur across current tooling. First, **logic
checking**: automatically scanning a large schedule for structural problems a human reviewer might miss in
thousands of activities — open ends (activities with no successor), missing predecessor links, illogical
date constraints, or an unusually high concentration of hard constraints that could be masking real logic
gaps (cross-reference KA 10.1, KA 10.2). Second, **scenario and sequencing assistance**: some newer
AI-native scheduling tools can generate and compare multiple feasible sequencing options against a defined
objective (shortest duration, resource-levelled, lowest cost) far faster than iterating manually. Third,
**delay prediction**: models trained on a schedule's historical update pattern (actual progress versus
planned, recurring slippage on similar activity types) can flag activities statistically likely to slip
before they formally do, giving an earlier prompt for attention than waiting for the next scheduled update.

**Limits & governance.** None of these tools can verify that the schedule's underlying logic reflects how
the work will genuinely be executed — that remains a planning judgement requiring construction-sequencing
knowledge a model does not have (cross-reference KA 10.1's treatment of activity sequencing). A logic-check
tool finds structural problems in the network as built; it cannot confirm the network represents reality. A
delay-prediction model is only as good as the historical data it was trained or calibrated on, and a model
trained on a different project type, region or contractor mix can transfer poorly to Meridian's specific
context — its output is a prompt to investigate, not a verified forecast (cross-reference KA 6.3's identical
caution about EAC methods). Scenario-generation tools optimise against whatever objective they are given;
an objective that omits a real-world constraint (a subcontractor's actual crew availability, a permitting
sequence) can produce a mathematically optimal but practically unworkable sequence.

**Representative tool types.** The dominant scheduling platforms of record — Oracle Primavera P6 and
Microsoft Project — are progressively adding AI-assisted features (logic-check assistants, natural-language
schedule queries) to their established CPM engines. A smaller set of AI-native scheduling-optimisation
tools (a category that includes vendors such as ALICE Technologies) apply generative/combinatorial
optimisation to construction sequencing specifically, generating and ranking multiple buildable schedule
options against a stated objective.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** Ahead of baselining Pipeline Segment 5A's schedule, Meridian's
planner runs an AI-assisted logic check over the draft 340-activity network. The tool flags two open-ended
activities (no defined successor) buried deep in the earthworks sub-network and a suspiciously high number
of "start-no-earlier-than" date constraints clustered around the permitting-dependent activities identified
as R6 in the Segment 4B risk register (KA 12.2) — a pattern the tool has learned is often used to paper over
a missing logic link rather than a genuine external constraint. The planner investigates both flags: the
open ends were a genuine oversight, corrected before baselining; the date constraints, on inspection, do
correctly represent real permitting dependencies and are left as constraints, not logic. The schedule is
baselined only after this human review — the logic-check tool shortened the review, it did not replace it.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Baselining a schedule on the strength of "the AI logic check passed" alone, without a human sequencing
  review — a structurally clean network can still be built on wrong assumptions about how work will proceed.
- Treating a delay-prediction flag as a confirmed forecast rather than a prompt to check the specific
  activity against current site knowledge.
- Adopting an AI-generated "optimal" sequence without confirming it against constraints the model was never
  given (labour availability, permitting sequence, safety segregation requirements).

**Honest AI note.** AI-assisted logic-checking is a mature, genuinely time-saving capability for large
schedules; scenario-generation and delay-prediction tooling is younger and its reliability varies more by
vendor and by how well-matched the training data is to the project at hand. In every case, the schedule
itself — its logic, its constraints, its baseline — remains the planner's professional output, reviewed and
owned by a named individual, not an artefact the tool produces unattended.

**Cross-references.** KA 10.1, 10.2 (schedule development and CPM, the logic these tools check); KA 10.3
(compression and schedule risk); KA 12.2 (R6, the permitting risk flagged in the use case); 13.4.7 (Monte
Carlo schedule risk, a related but distinct quantitative technique).

### 13.4.6 Cost, estimating & forecasting analytics

**Definition & purpose.** This category covers AI/ML-assisted tools that support **cost estimating**
(building a cost forecast for scope not yet executed, cross-reference KA 3.2) and **cost forecasting**
(projecting the ultimate cost of scope already under way, cross-reference KA 6.3's EAC family) using
historical data, pattern-matching and statistical or machine-learning models, rather than — or alongside —
purely manual, deterministic methods.

**How it helps.** For estimating, **parametric/ML estimating tools** can suggest a cost or productivity
rate for a new scope item by pattern-matching against a database of historical, comparable projects far
faster than a manual analogous-estimating exercise, and can help build a first-pass Class 4/5 estimate
(cross-reference KA 3.2's AACE estimate classes) for early-stage scoping. For forecasting, ML-based models
can supplement the classical EAC formulae (KA 6.3) by learning from **leading indicators** that a single
period's CPI/SPI reading does not capture on its own — for example, a pattern of extending procurement lead
times, a rising rate of RFIs on a specific work package, or a widening gap between committed and actual
cost — potentially flagging a cost-growth risk earlier than a lagging CPI trend alone would surface it.

**Limits & governance.** An ML-based estimate or forecast is only as reliable as the historical data it was
trained on; applied to a project type, region, or scale the training data does not represent well, it can
produce a confident-looking figure with poor real accuracy — the "garbage in, garbage out" principle KA
13.2 establishes in general terms applies with particular force to a numerical forecast a sponsor may act
on directly. These tools also do not know *why* a pattern is emerging (a genuinely deteriorating trend,
versus a one-off timing effect that will reverse next period) — that diagnosis remains the professional's,
exactly as KA 6.3's and KA 6.1.6's cautions about a bare EAC or an unexplained AI-drafted narrative already
establish for the classical methods this category augments, not replaces.

**Representative tool types.** The category spans parametric/analogous estimating software with growing
ML-assisted rate-suggestion features, cost-database and benchmarking platforms that apply pattern-matching
across a portfolio of historical projects, and predictive-EAC features increasingly appearing inside
broader cost-management and ERP platforms (13.4.12). It remains a fragmented market compared with
scheduling or BI tooling, without a single dominant platform equivalent to Primavera P6 in scheduling.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** At the Month-5 data date on Pipeline Segment 4B (BAC
$600,000; CPI 0.80; SPI 0.75 — KA 6.3), a leading-indicator ML model Meridian's controls team piloted
earlier that quarter had already flagged WP2 (pipe & valve procurement) as elevated cost-growth risk back
at Month 3 — two months before the classical CPI trend made the shortfall clearly visible — based on a
pattern of extending supplier lead-time confirmations relative to Meridian's historical procurement
baseline. The controls team investigated the Month-3 flag, found the supplier's lead-time slippage was real
but, at that point, still within contractual tolerance, and logged it as a watch item rather than an
immediate escalation. By Month 5, the same underlying pressure had materialised into the CPI/SPI shortfall
formally reported in KA 6.3 — the model's early flag bought two months of lead time that a purely
lagging-indicator approach would not have provided, though it did not, on its own, tell the team how
serious the eventual impact would be.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Adopting an ML-suggested rate or forecast without benchmarking it against the team's own recent, directly
  comparable cost experience — a model's historical database is never a perfect match to the current
  project.
- Reporting an ML-based forecast alongside, but not reconciled with, the classical EAC figure (KA 6.3),
  leaving a sponsor unsure which number to act on.
- Treating an early leading-indicator flag as a confirmed cost overrun rather than a prompt to investigate,
  as Meridian's team correctly did with the Month-3 WP2 signal above.

**Honest AI note.** Leading-indicator and parametric ML models can genuinely surface a forming cost trend
earlier than lagging classical measures alone, as in the WP2 example — a real and useful capability, not
hype. They are probabilistic pattern-matches against historical data, not certainties, and their accuracy
depends heavily on how representative that historical data is of the current project; every model output
that will influence a funding or escalation decision is investigated and confirmed against current project
facts before it is acted on.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2 (cost estimation and AACE classes); KA 6.3 (the classical EAC family this
category supplements); KA 13.5 (the fuller applied workflow for AI-assisted forecasting across the
lifecycle); KA 13.2 (data quality as the precondition for any model's reliability).

### 13.4.7 Risk analytics & Monte Carlo tools

**Definition & purpose.** This category covers the specialist software used to run **quantitative risk
analysis** — expected monetary value calculations, decision trees, and, most distinctively, **Monte Carlo
simulation** of cost and schedule outcomes — the techniques developed in full in KA 12.2.4–12.2.6, together
with the growing set of AI/ML features vendors are adding around that established simulation core.

**How it helps.** Dedicated risk-analytics software automates what would otherwise be an extremely tedious
manual exercise: running thousands of simulation iterations, propagating sampled durations through a full
schedule network's logic (cross-reference KA 10.2), and producing the cumulative probability distributions
(P10/P50/P80/P90, KA 12.2.6) that a manual spreadsheet-based approach could not feasibly replicate at scale.
The AI/ML features increasingly layered on top of this simulation core add two further capabilities: 
suggesting a plausible three-point (optimistic/most-likely/pessimistic) range for a cost or duration
element by pattern-matching against a library of historical risk registers, and detecting likely
**correlations** between risk-affected elements — for example, recognising that two work packages exposed
to the same ground conditions should not be modelled as statistically independent, a modelling error KA
12.2.6 specifically warns against.

**Limits & governance.** As KA 12.2.6 established in full, the simulation's arithmetic is only as
trustworthy as the three-point ranges and correlation assumptions feeding it — an AI-suggested range or
detected correlation is a starting point requiring the assessing professional's review, not a validated
input. Faster, AI-accelerated simulation does not change where accountability sits: the funding percentile
chosen from the output (P50, P80, or another policy level) remains a professional and sponsor-level
decision, never an output the software selects on its own.

**Representative tool types.** Established quantitative schedule- and cost-risk simulation platforms —
Oracle Primavera Risk Analysis, Palisade's @RISK (an Excel add-in), and Safran Risk are widely used examples
— provide the Monte Carlo engine; several are progressively adding the AI-assisted range-suggestion and
correlation-detection features described above. General-purpose statistical/programming environments
(Python and R, with open-source simulation libraries) remain a common alternative for teams building
bespoke risk models rather than using packaged software.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** This category is the tooling behind the Pipeline Segment 4B
Monte Carlo simulation already worked in full in KA 12.2.6 — the 10,000-iteration run producing a P50 of
$600,000 and a P80 of $645,000 from the base estimate, the three-point ranges (including WP3's $180,000/
$200,000/$260,000 range) and the register's quantified risks (R1–R4). That worked example's cautions —
report the percentile and the policy behind choosing it, never a bare number; do not treat simulation
inputs as more reliable than the judgement behind them — are this category's governance rules in full and
are not repeated here; see KA 12.2.6 directly.

**Common pitfalls.** See KA 12.2.6 in full: reporting a P50 without its confidence level; building
three-point ranges only for the largest risks and ignoring smaller, numerous uncertainties; treating
correlated risks as independent.

**Honest AI note.** Monte Carlo simulation is itself a long-established, purely computational technique,
not generative AI; the genuine AI/ML advance in this category is in range-suggestion and correlation
detection layered around that established core, and in running larger simulations faster. Neither advance
removes the need for the professional judgement KA 12.2.6 sets out for every input and every funding
decision built on the output.

**Cross-references.** KA 12.2.4–12.2.6 (the full quantitative risk-analysis treatment this category
executes); KA 12.1 (risk appetite, the basis for choosing a funding percentile); 13.4.6 (cost forecasting,
a related but distinct category using different techniques for a related purpose); KA 13.6 (governance of
AI-assisted risk decisions).

### 13.4.8 Data platforms, BI & AI-assisted dashboarding

**Definition & purpose.** Business Intelligence (BI) and data-visualisation platforms connect to an
organisation's underlying data sources (the ERP, the cost ledger, the schedule, the risk register — KA
13.2) and present them as dashboards, reports and charts. This category covers those platforms together
with the AI features increasingly built into them: natural-language querying of the underlying data,
auto-generated visualisations, and AI-drafted narrative summaries of what a chart or table shows.

**How it helps.** A well-built BI dashboard already does the core job of KA 4.3's management-reporting and
KA 4.4's visualisation principles: turning raw data into a form a stakeholder can read and act on quickly.
The AI layer adds two further capabilities. **Natural-language query** lets a user ask a question in plain
English ("what's driving the cost variance on WP3 this month?") and receive a generated chart or
table without needing to build the query manually — useful for ad-hoc investigation by someone who is not
a report-building specialist. **Auto-generated narrative** drafts a short written summary of what a
dashboard shows, in the same spirit as KA 6.1.6's mini-case about AI-drafted EVM commentary — a genuine
time-saver for a routine reporting cycle, subject to the same verification requirement that example
established.

**Limits & governance.** A natural-language query is only as good as its interpretation of the question
and the underlying data model beneath it — an ambiguously phrased question, or a data model with a known
gap or quality issue (KA 13.2), can produce a fluent, wrong answer that looks exactly as credible as a
right one. An auto-generated narrative describes *what* the data shows, not *why* — it cannot know that a
cost spike was a one-off invoice-timing effect rather than a genuine trend unless that context is
separately fed to it, echoing exactly KA 6.1.6's caution about narrative-drafting tools generally.

**Representative tool types.** The dominant enterprise BI platforms — Microsoft Power BI, Tableau (Salesforce)
and Qlik among them — have each introduced Copilot-style natural-language query and auto-narrative
features into their core products in recent years; the specific feature set and branding of these AI
additions continues to evolve rapidly across all three and should be checked directly with the vendor
before being relied on for a procurement decision. AI-assisted analysis features built into spreadsheet
software (for example, natural-language formula and analysis assistance in Excel and Google Sheets) sit in
the same broad category at a lighter weight, for teams working primarily in spreadsheets rather than a
dedicated BI platform.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** Meridian's programme office has connected a BI dashboard
directly to the cost ledger and schedule feeds behind Pipeline Segment 4B and 5A (13.4.14 covers how). At
the Month-5 review, a programme director unfamiliar with the underlying EVM detail asks the dashboard's
natural-language query feature "why did Segment 4B's cost performance worsen since Month 3?" and receives
an auto-generated chart showing the CPI trend alongside a drafted narrative attributing the shift to WP2 and
WP3 cost growth. The project controls lead, reviewing the same output before it circulates further, confirms
the CPI trend is correctly read but tightens the narrative's causal claim — the auto-drafted text implied
WP2's cost growth was the larger driver, when the underlying ledger data (KA 6.1.3) actually shows WP3 as
the larger contributor — a correction the lead could only make by checking the AI-drafted narrative against
the source figures, not by trusting the dashboard's fluent output at face value.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Circulating an AI-generated narrative or chart without the same source-data check KA 6.1.6 and KA 4.4
  already require of any management-reporting output.
- Allowing an ungoverned natural-language query feature to run against a live, sensitive data connection
  without the access controls appropriate to who can see what (cross-reference KA 13.6).
- Mistaking a well-designed AI-generated chart for evidence that the underlying data itself is
  clean and reconciled — the tool visualises what it is given; it does not audit it (KA 13.2).

**Honest AI note.** Natural-language query and auto-narrative features are a genuine convenience for
non-specialist users and for speeding up routine reporting, and are now widely available across the major
BI platforms. They inherit every weakness of the underlying data connection and, like any AI-drafted text,
can state a plausible but wrong causal story; the professional preparing or approving a report remains
responsible for what it says, exactly as for a manually written one.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.3, 4.4 (management reporting and visualisation principles this category
implements); KA 6.1.6 (the AI-narrative verification caution this topic directly extends); KA 13.2 (the
data layer feeding these dashboards); 13.4.14 (connecting a BI tool to the data layer in practice).

### 13.4.9 RPA & workflow automation

**Definition & purpose.** Robotic Process Automation (RPA) is software that automates repetitive,
rules-based digital tasks by mimicking the steps a human would take in existing software interfaces —
logging in, extracting a value from one screen, entering it into another, sending a notification when a
condition is met. Modern RPA is increasingly described as **intelligent automation** where it is combined
with AI/ML components (frequently the document-intelligence techniques of 13.4.3) to handle inputs that are
not perfectly structured — a scanned invoice rather than a fixed-format data feed, for example.

**How it helps.** RPA excels at exactly the kind of repetitive, well-defined, high-volume task a controls
function performs every reporting cycle: extracting cost postings from an ERP and mapping them to control
accounts (KA 5.2, KA 6.1.3's AI-in-this-topic note introduced this use directly), matching invoices to
purchase orders in the Procure-to-Pay cycle (KA 11.2), or routing a newly logged risk to its owner and
prompting a reassessment when a review date falls due (KA 12.2.1's AI note). Because the rules are
explicit and the task is repeated identically each cycle, a well-built RPA bot is typically far more
consistent — and, once built, far faster — than the equivalent manual process.

**Limits & governance.** A rules-based bot does exactly what it is configured to do, including faithfully
repeating an error if the underlying source data or the bot's own logic is wrong — it does not judge
whether an extracted figure looks plausible the way a human preparer might. It also does not adapt on its
own to a change in the source system's layout or a new document format outside the automation's original
scope; a bot that has run reliably for months can silently fail, or silently produce a wrong result, the
moment the underlying system changes without the automation being updated to match. Regular reconciliation
between a bot's output and an independent source check remains essential, particularly for a process
feeding directly into financial figures.

**Representative tool types.** Established RPA platforms include UiPath, Automation Anywhere and Microsoft
Power Automate, spanning from pure rules-based automation to increasingly AI-augmented "intelligent
automation" offerings that incorporate document-intelligence and, in some cases, generative AI components
for handling less structured inputs.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** Meridian's controls team has an RPA bot that extracts the
monthly cost-element postings for Pipeline Segment 4B directly from the ERP each period, maps them to the
five control accounts (WP1–WP5) via a pre-configured cost-coding rule set, and populates the AC figures used
in KA 6.1.3's Month-3 worked example — reducing what was previously a half-day manual extraction and
reconciliation task to a same-day, largely automated one. When Meridian's IT function updated the ERP's
export format ahead of Month 6, the bot initially mapped one cost element to the wrong control account
silently; the discrepancy was caught only because the controls team's standing practice — an independent
reconciliation of the bot's output total against the ERP's own control total each period — flagged a
$4,000 mismatch, which was traced to the format change and corrected before the Month-6 AC figure was
reported.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a bot's output as self-verifying once it has run reliably for a period, rather than maintaining
  the independent reconciliation check that caught Meridian's Month-6 mapping error above.
- Building automation around a source system's current layout without a change-monitoring process to catch
  when that layout changes.
- Confusing RPA's deterministic, rules-based automation with GenAI's probabilistic text generation — the
  two are frequently combined in "intelligent automation" but fail in different ways and need different
  checks.

**Honest AI note.** RPA delivers real, well-established speed and consistency gains for repetitive,
rules-based project-controls tasks, and the addition of AI components extends it usefully to less
structured inputs. It is not self-correcting: a bot faithfully repeats whatever its configuration and
source data tell it to, including an undetected error, which is why an independent reconciliation check —
not a one-off validation at build time — remains part of the process, not an optional extra.

**Cross-references.** KA 5.2 (the cost control cycle this automates); KA 6.1.3 (AC extraction, worked in
full); KA 11.2 (Procure-to-Pay matching); 13.4.3 (document intelligence, frequently combined with RPA);
KA 13.6 (auditability of an automated process).

### 13.4.10 Computer vision for site progress

**Definition & purpose.** Computer vision applies machine learning to visual data — photographs, 360°
imagery, drone/aerial capture, or laser-scanned point clouds — to recognise and measure physical conditions
on a construction or infrastructure site. Applied to progress measurement, it compares captured site
imagery (often on a repeated, scheduled capture cycle) against a 3D model (BIM) or against a defined scope
of work, to generate an independent, imagery-derived estimate of physical percentage complete.

**How it helps.** Site progress is traditionally assessed by manual site walks, quantity surveys, and the
measurement-method judgements described in KA 6.1.4. Computer-vision progress tools offer a genuinely
useful **independent, imagery-based cross-check** on those manually reported figures — capturing a
site-wide record on a fixed cadence, without depending entirely on a site team's own self-reported progress,
and creating a time-stamped visual record that can be reviewed later if a dispute over what was actually
in place on a given date arises (a use with direct relevance to claims and delay analysis, 13.4.4).

**Limits & governance.** A computer-vision estimate of percentage complete depends entirely on what the
imagery can actually see: a single fixed-time flight or scan cannot capture work completed and then covered
(e.g. backfilled trenching) if it happens outside the capture window, and dense vegetation, weather, or
site obstructions can occlude the very areas the tool is trying to assess. A computer-vision reading is
therefore best treated as an **independent data point to reconcile against verified site data**, not as an
automatically superior "ground truth" that should override a properly measured EVM percentage-complete
figure without investigation — exactly as the worked mini-case below demonstrates.

**Representative tool types.** The category includes reality-capture and AI-progress-tracking platforms —
vendors such as OpenSpace, Buildots and Doxel are examples in this space — that combine 360°/drone/LiDAR
capture with computer-vision models trained to compare captured imagery against a BIM model or a defined
scope, generating a percentage-complete estimate and a browsable, time-stamped visual record of the site.

**Example 13.4.10 — Reconciling a drone-based computer-vision progress reading against WP3's reported EV.**

*Scenario.* Continuing KA 6.1.4's Month-3 worked example, Pipeline Segment 4B's WP3 (trenching &
earthworks, budget $200,000 for 2,000 metres at $100/metre) was reported at Month 3 as 900 metres
excavated — 45% complete, EV = $90,000, on a percentage-complete (units of measure) basis. Meridian's site
team separately commissions a drone-based computer-vision progress tool, which flies the alignment on the
morning of the Month-3 data date and analyses the resulting orthomosaic imagery.

*Required.* Compare the computer-vision reading against the reported EV figure, and determine the correct
next step.

*Working.* The drone imagery shows 810 metres of visible open or freshly backfilled trench surface
disturbance along the alignment — `810 / 2,000 = 40.5%`, implying an EV of `40.5% × 200,000 = 81,000`, a
`90,000 − 81,000 = 9,000` (4.5 percentage point) shortfall against the reported figure. Investigating the
gap before accepting either number, the site engineer checks the daily site diary and finds that a
night-shift crew completed a further 95 metres of trenching *after* the drone's morning flight window on
the same day, which the single fixed-time flight could not have captured: `810 + 95 = 905` metres, against
the reported 900 metres — a difference of 5 metres, within normal chainage-marker measurement tolerance.

*Answer.* The reported EV of **$90,000 (45% complete, 900 m)** is confirmed as materially correct once the
drone flight's timing limitation is accounted for; the computer-vision tool's raw reading of 40.5% was not
itself the more reliable figure, despite being AI-derived from sensor data.

*Interpretation.* This is the central, honest lesson of computer-vision progress tracking: a single
capture's coverage is bounded by *when and where* it looked, not only by the sophistication of the model
analysing what it saw. The tool still delivered real value here — it prompted a targeted investigation that
would not otherwise have happened that period — but the investigation, not the AI reading itself, is what
established which figure was actually correct. Reconciling the two also usefully confirmed the reported
45% was not a front-loaded or overstated figure (a failure mode KA 6.1.4 specifically warns against), which
the drone cross-check could not have shown on its own without the site-diary follow-up.

**Fig 13.4.10 — Reconciling computer-vision and reported progress: Pipeline Segment 4B, WP3, Month 3.**
Chart type: a horizontal comparison bar chart. Three bars, each showing metres of trench progress out of
2,000 m total: (1) "Reported EV (KA 6.1.4)" at 900 m, in brand blue `#1D4ED8`; (2) "Drone CV reading (same-day
morning flight)" at 810 m, in a lighter neutral tone, with a dashed extension segment to 905 m labelled
"+ 95 m night-shift work outside flight window"; (3) "Reconciled position" at 905 m, in brand blue, with a
small annotated gap of 5 m labelled "within chainage-marker tolerance." A callout box beside the chart
reads: "AI proposes an independent reading; the professional's investigation, not the reading alone,
resolves the discrepancy."

*Underlying data:* the working in Example 13.4.10 (reported 900 m/45%/$90,000; drone reading 810 m/40.5%/
$81,000; night-shift addition 95 m; reconciled 905 m against reported 900 m, 5 m tolerance).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Automatically trusting a computer-vision reading over a professionally measured and verified EVM figure,
  simply because the CV reading is sensor- and AI-derived and therefore feels more "objective."
- Scheduling capture at a single fixed time of day or a single flight path without considering what work
  patterns (night shifts, work under cover, work behind an obstruction) the capture window will systematically
  miss.
- Using a computer-vision percentage-complete reading as a wholesale replacement for the measurement
  methods established in KA 6.1.4, rather than as one additional, independently sourced data point.

**Honest AI note.** Computer-vision progress tools provide a genuinely valuable, independently sourced
check on reported physical progress and build a useful time-stamped visual record for later reference —
real capabilities with real value, illustrated by the discrepancy investigation above. They are bounded by
what their imagery capture actually saw, at the time and from the angle it was captured, and a raw CV
reading is not automatically more reliable than a properly verified manual measurement; discrepancies are
investigated, not resolved by simply preferring whichever figure is AI-generated.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.1.4 (the measurement methods this category cross-checks); KA 6.1.6 (front-loading
and data-integrity cautions this example reinforces); KA 7.2 (a time-stamped visual record's relevance to
claims and delay evidence); 13.4.4 (NLP correspondence analysis, a related independent-signal category).

### 13.4.11 Predictive analytics & ML platforms

**Definition & purpose.** This category covers general-purpose machine-learning and data-science platforms
— not built specifically for project controls, but used by an analytically capable team to build **bespoke
predictive models**: for example, a model scoring the likelihood of cost overrun or schedule delay across a
portfolio of projects, trained on that organisation's own historical project data.

**How it helps.** Where the earlier categories in this KA are largely pre-built products aimed at a
specific project-controls task, this category is the toolkit a controls or data-analytics function uses to
build something bespoke when no off-the-shelf tool fits the organisation's specific need — for example, a
model that scores every active project in a portfolio against an organisation-specific set of historical
overrun predictors, surfacing which projects deserve closer attention before their formal metrics (CPI,
SPI) show a problem. Because the model is trained on the organisation's *own* historical data, it can, done
well, reflect patterns genuinely specific to that organisation's projects, contracts and delivery context in
a way a generic vendor tool cannot.

**Limits & governance.** Building a reliable bespoke model requires a substantial volume of clean,
well-labelled historical data (KA 13.2) and genuine data-science skill to build, validate and maintain —
this is the most technically demanding category in this KA, and a poorly built or poorly validated bespoke
model can be actively misleading while still producing an authoritative-looking risk score. A model of this
kind also requires ongoing monitoring for **drift** — its accuracy degrading over time as the organisation's
projects, contracts or market conditions change from the patterns it was originally trained on — a
maintenance burden a packaged, vendor-maintained tool does not place on the buying organisation to the same
degree.

**Representative tool types.** Cloud-based ML platforms (such as Microsoft Azure Machine Learning, Google
Vertex AI and Amazon SageMaker) and open-source data-science stacks (commonly Python or R, with widely used
statistical/ML libraries) provide the general-purpose building blocks; some vendors also offer packaged
"AutoML" tools intended to lower the data-science skill required to build a first model. This category
overlaps with 13.4.6's cost-forecasting tools where the specific predictive task is cost, but is broader,
covering any bespoke predictive use a controls function might build.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** Meridian's central controls function builds a portfolio-level
delay-risk-scoring model, trained on five years of Meridian's own completed pipeline-segment projects,
scoring every active segment monthly against predictors including procurement lead-time variance, permit-
authority historical turnaround, and early CPI/SPI trend shape. At the point Pipeline Segment 4B's Month-5
CPI/SPI shortfall was formally reported (KA 6.3), the model's Month-2 score had already placed Segment 4B in
its top decile of portfolio delay risk — driven predominantly by the same WP2 procurement lead-time pattern
independently flagged by the cost-forecasting model in 13.4.6's use case. The two models, built for
different purposes on different techniques, converging on the same underlying signal gave Meridian's
programme director materially more confidence in escalating WP2 for senior review than either model's
output alone would have provided — though the decision to escalate, and the response that followed, remained
the director's, not the models'.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Deploying a bespoke model without an ongoing validation/monitoring plan, so drift goes undetected until
  the model's predictions have already become materially unreliable.
- Building a model on too small or too narrow a historical data set for the organisation to trust its output
  at the confidence level it is being presented with.
- Treating model convergence (two independent models agreeing) as proof of correctness rather than as one
  further, still-fallible piece of evidence — both models here drew on closely related underlying data about
  the same procurement pattern, so their agreement is less independent confirmation than it might first
  appear.

**Honest AI note.** A well-built, well-maintained bespoke predictive model can surface an organisation-
specific risk pattern a generic tool would miss, as in Meridian's portfolio example — a genuine capability,
not a hypothetical one. Building and maintaining one reliably requires real data-science skill, a
substantial clean historical data set, and ongoing drift monitoring that many controls functions
underestimate at the point of build; a model deployed once and never revisited is a governance risk in
itself, not a finished asset.

**Cross-references.** KA 13.2 (the data-quality precondition for any bespoke model); 13.4.6 (cost-specific
forecasting, a related application); KA 12.3 (contingency and reserve decisions a portfolio risk score might
inform); KA 13.7 (the analytical maturity a controls function needs to build and sustain this category).

### 13.4.12 The "AI embedded in existing PM/ERP tools" pattern

**Definition & purpose.** Rather than adopting a separate, dedicated AI product for a given task, an
organisation can increasingly access broadly equivalent AI capability through a **feature already added
into a PM, scheduling, cost-management, ERP or BI platform it has already licensed and is already using**.
This is less a distinct technical category than a **deployment pattern** cutting across every category
already covered in this KA — a general-purpose assistant embedded in a word processor (13.4.2), a
natural-language query feature inside a BI tool (13.4.8), or a delay-prediction feature inside a scheduling
platform (13.4.5) are all instances of the same underlying pattern.

**How it helps.** An embedded feature typically requires far less integration effort than a new, separate
tool: the data connection to the system of record already exists (it is the same system), the access
controls and audit logging usually already match the organisation's existing governance for that system
(cross-reference KA 13.6), and there is no new vendor relationship, contract, or data-export pathway to
stand up. For an organisation earlier in its AI adoption journey (cross-reference KA 13.7's maturity model),
enabling an already-licensed embedded feature is frequently the fastest, lowest-risk way to get real value
from AI without a new procurement cycle.

**Limits & governance.** An embedded feature is usually less specialised, and often less mature, than a
best-of-breed dedicated tool built specifically for that one task — the vendor's core product was not
originally designed around this feature, and the AI capability may be newer, more limited in scope, or
slower to improve than a point solution whose entire business is that one task. It also, by definition,
ties the organisation's use of that AI capability to that vendor's product roadmap, pricing and continued
investment in the feature — a form of dependence distinct from, but related to, the vendor lock-in
discussed in 13.4.13.

**Representative tool types.** This pattern now appears across most major enterprise software categories
relevant to project controls: enterprise resource planning suites (for example, SAP's "Joule" generative-AI
assistant introduced across parts of its enterprise portfolio, and AI features within Oracle's Fusion and
Primavera product lines), project- and construction-management platforms (AI features within Procore and
Autodesk Construction Cloud, among others), and office/productivity suites (Microsoft's Copilot integrated
across Project, Dynamics and Office). The specific feature set behind each of these names changes
frequently and should always be confirmed directly with the vendor rather than assumed from this list.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** This is the deciding option in the worked tool-selection
example in 13.4.15: rather than procuring a new BI connector project to get an AI-drafted monthly narrative
for Pipeline Segment 4B, Meridian evaluates simply switching on an AI copilot feature already bundled, but
currently unused, within its existing ERP/PM suite licence — see 13.4.15 for the full worked comparison
against the alternative categories.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assuming an embedded feature has the same depth of capability as a dedicated best-of-breed tool in that
  category, without checking — the two are not automatically equivalent.
- Enabling an embedded AI feature without applying the same governance review (data access, output
  verification, audit trail) that a newly procured stand-alone tool would have gone through, simply because
  it "came with" an already-approved system.
- Building a long-term workflow entirely around one vendor's embedded feature without considering what
  happens if that vendor's roadmap for the feature changes or is discontinued.

**Honest AI note.** The embedded-AI pattern is one of the fastest-growing trends across enterprise software
relevant to project controls, and is frequently the pragmatic first move for an organisation building AI
capability, as 13.4.15's worked example shows. It is not automatically the best-fitting tool for every task
— only for tasks where the already-licensed platform's core function is a good match — and every embedded
feature still requires the same output verification and governance review as a stand-alone product, not a
lighter-touch review simply because it arrived pre-installed.

**Cross-references.** Every other topic in this KA (the pattern applies across categories); 13.4.13 (build
vs buy, of which enabling an embedded feature is a specific "buy — already owned" case); KA 13.6 (governance
applies equally to embedded features); KA 13.7 (the maturity-model context for when this is the right first
step).

### 13.4.13 Build vs buy

**Definition & purpose.** For almost any AI-enabled capability a project controls function wants — an
automated variance narrative, a delay-prediction model, a contract-extraction workflow — there is a choice
between **buying** an existing product (a dedicated point solution, or an already-licensed embedded
feature, 13.4.12) and **building** a bespoke solution (typically using the general-purpose GenAI or
predictive-analytics building blocks of 13.4.2 and 13.4.11, assembled and maintained in-house). This is a
recurring strategic decision, not a one-off choice made once for the whole organisation — different needs
within the same controls function can, and often should, land on different sides of it.

**No new formula.** This decision is a structured judgement, not a calculation; the weighted-scoring
technique in 13.4.15 is one disciplined way to make it, applied there to a related but distinct question
(which category, having already decided broadly to buy, best fits a specific need).

**The underlying trade-off.** Buying is typically faster to deploy, lower initial technical risk, and
comes with vendor-maintained updates and support — but it fits the organisation's specific process only as
well as the vendor's product was designed to, creates a dependency on that vendor's continued investment
and pricing (13.4.12's lock-in point), and may expose data to a third party's systems, a governance question
KA 13.6 covers in depth. Building is typically slower, requires in-house technical capability the
organisation may not yet have (13.4.11's data-science skill requirement), and creates an ongoing maintenance
burden the organisation owns indefinitely — but it can fit a genuinely specific need exactly, keeps data
inside the organisation's own environment, and avoids dependency on a single vendor's roadmap.

**A practical decision framework.** Four questions tend to separate the cases reasonably cleanly:

- **Is this need common across the industry, or specific to us?** A common need (drafting a variance
  narrative, extracting fields from a standard contract) is more likely to be well served by an existing
  product; a genuinely organisation-specific need (a predictive model reflecting Meridian's own historical
  delivery pattern) is more likely to need bespoke building.
- **How mature is our internal technical capability?** Building well requires real, sustained data-science
  or software capability (13.4.11); an organisation without it, or without the appetite to build it, will
  likely get more reliable value from buying, at least initially (cross-reference KA 13.7's maturity model).
- **How sensitive is the underlying data, and what does that require?** Highly sensitive data may favour
  either a tightly governed in-house build or a vendor product with strong, contractually confirmed data-
  handling commitments — the deciding factor is the strength of governance available, not the build/buy
  label itself.
- **What does the total cost of ownership look like over several years, not just at initial deployment?**
  A "buy" option's subscription cost recurs indefinitely; a "build" option's initial cost is often lower
  but its maintenance, monitoring (13.4.11's drift point) and eventual re-platforming cost is easy to
  underestimate at the outset.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Deciding build vs buy purely on upfront cost, ignoring the multi-year total cost of ownership on both
  sides.
- Building bespoke tooling for a genuinely common, well-served need, duplicating capability a mature vendor
  product already provides reliably.
- Buying a specialised point solution for a need an already-licensed embedded feature (13.4.12) would have
  served adequately, at materially lower integration cost.

**Honest AI note.** Neither build nor buy is inherently the safer or more capable choice — each carries a
different profile of speed, fit, dependency and governance risk, and the right answer genuinely varies by
need, by organisation, and over time as internal capability matures (KA 13.7). A build/buy decision made
once and never revisited is itself a risk, given how quickly both the vendor market and an organisation's
own capability can change.

**Cross-references.** 13.4.12 (the embedded-feature "buy, already owned" case); 13.4.11 (the technical
capability building requires); KA 13.6 (data governance considerations on both sides); KA 13.7 (maturity and
capability-building over time); 13.4.15 (a worked selection decision that operationalises this framework).

### 13.4.14 Integrating an AI tool with the data layer

**Definition & purpose.** Almost every category in this KA is only as useful as its connection to the
organisation's actual project data — the ERP/cost ledger, the schedule, the contract register, the risk
register (KA 13.2's "fuel"). This topic covers the practical patterns for making that connection, and the
governance that must accompany it.

**The underlying principle.** KA 13.2 establishes, in general terms, that data quality and structure
determine AI outcomes more than any tool's sophistication does; this topic is where that principle meets
practical implementation. Three connection patterns recur:

- **Manual transfer** — a person exports or copies data from the source system and pastes or uploads it
  into the AI tool (as in 13.4.2's basic use case). Simplest to set up, but slow, error-prone at scale, and,
  where a general-purpose ungoverned assistant is involved, a genuine confidentiality risk (13.4.2's
  caution).
- **API/connector-based integration** — the AI tool connects programmatically to the source system, pulling
  (or, less commonly, pushing) data automatically on a defined schedule or trigger. This is how a BI
  dashboard's AI features (13.4.8) or an RPA bot (13.4.9) typically operate, and how Meridian's dashboard in
  13.4.8's use case was built. It requires genuine integration effort and ongoing maintenance (as Meridian's
  RPA mini-case in 13.4.9 illustrates when the source system's export format changed) but scales far better
  than manual transfer once built.
- **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** — a pattern specific to GenAI assistants, where the assistant is
  given controlled, governed access to search and retrieve from a defined set of the organisation's own
  documents or data at the moment of answering a question, rather than relying only on its general training.
  This lets a GenAI assistant answer questions grounded in Meridian's *own* contracts, procedures or project
  data, with a materially lower hallucination risk than an ungrounded general query, because the model is
  retrieving and citing specific source passages rather than generating purely from its general training.

**Governance.** Every integration pattern raises the same core questions, regardless of category: who can
access what data through the tool (role-based access control matching the organisation's existing
permissions, not a separate, looser standard); is the connection read-only or can the tool write back into
the system of record (a write-back capability, as in some embedded-AI and RPA cases, raises materially
higher governance stakes than a read-only connection); and is there an audit trail of what the tool
accessed and produced, sufficient to reconstruct, after the fact, what an AI-assisted output was based on
(KA 13.6's auditability requirement). A tool that is technically well integrated but ungoverned on these
questions is a bigger risk than a poorly integrated tool used cautiously, because integration itself
increases the tool's reach into live, sensitive data.

**Use case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC.** Building the BI-dashboard connection referenced in 13.4.8
required Meridian's IT function to establish an API-based, read-only connector from the ERP and scheduling
systems into the BI platform, with access permissions mirrored exactly from the existing ERP role structure
(so a user who could not see Segment 5A's commercial detail in the ERP could not see it via the dashboard
either) and a logging record of every natural-language query run against the connection. This governance
work — not the AI features themselves — was the majority of the implementation effort and timeline, a
pattern 13.4.15's worked selection example weights explicitly through its "integration effort" criterion.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Connecting an AI tool to a live data source with broader access permissions than the equivalent human
  user would have, "for convenience," rather than mirroring existing role-based access exactly.
- Granting a tool write-back access to a system of record before its outputs have a track record of
  reliability, when a read-only connection with human-reviewed write-back would materially reduce risk at
  little cost in convenience.
- Treating integration as a one-off technical project rather than an ongoing relationship requiring
  monitoring for source-system changes (13.4.9's mini-case) and periodic access-permission review.

**Honest AI note.** A well-integrated AI tool, grounded in an organisation's own governed data (through an
API connection or a RAG pattern), is materially more useful and materially safer against hallucination than
the same tool used with manually copied, ungoverned data — a real and worthwhile investment, illustrated by
Meridian's dashboard build. Integration effort is very commonly the majority of a real AI-tooling project's
cost and timeline, not the AI capability itself, and any selection decision that ignores that (13.4.15)
under-costs the true effort involved.

**Cross-references.** KA 13.2 (data quality, structure and governance in full); KA 13.6 (access control,
auditability and confidentiality governance); 13.4.8, 13.4.9 (categories built around this kind of
integration); 13.4.15 (the worked selection example that weights integration effort explicitly).

### 13.4.15 Evaluating and selecting an AI tool: a selection rubric

**Definition & purpose.** Having surveyed the categories, this closing topic gives a repeatable method for
choosing *between* them for a specific, real need — a **weighted-scoring rubric**: a small set of decision
criteria, each assigned a weight reflecting its relative importance to the organisation, against which each
candidate option is scored and a total weighted score computed. It formalises, rather than replaces, the
professional judgement already used informally throughout this KA's use cases.

**Formula.** `Weighted score (option i) = Σ (criterion weight_j × option i's rating on criterion j)`,
summed across all criteria j, with weights expressed as decimals summing to 1.00 and ratings on a common
scale (here, 1–5, 5 = best fit) so that every option is compared on the same footing.

**Example 13.4.15 — Selecting an approach to automate Pipeline Segment 4B's monthly cost-narrative and AC
reconciliation.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's project controls team wants to reduce the recurring manual effort behind two linked
monthly tasks on Pipeline Segment 4B (and, prospectively, Segment 5A): reconciling AC from the ERP cost
ledger (KA 6.1.3) and drafting the EVM narrative commentary (KA 6.1.6). Four candidate approaches, each
drawn from a category already covered in this KA, are shortlisted:

- **Option A** — a general-purpose GenAI assistant (13.4.2), used by manually copying the month's EVM
  figures into the chat interface each period and prompting it to draft the narrative.
- **Option B** — a BI platform with embedded AI features (13.4.8), connected directly to the ERP and
  schedule data via a new API integration (13.4.14).
- **Option C** — an RPA bot (13.4.9) automating AC extraction and reconciliation only, feeding the existing
  manual spreadsheet-based reporting process (no narrative-drafting AI).
- **Option D** — enabling the AI copilot feature already bundled within Meridian's existing ERP/PM suite
  licence (13.4.12), currently switched off.

The team agrees six weighted criteria, reflecting Meridian's own priorities for this decision:

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Data governance / confidentiality fit | 0.25 |
| Integration effort required (lower effort scores higher) | 0.20 |
| Time-to-value / speed of deployment | 0.15 |
| Cost fit against the team's illustrative internal pilot budget | 0.15 |
| Accuracy / fit for the specific narrative + reconciliation task | 0.15 |
| Scalability to Segment 5A and future projects | 0.10 |
| **Total** | **1.00** |

*Required.* Each option's rating (1–5) on each criterion, the resulting weighted score, and a
recommendation.

*Working.*

| Criterion (weight) | A: GenAI assistant | B: BI + AI, new integration | C: RPA only | D: Embedded ERP AI (enable existing) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Governance (0.25) | 2 — manual copy-paste of ledger data into a chat tool each period risks confidentiality exposure unless tightly governed (13.4.2) | 4 — data stays inside Meridian's own governed BI environment | 4 — rule-based, stays within Meridian's environment | 5 — data never leaves the existing, already-audited system of record |
| Integration effort (0.20) | 5 — no integration needed | 2 — a genuine new connector build required (13.4.14) | 3 — moderate RPA configuration effort | 4 — largely a configuration/enablement exercise, not a new build |
| Time-to-value (0.15) | 5 — usable immediately | 2 — a multi-week/month integration project | 3 — moderate | 4 — faster than B; no new connector to engineer |
| Cost fit (0.15) | 5 — within existing subscription, no new spend | 3 — moderate implementation cost | 4 — modest RPA licensing | 4 — feature already covered by the existing licence, aside from modest enablement/training cost |
| Accuracy / task fit (0.15) | 3 — fluent drafting, but no live data link; figures re-typed each period, a transcription risk | 5 — narrative generated directly from live, reconciled data | 2 — solves reconciliation well but does nothing for the narrative-drafting task itself | 4 — generated from the same live system of record; solid though less proven at Meridian than a mature BI platform |
| Scalability (0.10) | 2 — manual copy-paste does not scale cleanly across projects | 5 — extends cleanly to Segment 5A on the same data model once built | 4 — bots are reusable with configuration | 4 — extends to other projects on the same ERP instance |

`Weighted score (A) = 0.25(2) + 0.20(5) + 0.15(5) + 0.15(5) + 0.15(3) + 0.10(2)`
`= 0.50 + 1.00 + 0.75 + 0.75 + 0.45 + 0.20 = 3.65`

`Weighted score (B) = 0.25(4) + 0.20(2) + 0.15(2) + 0.15(3) + 0.15(5) + 0.10(5)`
`= 1.00 + 0.40 + 0.30 + 0.45 + 0.75 + 0.50 = 3.40`

`Weighted score (C) = 0.25(4) + 0.20(3) + 0.15(3) + 0.15(4) + 0.15(2) + 0.10(4)`
`= 1.00 + 0.60 + 0.45 + 0.60 + 0.30 + 0.40 = 3.35`

`Weighted score (D) = 0.25(5) + 0.20(4) + 0.15(4) + 0.15(4) + 0.15(4) + 0.10(4)`
`= 1.25 + 0.80 + 0.60 + 0.60 + 0.60 + 0.40 = 4.25`

*Answer.* **Option D (enable the existing, already-licensed embedded ERP/PM AI feature) scores highest, at
4.25 out of 5**, ahead of Option A (3.65), Option B (3.40) and Option C (3.35).

*Interpretation.* The result is a useful corrective to an instinct this KA has flagged more than once: the
fastest, cheapest option to start using (Option A) is not the highest-scoring once governance and
scalability are weighted at their genuine importance to Meridian, and the option requiring the most new
integration effort (Option B) does not automatically win just because its raw accuracy/task-fit score is
highest. Option D wins specifically *because* it combines strong governance (already inside an audited
system) with low incremental integration effort (already connected) — the embedded-AI pattern's structural
advantage described generally in 13.4.12, confirmed here by a disciplined, weighted comparison rather than
by instinct alone. Two cautions belong alongside the number, not after it: first, the weights themselves
are a policy choice Meridian's team made deliberately (governance weighted highest, reflecting the
sensitivity of cost and commercial data) — a different organisation, or a different task, could reasonably
weight these criteria differently and reach a different answer, and the weights should be reviewed and
owned by the accountable professional, not treated as objectively fixed. Second, a rubric score is an input
to the decision, not the decision itself — Meridian's team used the 4.25 result to *recommend* Option D to
the sponsor for a governed pilot, not to authorise it automatically; the same **"AI proposes; the
professional disposes"** principle that runs through this entire KA applies to the rubric's own output as
much as to any AI tool's.

**Mini-case — six months later: piloting Option D.** Meridian's sponsor approves a governed pilot of Option
D on Pipeline Segment 4B, with the project controls lead reviewing every AI-drafted narrative against the
source EVM table before issue, exactly as in 13.4.2's earlier use case. Four months into the pilot, the
lead catches an instance where the embedded tool's auto-drafted narrative summarised the Month-5 CPI/SPI
shortfall (KA 6.3) without mentioning that the geotechnical survey (KA 12.2.5) had, by that point, already
reduced R1's probability and narrowed the range of likely outcomes — a true but incomplete narrative that
would have read more alarming than the full picture warranted. The lead adds a short manual review
checklist step — confirm the narrative reflects the *current* risk position, not only the *current* EVM
figures — to the pilot's standing procedure before it is rolled out beyond the pilot. The tool is judged a
success and extended to Segment 5A on schedule; the checklist addition, prompted by a real gap the pilot
surfaced, is folded into Meridian's wider AI-use procedure under KA 13.6's assurance framework.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a weighted-scoring rubric's numerical output as objective simply because it involves arithmetic
  — the weights and the ratings feeding it are both judgement calls that must themselves be reviewed and
  owned.
- Setting weights to justify a preferred answer already decided informally, rather than setting them first,
  independently of which option they will favour.
- Skipping the governed pilot step after a rubric recommends an option, treating the score itself as
  sufficient authorisation to roll a tool out at full scale.

**Honest AI note.** A weighted-scoring rubric is a genuinely useful discipline for making a build/buy or
category-selection decision explicit and comparable, rather than intuitive and undocumented — and, as
Meridian's result shows, it can produce a defensible answer that runs against the instinctive first choice.
It does not remove judgement from the decision; it structures where that judgement is applied — in setting
the weights, in scoring each option honestly, and in what happens after the number is produced, exactly as
the pilot mini-case's checklist addition illustrates.

**Cross-references.** 13.4.13 (the build-vs-buy framework this rubric operationalises); 13.4.12 (the
embedded-AI pattern the worked example confirms as the winning option here); 13.4.14 (integration effort, a
weighted criterion in the example); KA 13.6 (the governance and assurance framework the pilot mini-case
feeds into); KA 13.7 (building capability from a successful pilot).

### Key terms

- **AI tool landscape / category map** — positioning a category of AI tool by how general-purpose or
  project-controls-specific it is, and by how much autonomy it takes on before human review (13.4.1).
- **General-purpose GenAI assistant** — a conversational, LLM-based tool for drafting, summarising and
  reasoning over natural-language input, not built specifically for project controls (13.4.2).
- **Optical Character Recognition (OCR) / Intelligent Document Processing (IDP)** — technology that converts
  scanned or image-based documents into machine-readable, structured text and extracted fields (13.4.3).
- **Contract Lifecycle Management (CLM) / contract-analytics tools** — IDP applied specifically to
  contracts: clause extraction, obligation tracking and key-date alerting across a contract portfolio
  (13.4.3).
- **NLP correspondence/claims analytics** — natural-language processing applied to unstructured
  correspondence to surface themes, sentiment and candidate material relevant to claims and disputes
  (13.4.4).
- **AI-assisted logic checking** — automated structural review of a schedule network (open ends, missing
  links, constraint patterns) using AI/ML techniques (13.4.5).
- **Leading-indicator forecasting** — ML models that flag a forming cost or schedule trend from precursor
  signals, ahead of a lagging classical metric such as CPI/SPI making the trend clearly visible (13.4.6).
- **AI-assisted Monte Carlo** — traditional Monte Carlo simulation (KA 12.2.6) augmented with AI/ML features
  for range suggestion and correlation detection (13.4.7).
- **Natural-language query / auto-generated narrative** — BI features letting a user ask a plain-language
  question of connected data, and receive an AI-drafted written summary of a chart or table (13.4.8).
- **Robotic Process Automation (RPA) / intelligent automation** — software automating repetitive, rules-
  based digital tasks, increasingly combined with AI components for less structured inputs (13.4.9).
- **Computer vision for site progress** — machine-learning analysis of site imagery (photo/360°/drone/
  LiDAR) to generate an independent, imagery-derived estimate of physical percentage complete (13.4.10).
- **Predictive analytics / ML platform** — general-purpose machine-learning tooling used to build bespoke,
  organisation-specific predictive models (13.4.11).
- **Model drift** — the gradual degradation of a predictive model's accuracy as real-world conditions diverge
  from its training data over time (13.4.11).
- **Embedded AI ("copilot") pattern** — accessing AI capability through a feature already added into an
  already-licensed PM/ERP/BI platform, rather than a separate dedicated product (13.4.12).
- **Build vs buy** — the strategic choice between procuring an existing AI product and building a bespoke
  solution in-house (13.4.13).
- **Data layer integration** — the practical patterns (manual transfer, API/connector, retrieval-augmented
  generation) for connecting an AI tool to an organisation's actual project data (13.4.14).
- **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** — a pattern giving a GenAI assistant controlled, governed access
  to retrieve from an organisation's own documents or data when answering a question, reducing hallucination
  risk relative to an ungrounded query (13.4.14).
- **Weighted-scoring rubric** — a structured tool-selection method: `Weighted score = Σ (criterion weight ×
  option's rating)`, used to compare candidate tools or categories against agreed, weighted criteria
  (13.4.15).
- **Tool sprawl** — the accumulation of multiple, overlapping AI tools across an organisation without a
  coordinated selection or governance process.
- **Vendor lock-in** — dependency on a single vendor's product, pricing and roadmap created by adopting its
  tool or embedded feature, relevant to both 13.4.12 and 13.4.13.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 13.4.1 [topic 13.4.3, level: recall]**
Which category of tool is best described as using OCR and machine learning to extract structured fields
(parties, dates, payment terms) from a scanned or PDF contract?
A. A general-purpose GenAI assistant
B. **Document intelligence / contract-analytics tool** ✔
C. A robotic process automation (RPA) bot
D. A computer-vision progress-tracking tool
*Rationale:* Document intelligence/IDP and contract-analytics tools combine OCR with ML-based field
extraction specifically for documents such as contracts. A general-purpose assistant has no document-
specific extraction pipeline unless one is built around it; RPA automates rules-based digital steps rather
than extracting fields from scanned text; computer vision analyses site imagery, not documents.

**MCQ 13.4.2 [topic 13.4.2/13.4.14, level: application]**
A project controls analyst pastes unredacted, commercially sensitive cost and contract data into a free,
consumer-grade, ungoverned GenAI chat tool to draft a variance narrative. What is the primary governance
concern this illustrates?
A. Model drift
B. **Data confidentiality / potential data leakage** ✔
C. Vendor lock-in
D. Tool sprawl
*Rationale:* Pasting sensitive data into an ungoverned, consumer-grade tool risks that data being retained,
used to train the underlying model, or otherwise exposed outside the organisation's control — a
confidentiality risk covered in 13.4.2 and KA 13.6. Model drift concerns predictive-model accuracy over
time (13.4.11); vendor lock-in and tool sprawl are strategic, not confidentiality, concerns.

**MCQ 13.4.3 [topic 13.4.15, level: application — numerical]**
Using the weighted-scoring method (`Weighted score = Σ weight × rating`), a candidate tool scores 5 on a
criterion weighted 20%, 3 on a criterion weighted 30%, and 4 on a criterion weighted 50%. What is its total
weighted score, out of 5?
A. 3.0
B. 4.0
C. **3.9** ✔
D. 12.0
*Rationale:* `(0.20 × 5) + (0.30 × 3) + (0.50 × 4) = 1.0 + 0.9 + 2.0 = 3.9`. D wrongly sums the raw ratings
without weighting; A and B are plausible but incorrect roundings/estimates.

**MCQ 13.4.4 [topic 13.4.10, level: analysis]**
Which statement best reflects an honest assessment of computer-vision site-progress tools?
A. They always provide ground truth more reliable than a professionally verified percentage-complete
   measurement
B. **A computer-vision reading is bounded by what its imagery capture actually saw, and a discrepancy
   against a verified reported figure should be investigated before either figure is preferred** ✔
C. They eliminate the need to select a measurement method under EVM
D. They cannot meaningfully be applied to linear infrastructure projects such as a pipeline
*Rationale:* As shown in the WP3 drone-imagery mini-case, a CV reading can under- or over-read progress
depending on capture timing and coverage; discrepancies are investigated, not assumed resolved in the AI
tool's favour. C and D are false generalisations — CV tools do not replace EVM measurement methods (KA
6.1.4), and they are, in fact, well suited to linear infrastructure alignments.

**MCQ 13.4.5 [topic 13.4.12/13.4.13, level: analysis]**
An organisation chooses to enable an AI copilot feature already bundled within its existing, licensed ERP
suite, rather than procuring a new, separate best-of-breed AI product for the same task. Which consideration
does this decision most directly illustrate?
A. Model drift
B. **Build vs buy, favouring an already-owned "buy" option with typically lower integration effort and
   stronger existing governance fit** ✔
C. Retrieval-augmented generation
D. Front-loading
*Rationale:* Enabling an already-licensed embedded feature (13.4.12) is a specific instance of the "buy"
side of the build-vs-buy decision (13.4.13), typically favoured for its lower integration effort and its
inheriting the host system's existing governance. Model drift and RAG relate to predictive models and
GenAI-data integration respectively; front-loading is an EVM measurement-method distortion (KA 6.1.4),
unrelated here.

**MCQ 13.4.6 [topic 13.4.9, level: analysis]**
Which statement most accurately distinguishes classic Robotic Process Automation (RPA) from a generative-AI
document-drafting tool?
A. RPA can only be used in finance functions, while GenAI can be used anywhere
B. **RPA executes explicit, rules-based steps against structured or semi-structured inputs, while GenAI
   generates probabilistic, natural-language output and is better suited to unstructured drafting tasks** ✔
C. RPA and GenAI are simply two names for the same underlying technology
D. GenAI cannot be combined with RPA in the same automated workflow
*Rationale:* RPA follows deterministic, pre-configured rules against defined inputs (13.4.9); GenAI
generates language probabilistically and is suited to drafting and summarisation (13.4.2). The two are
increasingly combined into "intelligent automation" (13.4.9), which D wrongly rules out; A is an
unsupported restriction, and C conflates two distinct technologies.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Explain, in your own words, why document intelligence/OCR/contract-analysis tools (13.4.3) and NLP for
   correspondence & claims tools (13.4.4) are treated as two distinct categories in this KA, despite both
   processing text with AI. What kind of input and what kind of output does each typically handle?
2. A candidate tool scores 4 on a criterion weighted 40%, 2 on a criterion weighted 35%, and 5 on a
   criterion weighted 25%. Using the weighted-scoring formula from 13.4.15, calculate its total weighted
   score out of 5.
3. In the worked Meridian example (13.4.15), Option A (a general-purpose GenAI assistant, no new
   integration required) scored higher than Option B (a BI platform requiring a new integration) on four
   of the six criteria, yet Option D — not A or B — won overall. Explain, referring to the weights used, why
   this outcome is not a contradiction.
4. Using the WP3 computer-vision mini-case in 13.4.10, explain in one or two sentences why the reported EV
   figure (900 m / 45%) was ultimately judged correct even though the AI-derived drone reading (810 m /
   40.5%) initially disagreed with it.

**Answers**

1. Document intelligence/contract-analysis tools (13.4.3) extract *structured fields* from *defined,
   formal documents* (a contract, an invoice) — dates, amounts, named parties. NLP correspondence/claims
   tools (13.4.4) mine *unstructured, conversational text* (emails, site diaries, minutes) for *patterns* —
   themes, sentiment, candidate material for a claim — rather than discrete fields. The distinction matters
   because the two feed different downstream uses (structured data entry versus claims/dispute
   investigation) and carry different limits (extraction accuracy versus pattern-relevance judgement).
2. `(0.40 × 4) + (0.35 × 2) + (0.25 × 5) = 1.60 + 0.70 + 1.25 = 3.55`.
3. The weights were not evenly split: governance (0.25) and integration effort (0.20) together account for
   45% of the total score, and Option D scored highly on both (5 and 4 respectively) while Option A scored
   only 2 on governance. A high score on lower-weighted criteria (time-to-value, cost, both at 0.15) cannot
   overcome a weak score on the two highest-weighted criteria — the rubric is deliberately built so the
   organisation's stated priorities, not simply which option "wins the most categories," determine the
   result.
4. The drone flight's single, fixed morning capture window could not see the 95 metres of trenching a
   night-shift crew completed later the same day; once that 95 metres is added back (810 + 95 = 905 m),
   the reconciled figure sits within normal chainage-marker tolerance of the reported 900 m. The AI-derived
   reading was not wrong in what it measured — it simply had not seen the whole of what had actually been
   done, a coverage limitation rather than a modelling error.
