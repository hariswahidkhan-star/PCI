## KA 13.7 — Building an AI-augmented project-controls capability

KA 13.1–13.6 built the individual pieces of AI literacy a project controls professional needs: what AI
actually is and how it works (KA 13.1); why data quality determines whether any of it works reliably (KA

### 13.7.1 An AI maturity model for project controls: from ad hoc to optimised, with a self-assessment

**Definition & purpose.** A maturity model describes an organisation's capability in a given discipline as a
small number of ordered levels, each with observable characteristics, so a team can locate itself honestly and
see what the next level actually requires. Maturity models are well established in project management more
broadly — the Software Engineering Institute's **CMMI (Capability Maturity Model Integration)** and PMI's
**OPM3 (Organizational Project Management Maturity Model)** both use this ascending-level logic, though for
software-process and general project-management capability respectively, not AI specifically. This topic
adapts that general logic — in its own words, not reproducing either proprietary model — into a five-level AI
maturity model for a **project-controls** function, assessed across five capability dimensions.

**The underlying principle.** A maturity model is only useful if it is honest: its purpose is to expose gaps,
not to flatter. Two design choices matter. First, maturity must be assessed across **several independent
dimensions** (strategy, data, tooling, skills, governance), because an organisation can be advanced in one and
genuinely ad hoc in another — a single blended score hides that. Second, an organisation's *actual* capability
is realistically constrained by its **weakest** dimension, not its average: a team with sophisticated tooling
but no governance cannot safely rely on that tooling for anything consequential. Both views — the average and
the weakest link — are computed and reported together (Example 13.7.1), because they answer different
questions and, as the example shows, can point to different levels.

**The five levels.**

| Level | Name | Characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ad hoc / Unaware | Individuals experiment informally with whatever AI tools they can access, often ungoverned personal accounts; no organisational policy; quality and confidentiality depend entirely on the individual. |
| 2 | Aware / Experimental | The organisation is aware AI is in use; isolated champions run informal pilots; no consistent governance, funding or measurement yet. |
| 3 | Defined / Piloted | Documented use cases with success criteria; an AI-use policy exists (KA 13.6); pilots run to a scorecard and a scale/stop decision (13.7.8); governance is defined but not yet consistently applied everywhere. |
| 4 | Managed / Scaled | Multiple production use cases run across teams; roles are funded (13.7.5); benefits are tracked against projection (13.7.7); training is a standing programme, not a one-off event. |
| 5 | Optimised / Embedded | AI is a routine part of standard operating procedure, continuously monitored, governed as an operating rhythm (13.7.9) rather than a project, with benefits reinvested and the maturity assessment itself repeated as a matter of course. |

**Self-assessment rubric (formula).** For each of five dimensions — Strategy & sponsorship; Data foundations;
Tooling & process integration; Skills & roles; Governance & assurance — the function scores itself 1–5 against
descriptors anchored to the table above. Two composite readings are then taken:

`Average maturity score = (Σ dimension scores) / 5` (dimensionless, 1.0–5.0 scale)

`Weakest-link maturity level = MIN(dimension scores)` (dimensionless, 1–5, an integer level)

**Example 13.7.1 — Meridian's Month-0 AI maturity self-assessment.**

*Scenario.* Ahead of committing to an AI adoption roadmap, Meridian's project controls leadership scores its
own function against the rubric, informally, using evidence gathered over the prior month (existing AI use,
data-quality spot checks, staff interviews):

| Dimension | Evidence | Score (1–5) |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy & sponsorship | No documented AI strategy; sponsor aware but not actively driving it | 2 |
| Data foundations | Cost-coding largely consistent (KA 1.5) but several known gaps (KA 13.2) | 2 |
| Tooling & process integration | One BI platform already has AI features switched on and lightly used | 3 |
| Skills & roles | A handful of staff experiment with GenAI personally; no formal training | 2 |
| Governance & assurance | No AI-use policy; no sign-off protocol; no incident register | 1 |

*Required.* The average maturity score and the weakest-link maturity level, and an interpretation of the gap
between them.

*Working.*
`Average = (2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1) / 5 = 10 / 5 = 2.0`
`Weakest link = MIN(2, 2, 3, 2, 1) = 1`

*Answer.* **Average maturity score = 2.0 (Level 2, "Aware/Experimental"). Weakest-link level = 1 ("Ad
hoc/Unaware"), driven by Governance & assurance.**

*Interpretation.* The average reading is mildly encouraging — Meridian looks like an organisation that has
started experimenting. The weakest-link reading is the more operationally honest one: with no AI-use policy
and no sign-off protocol (KA 13.6) in place, *any* AI-assisted output Meridian currently issues carries
Level-1 risk, regardless of how capable the tooling in Tooling & process integration might be. This is the
number that should actually govern what Meridian is willing to rely on until Governance & assurance is
raised — precisely the discipline built into the adoption roadmap (13.7.2) and revisited in the Month-18
mini-case (13.7.11).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Scoring every dimension near the middle to avoid an uncomfortable finding — a maturity self-assessment that
  never produces a low score has usually not been done honestly.
- Reporting only the average, which can materially overstate real, usable capability (Example 13.7.1's Level
  2 vs Level 1 gap).
- Treating a maturity level as something purchased (buying an AI tool "to become Level 3") rather than
  earned through governance, skills and process change — a tooling purchase alone can move the Tooling
  dimension without moving any other.

**Honest AI note.** A maturity self-assessment is a structured judgement aid, not a certified audit; unlike
ISO/IEC 17024-style formal assessment schemes, there is no external body validating a project-controls AI
maturity score, and a GenAI tool asked to "assess our AI maturity" can only plausibly restate the evidence
it is given — it cannot independently verify that Meridian's governance is really absent versus merely
undocumented. The scoring, and the honesty behind it, remain the leadership team's responsibility.

### 13.7.2 Building an AI adoption strategy and roadmap

**Definition & purpose.** An **AI adoption strategy** is the sponsor-owned statement of *why* and *where* an
organisation will use AI in project controls, tied to genuine business goals (faster reporting, better
forecasts, lower rework) rather than to a generic ambition to "use more AI." An **adoption roadmap** is the
phased, resourced plan that turns that strategy into a sequence of funded work with decision gates — distinct
from a strategy in the same way a project charter (KA 8.1) is distinct from a schedule.

**The underlying principle.** Sequencing matters more than ambition. An organisation at Level 1–2 maturity
(13.7.1) that jumps straight to a Level-4 use case (e.g. autonomous forecasting with minimal review) is very
likely to fail expensively and publicly, poisoning appetite for the programme as a whole. A useful, real,
named framework for thinking about *how* adoption spreads through an organisation once it starts is Everett
Rogers' **Diffusion of Innovation** theory, which — in its own words, not Rogers' — describes adopters as
falling into successive groups (innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards) as a
new practice moves from a few enthusiasts to the mainstream. A roadmap should expect early wins to come from
willing "early adopter" teams, and plan deliberately for the harder work of bringing the early and late
majority along later (a theme developed further in 13.7.6).

**Formula/model — a four-phase roadmap structure.** `Foundation → Pilot → Scale → Embed`, each phase gated by
a sponsor decision, not by a calendar date alone (the gate concept reused from KA 8.1's stage-gate business
case discipline).

**Example 13.7.2 — Meridian's 18-month AI adoption roadmap.**

*Scenario.* Following the Example 13.7.1 self-assessment, Meridian's steering committee approves a phased
roadmap rather than an immediate, organisation-wide rollout.

| Phase | Months | Key activities | Gate decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 — Foundation | 0–3 | AI-use policy approved (KA 13.6); maturity baselined (13.7.1); use-case long-list scored (13.7.3); AI & Digital Controls Lead role appointed; tool stack rationalised (13.7.9) | Gate 0: sponsor funds the roadmap and names the two Phase-1 pilots |
| 1 — Pilot | 3–6 | Two prioritised pilots run to a scorecard (13.7.8): GenAI-assisted variance-commentary drafting; RPA-based invoice/PO matching | Gate 1: scorecard review — scale, iterate, or stop each pilot |
| 2 — Scale | 6–15 | Successful pilot(s) rolled out across the project-controls team; roles and training embedded (13.7.5); benefits tracked against projection (13.7.7); data-quality remediation begins for the two lower-feasibility "strategic bet" use cases (13.7.3, cross-ref KA 13.2) | Gate 2: annual review feeds into the Month-18 maturity re-assessment |
| 3 — Embed | 15–18+ | Governance operating rhythm fully running (13.7.9); Month-18 maturity re-assessment performed (13.7.11); a continuous-improvement backlog is opened for further use cases | Ongoing — no further "programme" gate; adoption becomes business as usual |

*Required.* Confirm the roadmap is internally consistent with the Example 13.7.1 finding, and state the
single biggest sequencing risk.

*Working/Interpretation.* Phase 0 explicitly closes Example 13.7.1's Level-1 governance gap (an AI-use
policy, KA 13.6) *before* any pilot begins — consistent with the weakest-link reading, not the more flattering
average. The biggest sequencing risk is Phase 2: scaling before the underlying data foundation for the
strategic-bet use cases is actually fixed would repeat, at larger scale, exactly the "garbage in, garbage
out" risk KA 13.2 warns against — which is why Phase 2 explicitly starts, rather than finishes, that
remediation, and why those two use cases are not scheduled for Phase 1.

- An adoption "roadmap" that is a single slide of aspirational logos and dates, with no named sponsor, budget

- Setting Phase 1 use cases without reference to the 13.7.3 prioritisation — picking whichever use case a
  vendor demonstrated most impressively rather than the one that actually scores best on value and
  feasibility.
- Treating Gate decisions as formalities that always say "continue" — a gate that has never once said "stop"
  is not really governing anything (developed further as pilot purgatory in 13.7.8).

**Honest AI note.** A GenAI tool can draft a plausible first-pass roadmap outline from a short brief — phase

risk appetite, and a draft roadmap that looks complete is not the same as one that is funded and owned. The
sponsor's approval at Gate 0 is what makes a roadmap real; an AI-drafted outline is a starting point for that
conversation, not a substitute for it.

### 13.7.3 Identifying and prioritising AI use cases: value versus feasibility and risk

**Definition & purpose.** Use-case identification is the process of surfacing candidate AI applications across
the project-controls lifecycle (the full workflow catalogue is KA 13.5's job, not this topic's — this topic
does not repeat it). Prioritisation is the disciplined act of ranking those candidates so that limited
Phase-1 capacity goes to the use cases that are actually worth it, rather than the ones that were pitched most
persuasively.

**The underlying principle.** Two independent questions must both be answered for every candidate: **how
much is it worth** (value) and **how ready are we to do it now** (feasibility — chiefly data readiness,
tooling availability, integration complexity and skills, cross-referencing KA 13.2 and KA 13.4)? A use case
can be high-value and low-feasibility (a genuine future priority, not a Phase-1 pilot) or low-value and
high-feasibility (an easy win, but not a strategic one). A third, independent axis — **risk** — must never be
folded into the same score: a high-value, high-feasibility use case can still carry consequences severe
enough (legal, commercial, safety) that it needs a governance gate before it proceeds, no matter how well it
scores. Collapsing risk into the value/feasibility score hides exactly the signal a reviewer needs to see.

**Formula/rubric.** Value (V) and Feasibility (F) are each scored 1–5 against short descriptive anchors (V:
1 = negligible impact … 5 = materially changes a core workflow's speed or quality; F: 1 = major data/tooling/
skills gaps … 5 = ready today with existing data and governed tools). Risk (R) is scored 1–5 separately
(1 = low consequence if wrong … 5 = severe commercial/legal/safety consequence if wrong).

`Priority score = V × F` (dimensionless, range 1–25)

*Quick reference — quadrants (using 4–5 = "High" and 1–3 = "Low" on each axis, a deliberately conservative
split that does not credit a mid-scale 3 as "high"):*

| | High feasibility (4–5) | Low feasibility (1–3) |
|---|---|---|
| **High value (4–5)** | Quick win | Strategic bet |
| **Low value (1–3)** | Fill-in | Reconsider/Park |

**Example 13.7.3 — scoring nine candidate AI use cases for Meridian's project-controls function.**

*Scenario.* Following the Phase-0 long-listing exercise (13.7.2), Meridian's AI & Digital Controls Lead scores
nine candidates drawn from across the lifecycle (each cross-referencing the domain it touches):

| # | Use case | V | F | V×F | R | Quadrant |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | GenAI-assisted variance-commentary drafting (KA 4.3/4.4, KA 13.5) | 4 | 4 | 16 | 2 | Quick win |
| 2 | Automated cost-code classification (KA 1.5, KA 13.2) | 3 | 4 | 12 | 2 | Fill-in |
| 3 | AI-assisted EAC/predictive forecasting (KA 6.3, KA 13.5) | 5 | 2 | 10 | 3 | Strategic bet |
| 4 | Contract clause/variation extraction (KA 7.1–7.2, KA 13.5) | 5 | 4 | 20 | 4 | Quick win — risk-gated |
| 5 | Meeting transcription & action tracking | 2 | 5 | 10 | 1 | Fill-in |
| 6 | AI schedule logic-checking (KA 10.2, KA 13.5) | 4 | 2 | 8 | 3 | Strategic bet |

*Required.* Rank the use cases and recommend Meridian's Phase-1 pilots (13.7.2).

*Working.* Ranked by priority score: #4 and #8 tie at 20; #1 at 16; #2 at 12; #3 and #5 tie at 10; #6 and #7
tie at 8; #9 at 2.

*Answer.* **#8 (RPA invoice/PO matching) and #1 (variance-commentary drafting) are recommended as the two
Phase-1 pilots** — both Quick wins with low-to-moderate risk (R = 1 and 2). **#4 (contract clause extraction)
ties for the highest raw score (20) but is risk-gated** (R = 4): its commercial and legal consequence if
wrong is too severe to pilot without first routing it through the governance review in KA 13.6 (13.7.8
develops the gate mechanism). **#3 and #6 are Strategic bets** — genuinely high value, but blocked on
feasibility (specifically data quality, KA 13.2) until Phase 2's remediation work (13.7.2) raises their
feasibility score. **#9 is explicitly parked**: low value, very low feasibility and the highest risk on the
register — a case that should be declined even if a vendor pitches it persuasively, precisely the discipline
this framework exists to enforce.

*Interpretation.* The highest-scoring use case (#4) is *not* the one that gets piloted first — a deliberately
counter-intuitive, honest result. A priority score proposes a ranking; governance still disposes of what
actually proceeds, and when.

risk tolerance, both of which depend on this organisation's specific commercial exposure, client
relationships and risk appetite (KA 12.1) — those remain strategic and governance judgements, not outputs a
model can generate reliably.

### 13.7.4 The operating model: people, process, technology, data and governance

**Definition & purpose.** An **operating model** is how an organisation's people, process, technology, data
and governance combine into a coherent, sustainable way of working — the difference between a funded pilot
that runs once and a durable capability that keeps running. Each of the five pillars is a common, widely used
lens in capability design (not unique to AI); this topic applies it specifically to an AI-augmented
project-controls function.

**The underlying principle.** The same weakest-link logic from 13.7.1 applies here at the design level: a
technology-first purchase with no matching process change, no data readiness, no defined roles and no
governance produces, at best, an underused feature nobody trusts — not a capability. The pillars must be
designed together, not sequentially bolted on after the tool is bought.

**Example 13.7.4 — Meridian's target operating model (Phase 0–2 design).**

| Pillar | Meridian's target state |
|---|---|
| People | An AI & Digital Controls Lead (0.5 FTE initially) plus a champions network — one nominated analyst per project team — accountable for training cadence and use-case backlog triage (13.7.5). |
| Process | An AI checkpoint embedded into the existing monthly EVM/forecasting reporting cycle (KA 6.1–6.3): AI drafts at a fixed point in the cycle; professional review and sign-off before issue is a mandatory step, never optional. |
| Technology | A rationalised stack: one governed, enterprise-tier general GenAI assistant for drafting/summarisation (KA 13.4); the BI platform's built-in AI features for exception detection; the scheduling platform's AI features for logic-checking. No ungoverned personal tools permitted on confidential project data (KA 13.6). |
| Data | A prioritised data-quality remediation plan (KA 13.2), starting with cost-code consistency (KA 1.5) — the binding constraint on the two Strategic-bet use cases identified in 13.7.3. |
| Governance | An AI Governance Committee (monthly cadence), an incident register, and an annual maturity re-assessment (13.7.1) built into the governance calendar (KA 13.6). |

*Required/Interpretation.* Reusing 13.7.1's five dimensions as design targets: Meridian sets a uniform target
of Level 4 ("Managed") on each dimension within 18 months — not Level 5, a deliberately realistic ambition
(developed as an honest point in 13.7.10 and tested against reality in 13.7.11).

### 13.7.5 Skills and roles: the AI-augmented project-controls professional and the human+AI division of labour

**Definition & purpose.** The **AI-augmented project-controls professional** combines core project-controls
competence — EVM, cost control, scheduling, contracts — with AI literacy: effective prompting and verification
(KA 13.3), awareness of tool categories and their limits (KA 13.4), and governance discipline (KA 13.6). This
is an augmentation of the existing professional, not a new, separate "AI specialist" role replacing domain
expertise — a distinction with real hiring and training consequences.

**The underlying principle.** For any AI-assisted task, the division of labour between AI and the human
professional should be made **explicit**, not left implicit — a concrete, task-level expression of "AI
proposes; the professional disposes" (the BoK's governing principle, stated throughout and cross-referenced
in full in KA 13.6).

**Model — a division-of-labour table.**

| Task | AI's role | Professional's role |
|---|---|---|
| Variance-commentary drafting | Generate a first-draft narrative from the EVM data set (KA 6.1.6) | Verify accuracy against the underlying numbers, add context AI cannot know, approve for issue |
| EAC forecasting | Compute a statistical EAC (KA 6.3 Method 2/3), flag anomalies | Choose the method (KA 6.3.6's diagnostic), confirm the root cause, sign off the figure |
| Cost-code classification | Suggest a code from a transaction description | Approve or correct the code, especially for ambiguous transactions (KA 1.5) |
| Contract clause extraction | Extract and tag clauses/obligations | Confirm interpretation, especially of liability/risk clauses (KA 7.1–7.2) |
| Schedule logic-checking | Flag missing links or logic errors | Confirm each flagged item and apply schedule judgement (KA 10.2) |

**Example 13.7.5 — quantifying the division of labour for variance-commentary drafting.**

*Scenario.* Before AI assistance, a Meridian project-controls analyst spends an average **6 hours per month**
manually drafting variance commentary and exception narrative across the control accounts they own. With the
Example 13.7.3 use-case #1 tool in place, the AI produces a first-full draft from the EVM data set
automatically; the analyst's remaining task is to verify, correct and approve it — measured at **1 hour per
month**.

*Required.* Hours saved per analyst per month, and the annual value across Meridian's five-person
project-controls team at a loaded rate of $70/hour.

*Working.*
`Hours saved per analyst per month = 6 − 1 = 5 hours`
`Annual hours saved (team) = 5 hours/analyst/month × 5 analysts × 12 months/year = 300 hours/year`
`Annual value = 300 hours/year × $70/hour = $21,000/year`

*Answer.* **5 hours saved per analyst per month; $21,000 per year across the team.**

*Interpretation.* This is the same time-saving figure carried forward as the steady-state benefit input to the
worked cost-benefit example in 13.7.7 — the division-of-labour redesign here is not a separate exercise from
the ROI case; it is the operational basis for it. Note, too, what the table does *not* eliminate: the 1
hour/month of verification is not overhead to be optimised away — it is the accountability step the entire
governance framework (KA 13.6) depends on.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Deskilling risk: if verification becomes rushed or habitual over time, the analyst's ability to catch a
  genuinely wrong AI draft degrades — the 1-hour review must remain a real check, not a rubber stamp.
- Hiring "AI specialists" with no project-controls domain knowledge instead of upskilling existing domain
  experts, producing tools nobody trusts because their outputs cannot be sanity-checked by their builders.
- Leaving accountability ambiguous when an AI-assisted output turns out wrong — the division-of-labour table
  only works if it is also the accountability table (cross-reference KA 13.6).

**Honest AI note.** The 5-hour/analyst/month saving in Example 13.7.5 is a genuine, measurable category of
benefit for a well-scoped drafting task — this is exactly the kind of workflow GenAI drafting tools do
reliably well (KA 13.4, KA 13.5). It is not evidence that AI can be trusted to draft *and issue* commentary
without review: the tool has no way to know whether an unusual variance reflects a genuine data error, a
scope change not yet reflected in the baseline (KA 6.1.2), or a real performance problem — that judgement is
exactly what the remaining 1 hour buys.

**Cross-references.** KA 13.3 (prompting/verification skills); KA 13.4 (tool categories); KA 13.6
(accountability and sign-off in depth); 13.7.6 (embedding new roles through change management); 13.7.7 (this
example's figures reused in the ROI case).

### 13.7.6 Change management: overcoming resistance to AI adoption

**Definition & purpose.** Change management is the discipline of moving people from a current way of working
to a desired one. For AI adoption specifically, the primary barrier is usually human, not technical — a point
this KA treats as central, not incidental.

**The underlying principle.** Resistance to AI adoption is frequently **rational**, not mere inertia: fear
that a role is being quietly eliminated, fear of being blamed for an AI error issued under one's own name,
and distrust of an output whose reasoning cannot be fully inspected are all legitimate concerns, and
addressing their root cause is more effective than mandating compliance. Two real, established
change-management models are useful here, described in their own words rather than reproduced from their
source material: **Kotter's 8-Step Change Model**, which frames change as a sequence — building urgency,
forming a coalition, shaping and communicating a vision, empowering broad action, generating visible
short-term wins, consolidating gains and anchoring the change in the organisation's culture; and Prosci's
**ADKAR model**, which frames change at the level of a single individual moving through five states —
**A**wareness of why change is needed, **D**esire to participate, **K**nowledge of how, **A**bility to do it,
and **R**einforcement to sustain it.

**Model — resistance sources and mitigations.**

| Resistance source | AI-specific manifestation | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Fear of job loss | "If AI drafts it, why do they need me?" | Make the division of labour explicit (13.7.5); communicate augmentation, not replacement, and mean it |
| Distrust of a "black-box" output | "I don't know why it said that" | Require AI outputs to show the underlying data they were drawn from; train verification skills (KA 13.3) |
| Loss of craft/ownership | A senior professional resents a tool drafting work they consider theirs | Involve senior staff in designing prompts/templates; position them explicitly as reviewers, not just approvers |
| Fear of blame | "If I sign off a bad AI output, it's my name on it" | A clear governance/sign-off protocol (KA 13.6) so accountability is understood in advance, not ambiguous after the fact |

**Mini-case — bringing a resistant senior reviewer on board with ADKAR.** A senior quantity surveyor on
Meridian's Pipeline Segment 4B team is initially resistant to the Example 13.7.3 variance-commentary tool,
viewing AI-drafted narrative as a threat to a task they see as core professional judgement. Applying ADKAR
over the 12-week Phase-1 pilot (13.7.2): **Awareness** — a team briefing explains why the tool exists and what
it does and does not do; **Desire** — an early win is shared explicitly with this reviewer: the freed time is
reallocated to deeper root-cause analysis, work they value more, not to a reduced headcount; **Knowledge** —
hands-on training in reviewing and correcting AI drafts; **Ability** — a supervised practice period with a
peer available for questions; **Reinforcement** — the reviewer's improved-quality commentary is specifically
recognised in the following performance review. Team-wide adoption tracked as follows:

| Week | 1 | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adoption (% of target group actively using the tool) | 20% | 35% | 55% | 72% | 85% |

By Week 12, adoption reaches 85% — comfortably above the 70% pass threshold used in the 13.7.8 pilot
scorecard — including the previously resistant reviewer, now an active user.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Ignoring change management entirely** — mandating AI use by policy memo alone, without addressing why
  people are actually reluctant, and being surprised when adoption stalls.
- Treating a single town hall as sufficient (skipping Reinforcement) — adoption gained in Weeks 1–9 can and

- Focusing change-management effort on frontline staff while ignoring middle-management resistance, which is
  frequently the larger blocker to sustained adoption.

**Honest AI note.** Sentiment-analysis and survey-analytics tools can help quantify adoption and resistance
signals at scale — usage logs, survey text, help-desk ticket themes — genuinely useful inputs for tracking a
curve like Fig 13.7.6's. Interpreting *why* a specific individual or team resists, and designing the human
response to it (as the mini-case above required), remains a people-leadership skill; no current AI tool
performs that judgement.

### 13.7.7 Measuring the ROI and benefits of AI adoption: a worked cost-benefit

**Definition & purpose.** Benefits realisation is the discipline of tracking whether an AI investment actually
delivered the value projected for it, using the same investment-appraisal toolkit applied to any other capital
or operating decision (cross-reference KA 8.1.2's payback/NPV/IRR treatment of Meridian's excavator-purchase
decision, reused here for notational consistency).

**The underlying principle.** Benefits split into **hard** (quantifiable in dollars — time saved, rework
avoided) and **soft** (real but not safely quantifiable — decision quality, staff morale, client perception).
Only hard benefits belong in an NPV calculation; a soft benefit forced into a dollar figure manufactures false
precision and should instead be disclosed narratively alongside the number. Costs, similarly, must include
items routinely omitted from a vendor's pitch — implementation, training, ongoing licensing, *and* the
ongoing governance-oversight time the capability requires (KA 13.6) — not just the subscription price.

**Formula(e).**

`Annual time-saving benefit ($/year) = hours saved per person per month (hr/month) × loaded rate ($/hr) × 12 (months/year) × number of people`

`ROI (%) = (Total benefit − Total cost) / Total cost × 100`

`Payback period, NPV and IRR` are calculated exactly as defined in KA 8.1.2: `Payback = full years to
recovery + (unrecovered investment at start of that year / cash flow during that year)`; `NPV = Σ [CFₜ /
(1 + r)ᵗ]`; `IRR` estimated by linear interpolation between two trial discount rates.

**Example 13.7.7 — cost-benefit for Meridian's GenAI-assisted variance-commentary tool (use case #1).**

*Scenario.* Building directly on Example 13.7.5's quantification and Meridian's established **10% hurdle rate**
(KA 8.1.2), the five-year business case for the pilot use case is assembled as follows.

*Benefits (steady state, once fully adopted):*
- Time-saving (Example 13.7.5): $21,000/year
- Rework reduction (a conservative estimate — fewer commentary re-issues for inconsistency — disclosed as an
  estimate, not a measured figure): $7,000/year
- **Total steady-state annual benefit: $28,000/year (SAR 105,000)**

*Costs:*
- One-off, Year 0: implementation & integration $15,000 + training $5,000 = **$20,000 (SAR 75,000)**
- Recurring, annual: licence/subscription $9,000 + governance-oversight time (0.1 FTE of the AI & Digital
  Controls Lead at a $85,000 loaded annual salary = $8,500) = **$17,500/year (SAR 65,625)**

*Ramp-up adjustment.* Fig 13.7.6's adoption curve shows the pilot reaching full team-wide adoption only
gradually across Year 1; Year 1 is therefore modelled at **60% of steady-state benefit**, while the recurring
cost is incurred in full regardless of adoption level:

`Year 1 net cash flow = (60% × 28,000) − 17,500 = 16,800 − 17,500 = −700`
`Years 2–5 net cash flow (each) = 28,000 − 17,500 = 10,500`

*Required.* NPV at Meridian's 10% hurdle rate, the payback period, and the IRR.

*Working — NPV.*

| Year | Net cash flow ($) | Discount factor @10% | Present value ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | (20,000) | 1.000 | (20,000) |
| 1 | (700) | 0.909 | (636) |
| 2 | 10,500 | 0.826 | 8,673 |
| 3 | 10,500 | 0.751 | 7,886 |
| 4 | 10,500 | 0.683 | 7,172 |
| 5 | 10,500 | 0.621 | 6,521 |

`Sum of PVs, Years 1–5 = (636) + 8,673 + 7,886 + 7,172 + 6,521 = 29,616`
`NPV = 29,616 − 20,000 = +9,616`

*Working — payback (undiscounted, cumulative).*
`Year 0: (20,000); Year 1: (20,000) + (700) = (20,700); Year 2: (20,700) + 10,500 = (10,200); Year 3:
(10,200) + 10,500 = +300` — recovered during Year 3.
`Unrecovered at start of Year 3 = 10,200; fraction of Year 3 needed = 10,200 / 10,500 = 0.97`
`Payback period = 2 + 0.97 = 2.97 years` (approximately 2 years, 11½ months).

*Working — IRR (by interpolation).* At 20%: PVs of $(700), $10,500×4 at factors 0.833/0.694/0.579/0.482/0.402
sum to $22,065; `NPV@20% = 22,065 − 20,000 = +2,065`. At 25%: the same cash flows at factors
0.800/0.640/0.512/0.410/0.328 sum to $19,285; `NPV@25% = 19,285 − 20,000 = −715`.
`IRR ≈ 20% + [2,065 / (2,065 + 715)] × (25% − 20%) = 20% + 0.743 × 5% = 23.7%`

*Simple 5-year ROI (undiscounted).* `Total benefit = 16,800 + (4 × 28,000) = 128,800; Total cost = 20,000 +
(5 × 17,500) = 107,500; ROI = (128,800 − 107,500) / 107,500 × 100 = 19.8%`

*Answer.* **NPV at 10% = +$9,616 (SAR 36,060); payback ≈ 2.97 years; IRR ≈ 23.7%; simple 5-year ROI ≈
19.8%.**

*Interpretation.* All four measures agree the investment is worthwhile: NPV is positive, IRR comfortably
clears the 10% hurdle rate, and payback falls within the appraisal horizon. But the case is deliberately
modest, not spectacular — a Year-1 net cash outflow, a nearly-three-year payback — which is the honest
picture for a well-scoped drafting tool with real, disclosed adoption-ramp and governance-oversight costs, not
an inflated business case built to look impressive.

**Mini-case — how sensitive is the result to adoption speed?** Suppose Meridian's adoption ramp had instead
taken two full years to reach steady state (Years 1 *and* 2 both at 60% of full benefit) rather than one:

`Year 2 net cash flow = (60% × 28,000) − 17,500 = −700` (same as Year 1)

Recomputing NPV: `PV(Year1) = (636); PV(Year2) = (700) × 0.826 = (578); PV(Years 3–5, unchanged) = 7,886 +
7,172 + 6,521 = 21,579`
`Sum of PVs = (636) + (578) + 21,579 = 20,365; NPV = 20,365 − 20,000 = +365`

*Interpretation.* Extending the ramp-up by a single additional year reduces NPV from +$9,616 to +$365 — a
fall of roughly 96%. The recurring costs (licence, governance oversight) are incurred in full regardless of
adoption speed, while the benefit only scales with actual use; a slower rollout does not merely delay value,
it erodes a disproportionate share of it, because the highest-value years are the ones pushed furthest into
the future and therefore discounted most heavily. This is precisely why 13.7.6's change-management investment
is not a "soft," optional add-on to an AI business case — it is a direct driver of the case's financial
return.

and the finance function's judgement calls, exactly as in any other investment appraisal (KA 8.1).

### 13.7.8 From pilot to scale: avoiding pilot purgatory and AI theatre

**Definition & purpose.** A **pilot** is a bounded, time-boxed test of a use case with predefined success
criteria and a pre-agreed decision gate at the end — scale, iterate, or stop. This is a genuinely different
thing from a demonstration that impresses in a meeting but never touches real data or a real workflow (**"AI
theatre"**), and different again from a pilot that is quietly extended indefinitely because no one wants to
make the stop/scale call (**"pilot purgatory"**) — two of the most common, and most avoidable, ways an AI
adoption programme fails to convert genuine early promise into real capability.

**The underlying principle.** A pilot only functions as intended if its decision gate is real: agreed *before*
the pilot starts, measured against *predefined* thresholds, and capable of producing a genuine "stop" as well
as a "scale." A pilot scorecard with no fail threshold, or a sponsor unwilling to ever say stop, is not
actually governing anything — it is theatre wearing a pilot's clothing.

**Model — pilot scorecard.**

| Criterion | Target (agreed before pilot start) | Meridian's Week-12 measured result (13.7.6 pilot) | Pass/Fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adoption rate | ≥70% of target group | 85% | Pass |
| Time saved per analyst/month | ≥4.0 hours (a conservative threshold below the 5.0-hour projection, Example 13.7.5) | 4.6 hours | Pass |
| Governance incidents (KA 13.6) | ≤1 | 0 | Pass |
| User satisfaction (survey, 1–5) | ≥3.5 | 4.1 | Pass |

**Example 13.7.8 — the Gate 1 scale decision.**

*Scenario/Required.* At the end of Meridian's 12-week Phase-1 pilot (13.7.2, 13.7.6), the AI Governance
Committee reviews the scorecard above against the pre-agreed thresholds.

*Working/Answer.* All four criteria pass. **Decision: scale** the variance-commentary tool from the single
pilot team to Meridian's full project-controls portfolio, entering Phase 2 (13.7.2).

*Interpretation.* Note the honest gap between projection and measurement: 4.6 hours saved, against the 5.0
hours originally projected in Example 13.7.5 — a real, disclosed, roughly 8% shortfall against the original
estimate, still comfortably above the 4.0-hour pass threshold. A genuine pilot scorecard reports the measured
figure, not the projected one; the actual, slightly-lower number is what should now be used to re-validate the
13.7.7 business case for the full-scale rollout, not the original projection.

- **Pilot purgatory** — a pilot with no fixed end date or fail threshold, quietly kept "running" for a year

- **AI theatre** — a polished demonstration on curated or synthetic data, presented internally or to a client

  enters a real workflow.
- Scope creep during a pilot — adding requirements mid-pilot so the success criteria become a moving target,

- Confusing "the pilot didn't scale" with "the process failed" — #4 and #9 in Example 13.7.3 were correctly

the scale/stop decision itself — that is, and must remain, a sponsor/governance decision (KA 13.6) — and a
tool tuned to maximise its own usage metrics has no particular incentive to recommend "stop," which is worth
watching for as its own subtle governance risk.

### 13.7.9 Data and tooling foundations, and embedding governance into the operating rhythm

**Definition & purpose.** Scale is only possible once the foundations underneath it are sound. This topic does
not repeat KA 13.2's treatment of data quality/lineage or KA 13.4's tool-category catalogue or KA 13.6's
governance framework — it synthesises the specific readiness checks a capability-building programme must run
before scaling, and describes how a one-off governance *policy* becomes an ongoing governance *rhythm*.

**The underlying principle.** A foundation gap discovered during a small pilot is an inconvenience; the same
gap discovered after scaling to the whole organisation is a much larger, harder-to-reverse problem. Readiness
should therefore be checked explicitly, against a short list of criteria, before every scale decision — not
assumed because the pilot happened to work.

**Model — foundation readiness checklist.**

| Criterion | Why it matters | Meridian's Month-0 status |
|---|---|---|
| Data completeness & coding consistency (KA 1.5, KA 13.2) | Blocks any use case needing reliable historical data | Amber — cost-code inconsistency is the binding constraint on the two Strategic-bet use cases (Example 13.7.3, #3 and #6) |
| Integration/API availability with source systems | Determines whether an AI output plugs into the real workflow or stays a manual copy-paste exercise | Green for the ERP/BI stack; Amber for the scheduling platform |
| Tool rationalisation vs sprawl (KA 13.4) | Overlapping subscriptions raise cost and dilute governance oversight | Red at Month 0 (see mini-case below); rationalised to the three governed tools in Example 13.7.4 by Month 3 |
| Security, confidentiality & data residency terms (KA 13.6) | Determines whether project-confidential data may lawfully and safely be used with a given tool at all | Cleared for the three rationalised tools before Phase 1 pilots began |
| Licensing & cost model | Per-seat vs consumption pricing changes the economics of scale (13.7.7) | Per-seat licensing adopted for predictable budgeting |

**Mini-case — finding shadow AI.** During the Phase 0 readiness review, Meridian's AI & Digital Controls Lead
discovers **three ungoverned personal GenAI accounts** already in informal use by staff for project-related
drafting — a live example of "shadow AI": tools adopted informally, outside any governance review, potentially
already exposing confidential project data (cross-reference KA 13.6). All three are brought inside the
governed, enterprise-tier stack (Example 13.7.4) by Month 3, closing the gap before it could compound at
scale.

**Governance operating rhythm.** The AI Governance Committee (13.7.4) meets monthly to review the incident
register, the current pilot/scale scorecards (13.7.8), and — annually, not as a one-off exercise — re-run the
maturity self-assessment (13.7.1), first repeated at Month 18 (13.7.11). A policy document filed away and
never revisited is not governance; the recurring calendar slot is what makes it one.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Tool sprawl and shadow AI, as found in the mini-case above — a genuine, common, and easily overlooked risk.
- Treating the AI-use policy (KA 13.6) as a document to be signed once, rather than an operating rhythm with
  a standing agenda item and an incident register that is actually reviewed.
- Skipping the annual maturity re-assessment once initial programme momentum fades — the single biggest risk
  to sustaining Level 4–5 maturity over time.

**Honest AI note.** Software-asset-management and data-loss-prevention-style discovery tools can help surface
ungoverned ("shadow") AI use across an organisation's network — this is, in practice, how findings like the
mini-case's three accounts are actually made. The tool only detects usage; it does not decide the rationalised
target stack, and it cannot itself fix the data-quality constraint such a discovery often reveals. Both remain
governance and data-management decisions for the professional and the AI Governance Committee.

**Cross-references.** KA 13.2 (data foundations in depth); KA 13.4 (tool categories in depth); KA 13.6
(governance framework in depth); 13.7.1 (the periodic re-assessment this rhythm sustains); 13.7.3 (the
feasibility scores this foundation work is meant to raise); 13.7.4 (the technology pillar this checklist
verifies).

### 13.7.10 The future outlook: an honest, non-hype view

**Definition & purpose.** Any statement about "what AI can do" dates quickly, in both directions — some
claimed limits get overcome, and some hyped capabilities fail to materialise as marketed. This topic states,
explicitly and without over-claiming, what looks likely to keep improving, what looks likely to stay genuinely
hard, and what remains honestly uncertain, at the time of writing (2026).

**The underlying principle.** Gartner's **Hype Cycle** is a well-known, real, named way of describing a
common pattern: an emerging technology is typically over-hyped early (a "peak of inflated expectations"),
then under-appreciated once early over-promising is exposed (a "trough of disillusionment"), before more
modest, genuinely productive use settles in over time (a "plateau of productivity") — described here in
general terms, not reproducing Gartner's proprietary graphic or specific placements. The practical discipline
this suggests is straightforward: treat both extremes — "this changes everything, immediately" and "this is
just hype, ignore it" — with equal scepticism, and keep testing actual capability against actual use cases
(13.7.3) rather than against either narrative.

**What plausibly keeps improving.** Integration depth into existing PM/ERP/scheduling tools (reducing manual
copy-paste); the cost and accessibility of GenAI capability; the quality of extraction and summarisation
tasks; and, actively but unevenly, "agentic" systems capable of carrying out multi-step tasks with less
continuous human prompting at each step.

**What looks likely to stay genuinely hard.** Data quality remains the persistent bottleneck (KA 13.2) — no
model improvement fixes a badly coded cost ledger. Contextual professional judgement — knowing that an
unusual variance reflects a site condition observed yesterday, or that a clause's commercial intent differs
from its literal wording — is not close to being replicated. Accountability for a decision cannot, by
construction, be delegated to a tool (KA 13.6) regardless of how capable it becomes.

**Genuinely uncertain — flagged as such.** Agentic AI — systems that not only draft a commentary but might
also post it, or not only flag a contract risk but escalate or act on it with reduced human-in-the-loop
review — is an active, fast-moving development area. Whether, and under what safeguards, this is appropriate
for assurance-critical project-controls tasks (a signed-off forecast, a contractual position) is genuinely
unresolved at the time of writing, and how professional-standards and audit expectations will eventually treat
AI-assisted work is likewise still evolving. Neither should be treated as settled in either direction —
neither "it will obviously be fine" nor "it will obviously never be permitted."

**Mini-case — staying current without chasing hype.** Meridian adds a standing five-to-ten-minute
"horizon-scanning" item to the monthly AI Governance Committee agenda (13.7.9) to note emerging capabilities
and tool releases. Critically, a new capability discovered this way does **not** bypass the 13.7.3 value/
feasibility scoring process — it simply joins the long-list for the next prioritisation round. This
deliberately prevents FOMO-driven procurement of whatever tool is newest, while still keeping the function
aware of what is changing.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Chasing every new tool release outside the normal prioritisation process, driven by fear of missing out
  rather than by an actual value/feasibility case.
- The opposite failure: dismissing a genuine, mature advance because an earlier, over-hyped version of a
  similar idea disappointed — the "trough of disillusionment" swinging too far the other way.
- Presenting this KA's specific tool-capability claims, or any vendor's roadmap promises, as settled fact
  rather than a snapshot that should be periodically re-tested (13.7.1, 13.7.9).

**Honest AI note.** This is itself the meta-honesty point of the whole KA: every specific claim about current
AI capability made across KA 13.1–13.7 reflects tool capability as observed at the time of writing and should
be expected to date — which is exactly why 13.7.1's and 13.7.9's periodic re-assessment discipline exists. It
tests this chapter's assumptions against current reality; it does not treat this chapter as a permanent
statement of what AI can and cannot do.

### 13.7.11 Capability-building mini-case: Meridian's AI-augmented project-controls function, 18 months on

At Month 0, Meridian's project-controls function scored an average AI maturity of 2.0 and a weakest-link level
of 1, driven by an absent governance framework (Example 13.7.1). Eighteen months later, following the roadmap
in Example 13.7.2, the AI Governance Committee re-runs the same five-dimension self-assessment as a matter of
standing practice (13.7.9), not a special exercise:

| Dimension | Month 0 | Month-18 target | Month-18 actual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategy & sponsorship | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| Data foundations | 2 | 4 | 3 |
| Tooling & process integration | 3 | 4 | 4 |
| Skills & roles | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| Governance & assurance | 1 | 4 | 4 |

`Average = (4 + 3 + 4 + 4 + 4) / 5 = 19 / 5 = 3.8` → rounds to **Level 4 ("Managed/Scaled")**
`Weakest link = MIN(4, 3, 4, 4, 4) = 3` → **Level 3 ("Defined/Piloted")**, driven by Data foundations

Four of the five dimensions hit their target exactly; Governance & assurance, the Month-0 weakest link,
improved the most — a direct result of the AI Governance Committee's monthly cadence and the incident register
(13.7.9) becoming genuinely embedded, not just documented. Data foundations, by contrast, fell one point short
of its target: the cost-code remediation work begun in Phase 2 (13.7.2) reduced, but did not eliminate, the
inconsistency identified in Example 13.7.1, and it remains the constraint holding the two Strategic-bet use
cases (Example 13.7.3, #3 and #6) below full production readiness. This is reported honestly, not smoothed
over — an organisation that has genuinely reached Level 4 on four dimensions but not yet on the fifth is a
believable, creditable outcome; a report claiming uniform Level 4 or 5 across the board after eighteen months
would be the less believable one.

Along the way: the two Phase-1 pilots (Example 13.7.3, #1 and #8) both passed their scorecards (13.7.8) and
scaled; the variance-commentary tool's measured benefit ($19,320/year, from Example 13.7.8's 4.6 hours/
analyst/month) came in roughly 8% below its original projection ($21,000/year, Example 13.7.5) — a modest,
disclosed shortfall, not a failure, and the figure now used to re-validate the ongoing business case (13.7.7)
rather than the original estimate. Use case #4 (contract clause extraction) completed its governance risk
review (KA 13.6) and entered its own pilot in Month 9. Use case #9 (fully autonomous forecasting with no human
review) remains parked, and was reconsidered and declined again at the Month-12 committee review when a
vendor proposed it a second time — the framework holding under a second round of pressure, not just the
first.

**Interpretation.** Eighteen months of disciplined work moved Meridian from a Level-1, ungoverned starting
point to a genuine, largely Level-4 capability — real progress, honestly measured, with one clearly named
remaining constraint and a live plan (Phase 2's continuing data remediation) to close it. Consistent with
13.7.10's honest outlook, this is not "Optimised" (Level 5): that is typically a multi-year journey, not an
18-month one, and a claim to have reached it this quickly would itself be a signal to distrust, not celebrate.

**Common pitfalls (closing).**
- Declaring victory at Month 18 and disbanding the governance rhythm that produced the result — maturity is
  sustained by the operating rhythm (13.7.9), not by the milestone report that describes it.
- Treating the Month-18 assessment as a finish line rather than the next of many periodic checkpoints.

**Honest AI note.** Even a well-executed, well-resourced 18-month capability-building programme, of the kind
modelled throughout this KA, plausibly lands at "Managed," not "Optimised" — and the single most persistent
constraint (data quality) is, unsurprisingly, the one this entire BoK has repeatedly identified as the
foundation everything else depends on (KA 13.2). That consistency, across a fictional but carefully worked
case, is itself the point: there is no shortcut around data quality, no matter how capable the AI layer above
it becomes.

**Cross-references.** Every prior topic in this KA (13.7.1–13.7.10); KA 13.1–13.6 (the individual literacies
this capability was built on); KA 1.5, KA 13.2 (the data-foundation constraint still open at Month 18).

### Key terms

- **AI maturity model** — a five-level description (Ad hoc/Unaware → Aware/Experimental → Defined/Piloted →
  Managed/Scaled → Optimised/Embedded) of an organisation's AI capability, assessed across several
  dimensions.
- **Weakest-link principle** — the view that real, usable capability is constrained by an organisation's
  lowest-scoring dimension, not its average.
- **Composite/average maturity score** — the simple mean of dimension scores; a different, and sometimes more
  flattering, reading than the weakest-link level.
- **AI adoption strategy** — the sponsor-owned statement of why and where an organisation will use AI.
- **Adoption roadmap** — the phased, gated, resourced plan turning that strategy into funded work.
- **Diffusion of Innovation** — Rogers' theory describing adoption spreading through successive adopter
  groups (innovators, early adopters, early/late majority, laggards).
- **Value/feasibility prioritisation matrix** — a 2×2 framework scoring candidate AI use cases on value and
  feasibility (each 1–5), independent of a separately scored risk rating.
- **Priority score (V × F)** — the product of value and feasibility scores, dimensionless, range 1–25.
- **Risk-gating** — routing a high-scoring but high-consequence use case through governance review before it
  proceeds, regardless of its priority score.
- **Quick win / Strategic bet / Fill-in / Reconsider-Park** — the four quadrants of the value/feasibility
  matrix.
- **Operating model** — the combination of people, process, technology, data and governance that sustains a
  capability.
- **AI-augmented project-controls professional** — a domain expert with added AI literacy, not a separate
  "AI specialist" role.
- **Human+AI division of labour** — an explicit, task-level statement of what the AI does and what the
  professional does, operationalising "AI proposes; the professional disposes."
- **Kotter's 8-Step Change Model** — a sequential change framework (urgency, coalition, vision, communication,
  empowerment, short-term wins, consolidation, culture).
- **ADKAR** — Prosci's individual-level change model: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement.
- **Benefits realisation** — tracking whether an AI investment delivered its projected value.
- **Hard vs soft benefits** — quantifiable dollar benefits versus real but not safely quantifiable ones.
- **ROI, payback period, NPV, IRR** — investment-appraisal measures (cross-reference KA 8.1.2), applied here
  to an AI initiative.
- **Pilot** — a bounded, time-boxed use-case test with predefined success criteria and a decision gate.
- **Pilot purgatory** — a pilot kept running indefinitely with no stop/scale decision ever made.
- **AI theatre** — an impressive demonstration that never enters a real, production workflow.
- **Shadow AI** — ungoverned personal AI tool use, outside any policy or review.
- **Tool sprawl** — multiple overlapping, ungoverned AI subscriptions across an organisation.
- **Gartner Hype Cycle** — a named, real framework describing the typical over-hype-then-under-appreciation
  pattern of an emerging technology.
- **Agentic AI** — AI systems capable of carrying out multi-step tasks with reduced human-in-the-loop review;
  flagged in this KA as an active, genuinely unresolved area for assurance-critical use.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 13.7.1 [topic 13.7.1, level: application — numerical]**
Meridian's Month-0 self-assessment scores five AI-maturity dimensions: Strategy & sponsorship 2, Data
foundations 2, Tooling & process integration 3, Skills & roles 2, Governance & assurance 1 (each 1–5). What
is the composite (average) maturity score?
A. 1.8
B. **2.0** ✔
C. 2.2
D. 3.0
*Rationale:* Sum = 2+2+3+2+1 = 10; average = 10/5 = 2.0. A and C are plausible arithmetic slips; D mistakes
the highest individual score for the average.

**MCQ 13.7.2 [topic 13.7.1, level: analysis]**
Using the same five scores, applying the weakest-link principle instead of the average, which maturity level
actually constrains Meridian's capability, and why?
A. Level 2, because that is the average
B. Level 3, because Tooling & process integration is the highest-scoring dimension
C. **Level 1, because Governance & assurance, the lowest-scoring dimension, gates what the organisation can safely rely on** ✔
D. Level 5, because at least one dimension will eventually reach that level
*Rationale:* The weakest-link principle holds that real capability is constrained by the weakest dimension —
an organisation with strong tooling but ad hoc governance cannot safely rely on it for consequential outputs.
A describes a different measure; B and D misapply the principle.

**MCQ 13.7.3 [topic 13.7.3, level: application — numerical]**
A candidate AI use case is scored Value = 5, Feasibility = 4 (each 1–5). Using the priority score
(Value × Feasibility) and the convention that 4–5 is "High" and 1–3 is "Low" on each axis, what is its score
and quadrant?
A. 9; Fill-in
B. **20; Quick win (High value, High feasibility)** ✔
C. 1; Reconsider/Park
D. 20; Strategic bet
*Rationale:* `5 × 4 = 20`; both axes score in the "High" band, placing it in the Quick-win quadrant. A adds
instead of multiplying; C inverts the scores; D misclassifies a High/High result as a Strategic bet
(High value/Low feasibility).

**MCQ 13.7.4 [topic 13.7.3, level: analysis]**
A use case ties for the highest priority score in the set but also carries the highest risk rating on the
register. What does the KA 13.7.3 framework recommend?
A. Automatically deprioritise it, since high risk always outweighs a high score
B. Approve it for immediate full-scale rollout, since the priority score already reflects everything relevant
C. **Route it through a governance risk review before piloting or scaling, even though its value/feasibility score is strong** ✔
D. Average its risk score into the priority score to produce a single ranking
*Rationale:* the priority score and the risk rating answer different questions; a strong score proposes a use
case for attention, but a high risk rating means governance must dispose of it through review before it
proceeds. A and B each collapse a two-part judgement into one axis; D would hide the risk signal a reviewer
needs to see.

**MCQ 13.7.5 [topic 13.7.6, level: recall]**
Which pair correctly names two real, established change-management models referenced in this KA for building
an AI-adoption capability?
A. SWOT analysis and the Ansoff Matrix
B. **Kotter's 8-Step Change Model and Prosci's ADKAR model** ✔
C. CMMI and PMI's OPM3
D. The Agile Manifesto and the Scrum Guide
*Rationale:* Kotter's 8-Step Change Model and ADKAR are the two change-management models named in 13.7.6. C
names two real maturity models (used in 13.7.1 for a different purpose); D names agile frameworks (Domain 9),
not change-management models.

**MCQ 13.7.6 [topic 13.7.7, level: application — numerical]**
An AI initiative has a one-off Year-0 cost of $20,000. Cumulative undiscounted cash flow is −$10,200 at the
end of Year 2, and Year-3 cash flow is $10,500. What is the payback period, to two decimal places?
A. 2.00 years
B. **2.97 years** ✔
C. 3.00 years
D. 1.97 years
*Rationale:* `Payback = 2 + (10,200 / 10,500) = 2 + 0.97 = 2.97 years`. A ignores the fractional year; C
rounds up rather than computing the fraction precisely; D miscalculates the fraction.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Explain, in your own words, why an organisation's average maturity score and its weakest-link maturity
   level can point to two different levels, and why the weakest-link view usually matters more for
   governance-sensitive AI use cases.
2. Using Meridian's Month-18 dimension scores (Strategy 4, Data 3, Tooling 4, Skills 4, Governance 4),
   calculate the average maturity score and the weakest-link level.
3. A colleague proposes measuring an AI pilot's success solely by its user-adoption rate. Using the 13.7.8
   pilot scorecard, explain why adoption rate alone is an insufficient scale/stop criterion.
4. Using the 13.7.7 sensitivity mini-case, explain in one or two sentences why extending the adoption
   ramp-up by a single additional year had such a disproportionate effect on the project's NPV.

**Answers**

1. The average blends all dimensions into one figure and can look reassuring even when one dimension is
   badly underdeveloped; the weakest-link view exposes that underdeveloped dimension directly. For
   governance-sensitive use cases, the risk an organisation is actually exposed to is set by its weakest
   safeguard (typically governance itself), not by how advanced its best dimension happens to be — so the
   weakest-link reading is the more operationally honest one to act on.
2. `Average = (4 + 3 + 4 + 4 + 4) / 5 = 19 / 5 = 3.8`, rounding to Level 4. `Weakest link = MIN(4, 3, 4, 4,
   4) = 3` (Data foundations), i.e. Level 3. This matches Example 13.7.11 exactly.
3. High adoption of a tool that is producing poor-quality, ungoverned, or unverified outputs is not success —
   it may simply mean a flawed capability is now in wider use. The scorecard also requires the benefit
   actually achieved (time saved), governance incidents, and user satisfaction to pass, so a scale decision
   reflects real, measured value and safety, not usage volume alone.
4. The recurring costs (licence, governance oversight) are incurred in full regardless of how quickly
   adoption ramps up, while benefit only scales with actual use; a slower ramp therefore produces more
   near-term loss-making years, and because later cash flows are discounted more heavily, pushing the
   high-value years further into the future erodes a disproportionate share of the project's NPV rather than
   merely delaying it.
