# Domain 13 — AI for Project Controls & Project Management: Concepts, Tools & Practice

## KA 13.6 — Governance, ethics, risk & assurance of AI

Every earlier Knowledge Area in this domain has shown AI *doing* something useful for a project controls
professional — drafting a variance commentary, extracting data from a contract, suggesting a probability
band for a risk, recommending an EAC method. This Knowledge Area is not about a new capability. It is about
the discipline that makes every one of those capabilities safe to use, defensible to an auditor, and
answerable to a client, a regulator or a court: **governance**. "AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable" has
run through this book as a single-sentence discipline; KA 13.6 is where that discipline becomes an
**operating model** — a policy, a register, a risk-tiering method, defined roles, change control, monitoring
and an incident-response process — rather than a slogan repeated in every "AI in this topic" box. Everything
in this KA is the **governance backbone** those boxes assumed was there.

This KA does not repeat two domains it depends on. **Domain 12** (Risk Management) already supplies the
generic risk process — ISO 31000's identification, analysis, response and monitoring cycle (KA 12.1, KA
12.2) and the funded contingency this process produces (KA 12.3). This KA does not re-teach that process; it
applies it to a specific, AI-shaped risk vocabulary (hallucination, drift, data leakage and the rest,
sections 13.6.2–13.6.3) and shows how AI use cases get *identified, scored and treated* using the same underlying
discipline. **Domain 11** (Business Process Cycles) already supplies the control vocabulary this KA leans on
throughout — preventive/detective/corrective controls (KA 11.3.3), an authorisation matrix and reconciliation
(KA 11.3.4), segregation of duties (KA 11.3.5), compensating controls (KA 11.3.6) and, especially, the audit

ideas look like when the "transaction" being controlled is an AI-generated output rather than a purchase

both.

### 13.6.1 Why AI governance matters: accountability and auditability

**Definition & purpose.** AI governance, for a project controls function, is the set of policies, roles,
processes and controls that ensure every use of AI in the function's work is identified, risk-assessed,
appropriately overseen by a human, and traceable after the fact to who used it, for what, and who checked and
approved the result. It is not a separate discipline bolted onto project controls — it is the same
accountability and control discipline this entire book teaches (Domain 11's control environment, Domain 12's
risk process), applied to a new class of tool.

**The underlying principle.** Two features of AI make governance necessary in a way that, say, a calculator
or a spreadsheet formula never required. First, **AI output is probabilistic, not deterministic**: the same
prompt against the same model can produce different wording, different emphasis, or — in a hallucination — a
different, unsupported fact each time it is run, so an AI output cannot be trusted simply because "the tool
produced it," the way a correctly-written SUM() formula can be. Second, **AI outputs look authoritative
regardless of whether they are correct** — fluent, confident, well-formatted prose carries no visible marker
of the underlying uncertainty a statistical model actually has about its own answer. Together, these two
features mean that responsibility for an AI-assisted output can quietly and invisibly drift away from the
named professional who is supposed to hold it, unless governance actively holds it in place. This is exactly
what "AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable" is a discipline *against*: without a register, a tiering method,
a human-oversight requirement and an audit trail, the "disposal" step becomes optional in practice even where
it remains mandatory in policy.

**Accountability, in practice.** Accountability for an AI-assisted project-controls output never transfers to
the AI tool or its vendor. If an AI-drafted EAC commentary is issued to a client and is later found to be
wrong, the professional who signed it off — not the AI tool, and not the analyst who drafted it without
sign-off — is the one who answers for it, in exactly the way a professional who signs a report built on a
junior colleague's draft answers for that report today. Governance does not change *where* accountability
sits; it makes that pre-existing accountability practically enforceable by documenting who used AI, for what,
under what oversight, and who approved the result before it left the building.

**Auditability, in practice.** An auditable AI-assisted process is one where, months or years later, an
internal auditor, an external auditor, a regulator or a court can reconstruct: which AI tool produced a given
draft or figure; what data was given to it; what the human reviewer checked, changed or rejected; and who
signed off the final output and when. Without this trail, an organisation cannot distinguish a properly
governed AI-assisted output from one that was rubber-stamped — the same distinction Domain 11 draws between a
control that is *designed* and one that is actually *operated* (KA 11.3.7). The remainder of this KA builds
the specific mechanisms — the AI use register (section 13.6.6), the risk tiering (section 13.6.7), the sign-off practice
(section 13.6.9) — that make an AI-assisted process auditable in this sense.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating AI governance as an IT or information-security matter alone, disconnected from the project controls
  function's own accountability for the numbers it reports — the two overlap but are not the same thing
  (section 13.6.6 separates the roles).
- Assuming a vendor's marketing claim about a tool's accuracy or safety substitutes for the organisation's own
  risk assessment of *its specific use case* (section 13.6.7) — a generally capable tool can still be misapplied to a
  use case it was never evaluated for.
- Waiting for a governance failure before building a register or a policy — reactive governance, built only
  after an incident (section 13.6.11), costs materially more than a proportionate policy built in advance.

**AI in this topic.** Governance, risk and compliance (GRC) platforms are beginning to add AI-specific modules
that can inventory which AI tools are in use across an organisation's network (from browser and API traffic)
and flag likely unregistered ("shadow") AI use (cross-reference section 13.6.6, section 13.6.8) — a genuine visibility gain
over relying on staff to self-report. Such a tool can tell an organisation *that* an unregistered AI tool is
being used; it cannot itself decide whether that use is acceptable, assign it a risk tier, or design the
compensating response — those remain the accountable governance owner's decisions, consistent with **"AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable"** applied here to the governance function itself.

reuses); KA 12.1 (risk framework, applied to AI-specific risk from section 13.6.2); section 13.6.6 (turning this principle
into a policy and a register); section 13.6.9 (the sign-off practice that operationalises accountability).

### 13.6.2 The AI risk taxonomy I — hallucination, bias & fairness, and model drift

**Definition & purpose.** Before any use case can be risk-tiered (section 13.6.7) or controlled, a project controls
professional needs a working vocabulary for *what specifically can go wrong* with an AI output. This topic and
section 13.6.3 build that vocabulary across seven named risk types. This topic covers the three that arise from how a
model was built and how it generates: **hallucination**, **bias and fairness**, and **drift**.

**Hallucination.** A hallucination is a confident, fluent, plausible-sounding output that is factually wrong —
a fabricated figure, a citation to a document or clause that does not exist, or a calculation error dressed in
correct-looking working. It is not a rare glitch; it is a structural property of how large language models
generate text — predicting a plausible next word given everything before it, with no built-in mechanism that
distinguishes "a fact I can verify" from "a fact that merely sounds right in context." A hallucination is most
dangerous precisely where it is least visible: a fabricated clause reference reads exactly like a real one
until someone checks the source document.

**Rubric — a source-traceability check.** Every material factual claim or figure in an AI output can be scored
against three yes/no questions: (1) *Is this claim traceable to a specific, checkable source* (the input data
provided, a named and verifiable document, or a source the reviewer can independently open)? (2) *Has the
number, if any, been independently recomputed or reconciled against the source data*, not merely visually
checked? (3) *Is the claim stated with a level of certainty the underlying evidence actually supports* (a
single data point presented as a confirmed trend is a certainty failure even if the data point itself is
real)? An output failing any one of the three should not be used unverified; section 13.6.9 builds this rubric into a
full assurance checklist.

**Bias and fairness.** An AI model trained on historical data reproduces the patterns in that data — including
patterns that unfairly disadvantage a group the model was never intended to discriminate against. In a
project-controls setting this most commonly arises in **vendor and subcontractor scoring**: a model trained on
Meridian's historical procurement data might learn that suppliers with a long registered relationship history
score better on a risk model, not because newer suppliers perform worse, but because the training data simply
contains more track record for incumbents — a proxy that quietly disadvantages newer, smaller or minority-owned
subcontractors bidding for the first time, without the model, or an inattentive reviewer, ever seeing "supplier
tenure" flagged as a fairness-sensitive variable. Fairness risk is not limited to protected-characteristic
discrimination in the legal sense; any systematic, unjustified disadvantage baked into historical data and
reproduced by a model is a governance concern.

**Drift.** A model's accuracy can degrade over time, or in a new context, without any visible change in how
confidently it presents its output — this is **drift**. A cost/productivity forecasting tool trained on
historical trenching data from Meridian's typical geography can drift when applied to Pipeline Segment 4B's
ground conditions if those conditions differ materially from the training data (cross-reference KA 6.3's
Method 2 EAC caution that a "stable, representative" CPI needs a genuine track record, not a single reading —
the same logic applies to a model's training data being representative of the situation it is now being asked
to forecast). Drift is easy to miss because nothing about the tool's interface changes; only its accuracy does.

**Example 13.6.1 — a hallucinated clause reference in an AI-drafted contract summary.** *Scenario.* A Meridian
commercial analyst asks a GenAI assistant to summarise a lengthy subcontract for Pipeline Segment 4B's WP2 pipe
and valve package, specifically the retention and defects-liability terms. The draft summary states: "per
clause 14.3, retention is released in two tranches: 50% at Taking-Over and 50% at the end of the 12-month
Defects Notification Period." *Required.* Apply the source-traceability rubric above. *Working/Answer.* On
review against the actual signed subcontract, no clause 14.3 exists — the document's retention clause is
numbered 11.2, and it specifies a single-tranche release at the end of the Defects Notification Period only,
with no interim release at Taking-Over. The AI's output fails question (1) (the clause number is fabricated)
and materially misstates the commercial term itself. *Interpretation.* The fabricated clause number is the
visible failure signal here — a genuine clause reference the analyst could check in seconds — but the more
consequential error is the substantive one (a two-tranche release that does not exist), which a reviewer who
only skimmed for "does this look like a normal retention clause" would very plausibly have missed, because it
does look normal. This is why the rubric requires checking the *source document* directly, not merely
assessing whether the AI's prose is internally plausible.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assuming a hallucination will always be an obviously absurd claim — the most dangerous hallucinations are
  plausible, in-register, and wrong only on the specific fact that matters.
- Treating "the AI cited a source" as proof the citation is real or says what the AI claims it says — the
  citation itself can be fabricated or misquoted.
- Assuming bias is only a legal or HR concern — a biased vendor-scoring or resourcing recommendation is a
  project-controls governance failure with commercial and reputational consequences of its own.
- Trusting a forecasting tool's historical accuracy indefinitely, without periodically re-validating it against
  current conditions (section 13.6.8 builds this into the monitoring cadence).

**AI in this topic.** Model-evaluation and "AI red-teaming" tools can systematically probe a model for known
hallucination patterns or bias in its outputs before it is deployed for a given use case (cross-reference
section 13.6.7's pre-deployment tiering), and some enterprise AI platforms now offer built-in citation-verification
features that check a generated citation against the source corpus automatically. These tools improve the
odds of catching a problem before a human reviewer does — they do not eliminate the need for the reviewer,
because both hallucination and bias can appear in patterns a prior evaluation did not anticipate, particularly
on a specific, novel use case the evaluation never tested.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.3 (Method 2's "stable, representative" trend assumption — the same logic applies to
drift); KA 11.3.4 (three-way match as a non-AI analogue to source-traceability verification); section 13.6.9 (the
full assurance checklist this rubric feeds); section 13.6.3 (the remaining four risk types).

### 13.6.3 The AI risk taxonomy II — confidentiality, security, over-reliance, and IP & legal exposure

**Definition & purpose.** This topic completes the seven-item risk taxonomy begun in section 13.6.2, covering the four
risk types that arise not from how a model was built, but from **how it is used**: data leakage and
confidentiality, security (including prompt injection at awareness level), over-reliance and automation bias,
and intellectual-property and legal exposure.

**Data leakage and confidentiality.** Pasting confidential project, commercial or client data into a **public,
consumer-grade AI tool** — one with no signed data-processing agreement, no contractual no-training/
no-retention commitment, and standard consumer terms that may permit the vendor to retain or use submitted
text — risks that confidential data leaving the organisation's control entirely, with no practical way to
retrieve or delete it afterwards. This is categorically different from using a **governed, enterprise-licensed**
AI tool procured with an explicit contractual commitment on data handling (section 13.6.6, section 13.6.10). The distinction
is not "AI versus no AI" — it is "governed versus ungoverned," and it is the single most common real-world AI
governance failure in professional services generally. Section 13.6.11's mini-case walks through exactly this failure
at Meridian in full.

**Security, including prompt injection (awareness level).** Beyond conventional data-security risk (an AI
vendor's own systems being breached, credential compromise), AI systems introduce a risk category specific to
how large language models process instructions: a **prompt injection** occurs when text the AI processes —
not from its authorised user, but embedded within a document, email, web page or other data the AI has been
asked to read or summarise — contains hidden instructions designed to manipulate the AI's behaviour. Because a
language model does not reliably distinguish "an instruction from the person operating me" from "text I am
merely reading," a maliciously (or even accidentally) worded passage inside a subcontractor's claim narrative,
for example, could cause a document-summarisation tool to omit, reframe or misrepresent content in a way the
human user never intended or authorised. This is stated here strictly at **awareness level**: the practical
implication for a project controls professional is not to understand the underlying mechanism in technical
depth, but to know that AI tools processing **externally sourced, untrusted documents** (a subcontractor's
submission, an unsolicited tender, an email attachment) carry this risk, and that such tools should never be
given autonomous authority to act (approve, release payment, send correspondence) without a human confirming
the output first — exactly the human-in-the-loop requirement built into section 13.6.7's tiering.

**Over-reliance and automation bias.** Automation bias is the well-documented human tendency to trust an
automated system's output *more* than warranted, and to apply progressively less scrutiny to it over time as
familiarity and trust build — a professional who diligently checked an AI tool's first ten outputs may, by the
hundredth, be skimming rather than verifying. This is a governance risk in its own right, independent of
whether any individual output is actually wrong: a review process that has quietly stopped reviewing provides
no real assurance, in exactly the sense KA 11.3.7 distinguishes a control's *design* from whether it is
actually *operated*. Section 13.6.9's worked example shows this risk materialising concretely.

**IP and legal exposure.** Two distinct exposures arise. First, an AI tool's output may reproduce, closely
paraphrase, or be derived from copyrighted third-party material (a competitor's proprietary methodology, a
licensed technical specification) in a way that creates infringement risk if used in Meridian's own deliverable
without review. Second, the **ownership and confidentiality status of AI-generated content itself** is often
contractually and legally unsettled — whether a client's contract, or an AI vendor's own terms of service,
grants Meridian full ownership and confidentiality protection over an AI-assisted work product is a question
that varies by vendor, by contract, and by jurisdiction, and should never be assumed favourably without
checking the applicable terms (cross-reference section 13.6.10).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating "our staff know not to paste confidential data into AI tools" as a control, when no technical
  enforcement or monitoring exists behind the awareness message (section 13.6.11's root-cause analysis finds exactly
  this gap).
- Feeding an externally sourced document (a subcontractor claim, an unsolicited email) into an AI summarisation
  tool and then acting on its output without independently reading the source document at least once.
- Assuming automation bias only affects careless staff — it is a documented cognitive pattern that affects
  diligent professionals too, which is why section 13.6.7's tiering assigns *mandatory*, not discretionary, oversight
  requirements to higher-impact use cases.
- Assuming an organisation automatically owns the output of a public AI tool the same way it owns a staff
  member's own drafting — vendor terms vary and must be checked, not assumed.

**AI in this topic.** Data-loss-prevention (DLP) tools, already common in mature IT security stacks, can be
configured to detect and block confidential data patterns (project cost codes, subcontractor pricing
schedules) from being pasted into recognised public AI tool domains — a genuine, largely automatable technical
control for the confidentiality risk described above (cross-reference section 13.6.11). Such tooling cannot assess
*whether* a specific piece of text is actually confidential in a given context (some data is only sensitive
before a certain date, or in combination with other data) — that judgement, and the policy the DLP rule
enforces, remains a professional and legal decision, not a tool-generated one.

**Cross-references.** section 13.6.2 (the first three risk types); KA 2.4, KA 7.1 (contractual confidentiality
provisions this risk threatens); KA 11.3.5 (segregation of duties — prompt injection is, in effect, a new way
an "authorise" function can be manipulated); section 13.6.7 (tiering that assigns oversight proportionate to these

### 13.6.4 Ethical principles for AI in project controls

**Definition & purpose.** Beneath the specific risk types in sections 13.6.2–13.6.3 sit five recurring ethical
principles that international guidance on trustworthy AI converges on repeatedly (and which this KA draws out
explicitly because they recur, in different words, across every framework named in section 13.6.5): **transparency**,
**fairness**, **accountability**, **human oversight** and **privacy**. These are not abstract values —  each
has a direct, practical translation into how a project controls function should use AI.

**The underlying principle, and its translation into practice.**

| Principle | What it means | Practical translation for project controls |
|---|---|---|
| Transparency | Those affected by an AI-assisted output can know that AI was involved and, at a reasonable level, how | Disclose AI involvement where material (client reports, contractual deliverables — section 13.6.10); do not present an AI-drafted commentary as if it were produced by unaided professional judgement |
| Fairness | AI outputs do not systematically disadvantage individuals or groups without justification | Screen AI-assisted scoring or ranking tools (vendor selection, resourcing) for proxy variables that could reproduce historical bias (section 13.6.2) |
| Accountability | A named, qualified human remains answerable for the output, regardless of AI involvement | Every AI-assisted output that leaves the function carries a named sign-off (section 13.6.9) — never "the AI generated this" as an explanation on its own |
| Human oversight | A human retains a meaningful, not token, ability to review and override the AI's output before it has effect | Oversight proportionate to risk tier (section 13.6.7); oversight that is procedurally present but never actually exercised (automation bias, section 13.6.3) does not satisfy this principle |
| Privacy | Personal and confidential data processed by AI is handled lawfully, proportionately and only as necessary | Governed tools only for confidential/personal data (section 13.6.3, section 13.6.6); data minimisation — do not paste more than the AI task actually requires |

**Example 13.6.2 — applying the five principles to one use case.** *Scenario.* Meridian is evaluating whether
to let an AI tool auto-rank subcontractor bids for WP2's replacement supply agreement (following the material
price-escalation risk response in KA 12.2.7) using a weighted score of price, past performance and delivery
reliability. *Required.* Apply the five principles as a pre-deployment checklist. *Working/Answer.*
**Transparency** — bidders are told an AI-assisted scoring tool is used, and can request the scoring criteria.
**Fairness** — the "past performance" variable is checked for proxy bias against newer bidders with a shorter
Meridian track record (section 13.6.2's exact risk), and a manual override path exists for a bidder scored low
primarily on tenure. **Accountability** — the Regional Commercial Manager, not the tool, signs the final
ranking recommendation forwarded for approval. **Human oversight** — the tool produces a ranked shortlist, not
an automatic award; a human reviews all scored bids, not only the AI's top choice, before a decision is made.
**Privacy** — only bid data relevant to scoring is processed, not bidders' full company financial records.
*Interpretation.* None of the five principles individually blocks using the tool — together, they define the
*conditions* under which its use is acceptable, which is exactly what section 13.6.7's tiering turns into an
assignable, auditable control set.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating these as compliance boxes to tick once, rather than conditions that must hold every time the tool is
  actually used — a fairness review performed at pilot stage does not remain valid indefinitely as the tool's
  training data or use pattern changes (section 13.6.8).
- Assuming "human oversight" is satisfied by a human's name being on the approval, without that human having
  genuinely reviewed the substance (automation bias, section 13.6.3).
- Applying these principles only to client-facing AI use and not to internal tools (resourcing, performance
  scoring) that affect people just as materially.

**AI in this topic.** Some enterprise AI governance platforms now include "ethics checklist" or "responsible AI
assessment" workflow modules that prompt a use-case owner through questions resembling the table above before
a tool is approved. Such a workflow is a useful structuring aid and a record of the assessment having taken
place — it cannot itself judge whether an answer given is honest or complete; that remains the accountable
reviewer's responsibility, and a checklist completed without genuine engagement is no more meaningful here than
an unread exception report is as a control (cross-reference KA 11.3.3).

**Cross-references.** section 13.6.5 (the frameworks these five principles are drawn from); section 13.6.7 (turning
principles into an assignable risk tier and control set); KA 11.3.1 (COSO's control-environment "tone at the
top," the organisational precondition for these principles to mean anything in practice).

### 13.6.5 Governance frameworks at awareness level — ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI RMF, the OECD AI Principles and the EU AI Act's risk tiers

**Definition & purpose.** A project controls professional does not need to be a specialist in AI regulation,
but should recognise, at awareness level, the small number of internationally significant reference points
that an organisation's AI governance policy will typically be built on, mapped to, or benchmarked against.
Four are named here, each described in this book's own words — never as a verbatim extract, and never with a
fabricated clause or article number.

**ISO/IEC 42001.** Published in 2023, ISO/IEC 42001 is the first international standard specifying
requirements for an organisation's **AI management system (AIMS)**. It follows the same general management-
system logic used by other ISO standards familiar to project controls professionals from a quality or
information-security context (such as ISO 9001 or ISO/IEC 27001): leadership commitment and a documented
policy; planning that identifies and assesses AI-specific risks and the impacts an AI system may have on
individuals and groups; defined roles, responsibilities and competence; operational controls across the AI
system's lifecycle (from initial design and data sourcing through development, deployment and monitoring); and
a continuing cycle of performance evaluation and improvement. Organisations can seek external certification
against it, in the same way many seek certification against ISO 9001 or ISO/IEC 27001. For a project controls
function, ISO/IEC 42001 is best understood as the closest analogue to "what does a properly run AI governance
programme look like, end to end" — and section 13.6.6's operating model is deliberately built along the same broad
lines (policy, roles, lifecycle controls, monitoring, improvement), without claiming certification-level
compliance in itself.

**The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF).** Published in January 2023 by the United States' National
Institute of Standards and Technology, the NIST AI RMF is a voluntary framework (not a certification standard)
organised around four core functions, commonly summarised as: **Govern** — cultivating an organisational
culture and structure of AI risk management, including policy, roles and accountability; **Map** —
understanding the context an AI system will operate in and identifying the risks specific to that context and
use case (closely analogous to section 13.6.7's tiering exercise); **Measure** — using quantitative and qualitative
methods to analyse, assess, benchmark and track identified AI risks over time; and **Manage** — allocating
resources to the risks identified and prioritised, and responding to them (a response vocabulary that maps
closely to KA 12.2.7's mitigate/transfer/avoid/accept threat responses, applied here to AI risk specifically).
The NIST AI RMF is widely referenced internationally as a practical, non-mandatory reference architecture even
outside the United States.

**The OECD AI Principles.** First adopted in 2019 by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation
and Development and updated in 2024, these were the first intergovernmental standard on AI, adhered to by OECD
members and a number of non-member countries. At a summary level, they set out five values-based principles
for trustworthy AI: AI should benefit people and the planet by driving inclusive growth, sustainable
development and well-being; it should respect the rule of law, human rights and democratic values, including
fairness; it should be transparent and allow those affected to understand and, where appropriate, challenge its
outcomes; it should be robust, secure and safe throughout its lifecycle, with risks continually assessed and
managed; and organisations and individuals developing or operating AI systems should be accountable for their
proper functioning. These map closely to section 13.6.4's five practical principles; the OECD's are the
intergovernmental-policy articulation of essentially the same values.

**The EU AI Act's risk-tiering.** The European Union's AI Act takes a **risk-based regulatory approach**,
classifying AI systems into tiers with different obligations attached: **unacceptable risk** — a narrow set of
AI practices considered to carry unacceptable risk to safety or fundamental rights, which are prohibited
outright; **high-risk** — AI systems used in specified high-stakes contexts (the Act specifies particular
sectors and use cases; a project controls professional should treat "high-risk" as an EU legal-regulatory
classification requiring specialist legal advice to apply correctly, not a term this book redefines), which
must meet requirements including risk management, data governance, technical documentation, human oversight and
conformity assessment before use; **limited risk** — systems subject to transparency obligations, such as
informing a person that they are interacting with an AI system or that content has been AI-generated; and
**minimal or no risk** — the large majority of AI applications, which fall outside these specific obligations.
The Act has extraterritorial relevance: an organisation based outside the EU (as Meridian is) can still be
affected where its AI system's output is placed on the EU market or used in a way that affects people in the
EU — relevant to any professional working on an EU-linked programme, an EU-domiciled client, or an EU-based AI
vendor, even from a Gulf or other non-EU base.

**A note on this section, and on Meridian's own risk tiers.** These four references describe **external**
frameworks at awareness level; none of them is reproduced verbatim, and none should be treated as legal advice
— an organisation's actual regulatory exposure depends on its jurisdiction, sector, client base and the
specific AI systems used, and must be assessed with qualified legal counsel. Section 13.6.7's risk-tiering rubric is
Meridian's own **internal governance policy**, inspired by the same risk-based logic these frameworks share
(the more consequential and less-overseen a use case, the stricter the required controls) but it is not itself
a claim of legal compliance with any of the four.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating framework names as interchangeable — ISO/IEC 42001 is a certifiable management-system standard, the
  NIST AI RMF is a voluntary risk framework, the OECD AI Principles are intergovernmental policy values, and
  the EU AI Act is binding law within its jurisdictional scope; conflating "voluntary framework" with "law"
  materially misstates an organisation's actual obligations.
- Assuming a framework followed at awareness level substitutes for jurisdiction-specific legal advice on actual
  regulatory obligations (section 13.6.10 returns to this).
- Assuming these frameworks are static — AI regulation and standards are an actively evolving area; a policy
  built today should be reviewed on a fixed cadence, not treated as permanently settled (section 13.6.8).

**AI in this topic.** Regulatory-tracking and compliance-mapping tools can monitor for updates to named
frameworks and flag when a policy document may need review — a genuine time-saving aid for keeping a governance
programme current. Such tools cannot themselves determine whether a specific Meridian use case is compliant
with a specific legal obligation; that determination is a legal judgement, made by qualified counsel, not
generated by a compliance-tracking tool.

section 13.6.4 (the five ethical principles these frameworks converge on); section 13.6.6 (the operating model built along
similar lines to ISO/IEC 42001, without claiming certified compliance); section 13.6.10 (regulatory and contractual
considerations in practice).

### 13.6.6 The AI governance operating model — policy, roles and the AI use register

**Definition & purpose.** An AI governance **operating model** is the concrete set of artefacts and
responsibilities that turns the principles and frameworks of sections 13.6.1–13.6.5 into something a project controls
function actually runs day to day. Three components anchor it: a written **AI use policy**, clearly assigned
**roles** (reusing, not duplicating, KA 11.3.7's Three Lines Model), and a living **AI use register** — the AI
governance equivalent of KA 12.2's risk register: the single artefact every subsequent topic in this KA reads
from and writes to.

**The AI use policy.** A short, accessible policy statement covering, at minimum: which categories of AI tool
are pre-approved for general use (a governed enterprise assistant with a no-training/no-retention data
commitment), which are prohibited outright (any public, consumer-grade tool for anything involving Meridian
project, client or confidential data), the mandatory step of registering any new AI use case before deployment
(below), and the escalation path for a suspected incident (section 13.6.8, section 13.6.11). The policy is deliberately short
enough that staff can actually read and recall it — a lengthy document nobody reads provides no more real
governance than an unread exception report (cross-reference KA 11.3.3, KA 11.3.7).

**Roles, reusing the Three Lines Model (KA 11.3.7).** The **first line** is the project controls professional
or function proposing and operating a given AI use case day to day — accountable for using it within its
approved tier and escalating anything unusual. The **second line** is the **AI Governance Owner** (at Meridian,
the Head of Project Controls) who owns the policy and the register, chairs risk-tiering decisions (section 13.6.7),
and works alongside Information Security (data handling, vendor contract terms) and Legal (regulatory and
contractual exposure, section 13.6.10) as specialist second-line functions in their own right. The **third line** is
**Internal Audit**, which periodically tests — exactly as KA 11.3.7 tests a purchase-order control — whether
the AI governance operating model is actually *operating* as designed, not merely documented.

**The AI use register.** Every AI use case, before deployment, is logged with: a unique ID; the business
purpose; the tool and vendor (and whether it is governed/enterprise or public/consumer-grade); the categories
of data it will process, with an explicit yes/no field for *"does this use case involve confidential,
client-owned or third-party-confidential data?"*; the assigned owner; the risk tier and controls assigned
(section 13.6.7); the human-oversight requirement (section 13.6.8); and the date of last review. The register is reviewed on
a fixed cadence (section 13.6.8) and updated immediately on any material change to the tool, its prompt/configuration,
or its use — a **change** that is itself risk-tiered again, not assumed to inherit its original approval
indefinitely (section 13.6.8).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Building a register that captures *that* an AI tool is used but not the data-sensitivity field — the single
  most important field for catching a confidentiality risk before deployment, as section 13.6.11's mini-case shows in
  its absence.
- Assigning the AI Governance Owner role to Information Security alone — data handling and technical security
  are essential inputs, but the risk-tiering judgement (does this use case's *purpose* and *impact* warrant this
  tier) is a project-controls and business judgement, not a purely technical one.
- Treating the register as a one-off deployment gate rather than a living document reviewed on a cadence
  (section 13.6.8) — exactly the risk-register pitfall KA 12.2.9 warns against, applied here to AI.

**AI in this topic.** As noted in section 13.6.1, network-monitoring tools can help populate and validate the register
by detecting AI tool traffic an organisation did not know was occurring — a genuinely useful discovery aid
against "shadow AI." The register's substantive content (purpose, data sensitivity, tier, control assignment)
still requires the proposing professional's honest input and the AI Governance Owner's judgement; a
technically-detected tool with no declared purpose or owner is a finding requiring investigation, not an entry
the tool can complete on its own.

### 13.6.7 Risk-tiering AI use cases and assigning controls — a worked example

**Definition & purpose.** Risk-tiering is the process of scoring a proposed or existing AI use case against
two axes — the **impact** if its output were wrong and used unreviewed, and the **degree of human oversight**
built into how it is actually used — and assigning it to one of four governance tiers, each with a defined,
proportionate set of mandatory controls. It is the operational heart of the operating model in section 13.6.6, and it
is deliberately built on the same risk-based logic named generically in every framework in section 13.6.5, without
claiming to be a compliance mapping to any one of them.

**The underlying principle.** Not every AI use case warrants the same governance weight, any more than every
identified project risk warrants full quantitative treatment (KA 12.2.3's qualitative-screening funnel is the
direct analogue). A use case that only ever informs a first draft a human fully reviews before anyone else sees
it (full human review) carries materially less residual risk than the same tool's output being acted on with no
review at all (autonomous use) — and a use case whose wrong output would only affect an internal, informal note
carries less impact than one that feeds a client-facing commitment or a contractual position. Impact and
oversight together, not either alone, determine the proportionate response.

**Rubric — the impact × oversight tiering grid.** Impact is scored on four bands (Low: informational only, no
decision consequence; Medium: feeds an internal report or working decision; High: feeds a cost, schedule,
commercial or client-facing position; Critical: feeds a contractual, financial-statement, legal or
confidentiality-sensitive commitment). Oversight is scored on four bands (Full human pre-use review; Spot-check
of a sample; Light/exception-only review; Autonomous — no human review before use or effect). The two axes
combine, as a lookup grid rather than a multiplied score, into four tiers:

| Impact ↓ / Oversight → | Full review | Spot-check | Light review | Autonomous |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Tier 1 | Tier 1 | Tier 2 | Tier 2 |
| Medium | Tier 1 | Tier 2 | Tier 2 | Tier 3 |
| High | Tier 2 | Tier 3 | Tier 3 | Tier 4 |
| Critical | Tier 3 | Tier 4 | Tier 4 | Tier 4 |

**Tier definitions and controls.** **Tier 1 (Minimal governance)** — logged in the register; no special
approval beyond the function head. **Tier 2 (Standard governance)** — register entry reviewed by the AI
Governance Owner; mandatory documented human review before the output is used; periodic (quarterly) register
re-check. **Tier 3 (Enhanced governance)** — mandatory human-in-the-loop review using a documented checklist
(section 13.6.9); dual sign-off (preparer and an independent reviewer); AI Governance Owner and Information Security
approval before deployment; monthly monitoring. **Tier 4 (Not permitted without executive risk acceptance)** —
deployment blocked by default; requires an explicit, documented risk-acceptance decision by Meridian's
Executive Risk Committee before any pilot, with the same Tier 3 controls as a minimum floor if ever approved.

**Example 13.6.3 — risk-tiering six live AI use cases at Meridian.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's AI Governance Owner reviews six AI use cases proposed or already informally in use
across the Pipeline Segment 4B commercial and controls team, and applies the tiering grid to each.

*Required.* The impact band, oversight band and resulting tier for each, and the controls that follow.

*Working.*

| ID | Use case | Impact | Oversight (as currently used) | Tier | Assigned controls |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI-1 | GenAI drafting of an internal Month-5 variance commentary (KA 4.4), reviewed and edited by the controls manager before any internal issue | Medium | Full review | **Tier 1** | Register only; no special approval |
| AI-2 | AI-assisted EAC method recommendation and client-facing commentary draft from EVM data (KA 6.3) | High | Light review (analyst currently checks arithmetic only, section 13.6.9's finding) | **Tier 3** | Mandatory assurance checklist (section 13.6.9) + dual sign-off before any client issue |
| AI-3 | Document-intelligence three-way-match exception flagging (KA 11.3.4) — flags, does not release payment | Medium | Spot-check of flagged exceptions | **Tier 2** | Documented review of flagged items; quarterly register re-check |
| AI-4 | AI meeting-transcription/summarisation used during WP2 claims negotiation (KA 7.2) | High (confidentiality-sensitive even though not a "decision" output) | Light review (transcript auto-shared to the team unchecked) | **Tier 3** | Governed/enterprise tool only; confidentiality-specific review before circulation; dual sign-off on the summary |
| AI-5 | Public, consumer-grade GenAI chatbot used informally by a Regional Commercial Manager to draft commercial correspondence referencing project pricing (unregistered "shadow AI") | Critical (uncontrolled external data exposure, no data-processing agreement) | Autonomous (informal individual use, no organisational visibility) | **Tier 4** | **Not permitted** — blocked pending Executive Risk Committee review; section 13.6.11's mini-case is this use case's incident history |
| AI-6 | AI coding assistant drafting a cost-report automation script, tested and reviewed by an analyst before running against live data | Medium | Full review | **Tier 1** | Register only; code review remains mandatory practice regardless of tier |

*Answer.* Two use cases (AI-1, AI-6) require only baseline registration; two (AI-3, and — once reformed —
AI-4) sit at intermediate tiers with proportionate periodic review; one (AI-2) is escalated to mandatory
dual-sign-off governance despite already being informally in use, because its *current* oversight (light
review) does not match its *actual* impact (high); and one (AI-5) is not permitted at all under its current,
ungoverned use pattern.

*Interpretation.* The exercise's most consequential finding is not AI-5 (already known, informally, to be
risky) but **AI-2**: a use case already in routine use, whose oversight had never been consciously matched to
its actual impact. This is precisely the value risk-tiering adds beyond intuition — it forces an explicit,
comparable judgement across every use case rather than relying on which ones happen to feel risky.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Tiering the *tool* rather than the *use case* — the same underlying AI assistant can sit at Tier 1 for one
  use (an internal draft) and Tier 3 for another (a client-facing figure) depending entirely on impact and
  oversight, not on which product is used.
- Scoring "oversight" as whatever the policy *says* should happen rather than what actually happens in
  practice — AI-2's finding depends on honestly assessing current behaviour, not the aspirational process.
- Treating Tier 1 as "ungoverned" rather than "minimally governed" — even a Tier 1 use case remains in the
  register and subject to the periodic review cadence in section 13.6.8.

**AI in this topic.** As with section 13.6.1, workflow tools can help route a newly registered use case through the
tiering questionnaire and pre-populate a likely impact/oversight score from similar past entries, saving
administrative time. The actual scoring judgement — particularly the "impact" axis, which depends on
understanding what the use case's output actually feeds into — is a project-controls judgement a generic
workflow tool cannot make reliably on its own, and every tier assignment is reviewed and confirmed by the AI
Governance Owner before it is relied upon.

### 13.6.8 Human oversight, model/prompt change control and monitoring in operation

**Definition & purpose.** Tiering (section 13.6.7) assigns a control set at a point in time. This topic covers what
keeps that assignment valid as time passes: enforcing genuine (not token) **human-in-the-loop** oversight,
**change control** when a tool, its prompt, its configuration, or its underlying model changes, and ongoing
**monitoring** for drift, misuse or a use case's risk profile changing without anyone re-tiering it.

**Human-in-the-loop, made genuine rather than token.** A human-in-the-loop requirement is only as strong as the
reviewer's actual engagement with it (section 13.6.3, section 13.6.4's automation-bias caution). Two practical design choices
strengthen it: requiring the reviewer to **actively confirm specific checked items** (a structured checklist,
section 13.6.9, rather than a single "approved" checkbox) rather than a single blanket sign-off, and, for Tier 3 use
cases, requiring **two** reviewers (preparer and an independent second reviewer) so that one person's fatigue or
familiarity does not become the entire control.

**Model and prompt change control.** An AI tool's behaviour can change without any action by Meridian at all —
a vendor updates the underlying model, or Meridian's own configured prompt or instructions are edited by a
well-meaning user trying to improve an output. Either event is, in governance terms, a **new use case**, not a
continuation of the old one: it should trigger a re-tiering (Fig 13.6.6's loop back to node 3) rather than
silently inheriting the original approval. In practice this means the AI use register logs the specific
prompt/configuration version in use, and any material change to it — a materiality threshold set by the AI
Governance Owner, since not every trivial wording tweak needs a full re-tiering — resets the review clock.

**Monitoring cadence.** Every registered use case is reviewed on a cadence proportionate to its tier: Tier 1
annually; Tier 2 quarterly; Tier 3 monthly (aligned with Meridian's existing management-reporting cadence, KA
4.3, KA 8.4); Tier 4, if ever approved, continuously. A monitoring review asks the same four questions KA
12.2.9 asks of a risk register, translated to AI use: *is the assigned oversight actually being performed*
(not merely documented); *has the tool, prompt or its accuracy changed* since the last review (drift, section 13.6.2);
*has a new, unregistered use of an existing tool emerged*; and *has an incident or near-miss occurred* that
was not escalated through section 13.6.11's process.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Setting a monitoring cadence and never actually running it — exactly the "unread exception report" failure
  mode KA 11.3.3 and KA 11.3.7 warn against, transplanted to AI governance.
- Treating a vendor's routine, automatic model update as outside Meridian's control and therefore not requiring
  re-tiering — the update is outside Meridian's control, but its *effect* on a specific use case's risk profile
  is exactly what re-tiering exists to catch.
- Measuring "is the human-in-the-loop control working" only by whether sign-offs are being recorded, rather than
  periodically sampling the *substance* of what was actually checked (an audit-style test of operating
  effectiveness, KA 11.3.7, not merely of design).

**AI in this topic.** Usage-analytics features built into many enterprise AI platforms can report how often a
tool's output is edited, rejected or accepted unchanged by its human reviewers — a genuinely useful proxy signal
for whether review is substantive (frequent, substantial edits) or has drifted toward automation bias
(near-universal unchanged acceptance, a pattern worth investigating even though it is not proof of a problem on
its own). Such analytics describe *behaviour patterns*, not the *correctness* of any individual output, and a
low edit rate is consistent both with excellent AI performance and with a reviewer who has stopped genuinely
checking — distinguishing the two remains the AI Governance Owner's and Internal Audit's job, not the
analytics dashboard's.

**Cross-references.** KA 11.3.3 (preventive/detective controls — monitoring here is a detective control over
the preventive tiering decision); KA 12.2.9 (the risk-monitoring cadence this topic's cadence mirrors); section 13.6.6
(the register this monitoring updates); section 13.6.9 (the checklist that makes review substantive rather than
token).

### 13.6.9 Assurance and auditability of AI outputs — the evidence trail and the professional's sign-off

**Definition & purpose.** This topic supplies the practical assurance discipline every higher-tier AI use case
(section 13.6.7) requires: a structured **AI-output assurance checklist**, applied consistently, that converts "a human
looked at it" into a defensible, auditable statement of *what* was checked, *against what evidence*, and *who*
takes responsibility for the result. It is the AI-specific expression of KA 11.3.7's test-of-design/test-of-
operating-effectiveness distinction, and of the "evidence trail" this whole KA has referred to throughout.

**The underlying principle.** An AI output's assurance is not established by the output *looking* right — that
is precisely the failure mode section 13.6.1 and section 13.6.2 describe. It is established by an independent, documented
check against **ground truth**: the actual source data, the actual site or contractual facts, and — critically
— any material fact the AI's output may simply not have been given or have access to. The checklist below is
built for exactly that purpose, and Example 13.6.4 applies it to a case where the AI's arithmetic is entirely
correct and its conclusion is nonetheless wrong, because it was never given a fact that changes which method
is defensible.

**The AI-output assurance checklist.**

| # | Check | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Source data verified | Was the data given to the AI tool itself correct, current and complete? |
| 2 | Arithmetic independently verified | Has every number in the output been independently recomputed, not merely read? |
| 3 | Assumption stated and verified | Does the output state which assumption it relies on, and has that assumption been checked against ground truth the AI may not have had access to? |
| 4 | Material omissions checked | Does the output address every fact a knowledgeable reviewer would expect it to address, or does it omit something material? |
| 5 | Certainty calibration | Does the output's language claim more certainty than the underlying evidence supports? |
| 6 | Confidentiality | Was any confidential or third-party data used in a governed tool only (section 13.6.6), consistent with the AI use register entry? |
| 7 | Sign-off | Who is the named, accountable professional approving what is actually reported — and does the trail show what they changed or rejected? |

**Example 13.6.4 — applying the assurance checklist to an AI-drafted Pipeline Segment 4B EAC.**

*Scenario.* At the Month-5 data date (KA 6.3.2–6.3.4), a Meridian project controls analyst pastes Pipeline
Segment 4B's Month-5 cumulative EVM figures — BAC $600,000; PV $540,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250; CPI 0.80;
SPI 0.75 — into Meridian's governed enterprise GenAI assistant and asks it to recommend an EAC method and draft
a one-paragraph client-ready commentary. The assistant returns: *"Recommended EAC: Method 1, EAC = AC + (BAC −
EV) = $506,250 + $195,000 = $701,250. The cost variance to date reflects an isolated, non-recurring issue, and
the project is expected to converge back toward the original budget as remaining work proceeds at planned
rates."* This is use case **AI-2** from Example 13.6.3, tiered at Tier 3 following its oversight-versus-impact
mismatch.

*Required.* Apply the seven-point assurance checklist, and state what should actually be reported.

*Working.*

1. **Source data verified** — PASS. The BAC/PV/EV/AC figures match the current, reconciled Month-5 cost report
   (KA 6.3.2).
2. **Arithmetic independently verified** — PASS. `AC + (BAC − EV) = 506,250 + (600,000 − 405,000) = 506,250 +
   195,000 = 701,250`, recomputed and correct. The AI has not hallucinated a number here.
3. **Assumption stated and verified** — **FAIL**. Method 1 explicitly assumes the variance is a one-off, resolved
   cause (KA 6.3.2). The Month-5 schedule update on record (KA 6.3.4) confirms the shortfall lies on
   activities feeding the critical path and that recovery will require paid acceleration — a fact directly
   contradicting a "resolved, non-recurring" characterisation, and a fact the AI tool, given only the four EVM
   figures, was never provided.
4. **Material omissions checked** — **FAIL**. The draft commentary makes no mention of SPI (0.75) or the
   schedule position at all, despite schedule pressure being directly relevant to which EAC method is
   defensible (KA 6.3.4).
5. **Certainty calibration** — **FAIL**. "Expected to converge back toward the original budget" states a
   confident recovery narrative the evidence does not support once the critical-path finding is considered.
6. **Confidentiality** — PASS. The governed enterprise tool was used, consistent with the AI-2 register entry
   (Tier 3, section 13.6.7).
7. **Sign-off** — the analyst's initial sign-off, based only on checks 1–2, would have passed the AI's Method 1
   recommendation forward for client issue. Applying the full checklist, the reviewing Project Controls Manager
   rejects the AI's method recommendation.

*Answer.* Of seven checks, **three fail** (3, 4, 5) despite the arithmetic itself being entirely correct. The
Project Controls Manager instead reports **Method 3's EAC of $831,250** (KA 6.3.4: `AC + (BAC − EV)/(CPI × SPI)
= 506,250 + 195,000/0.60 = 506,250 + 325,000 = 831,250`), the method whose assumption — that both cost and
schedule pressure continue into the remaining, critical-path work — matches the confirmed schedule-update
evidence. The AI's draft, the checklist results, and the human override with its rationale are all retained in
the AI use register's evidence trail for this reporting cycle.

*Interpretation.* This is the central lesson of assurance, stated concretely: the AI did not hallucinate a
number (check 2 passed cleanly) and did not leak confidential data (check 6 passed) — it produced a
**mathematically correct answer to an incompletely specified question**, because it was never given the
schedule-update fact that determines which assumption is defensible. A reviewer who checks only arithmetic, as
the analyst's initial review did, would issue a materially optimistic EAC to a client. The checklist exists
precisely to catch what "does the maths check out" alone cannot.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Equating "arithmetic is correct" with "output is safe to use" — Example 13.6.4 is built specifically to show
  these are different questions.
- Applying the checklist mechanically without engaging with check 3 and 4's substance, which require the
  reviewer to know facts the AI's input did not contain — no checklist substitutes for the reviewer's own
  domain knowledge.
- Discarding the AI's draft entirely rather than retaining it in the evidence trail — the point of the trail is
  to show *what was checked and corrected*, which requires keeping the original alongside the correction.

**AI in this topic.** Some AI platforms can be configured to prompt the user for missing context before
generating a forecast-type output (for example, asking "is there a current schedule update relevant to this
data?" before recommending an EAC method) — a genuine, if still developing, mitigation against exactly the
omission in Example 13.6.4. Such a prompt increases the odds relevant context is supplied; it does not
guarantee the user provides an honest or complete answer, and the checklist above remains the control of
record regardless of how the tool is configured.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.3.2–6.3.4 (the three EAC methods and the Month-5 figures this example reuses in
full); KA 11.3.7 (test of design vs operating effectiveness, the audit-assurance logic this checklist applies);
section 13.6.2 (the source-traceability rubric this checklist extends); section 13.6.7 (AI-2's tiering, which mandated this
checklist in the first place).

### 13.6.10 Regulatory and contractual considerations, and when not to use AI

**Definition & purpose.** This topic closes the governance-design half of the KA with two practical questions
every project controls function must answer, beyond internal policy: what regulatory and contractual
obligations actually bind a specific AI use, and — the honest counterpart to every capability described
elsewhere in Domain 13 — when the right answer is simply **not to use AI at all**.

**Regulatory considerations.** section 13.6.5 named four international reference points; none of them is a substitute
for jurisdiction-specific legal advice. A project controls professional's practical obligation is narrower and
achievable without legal training: know that AI-specific regulation exists and is evolving, know which of the
named frameworks might plausibly apply given the organisation's markets and clients (an EU-linked programme,
for instance, brings EU AI Act relevance even for a Gulf-based contractor, per section 13.6.5), and escalate to Legal
*before* deploying a materially new AI use case rather than after — exactly the point at which section 13.6.7's Tier 3
and Tier 4 approvals already require legal input.

**Contractual considerations.** Two contractual questions recur specifically in project controls work. First,
**client and employer confidentiality clauses** (cross-reference KA 7.1, KA 7.2) very commonly restrict how
project and commercial data may be shared with third parties — and an AI vendor processing that data, even
briefly, is a third party for this purpose unless the contract with the vendor and the underlying client
contract both permit it. Second, **contract clauses may require or restrict disclosure of AI use itself** — an
increasing number of client contracts and tender conditions now ask a contractor to disclose whether, and how,
AI is used in preparing deliverables; failing to check for such a clause, and inadvertently breaching it, is
itself a contractual exposure distinct from any error in the AI's output.

**When not to use AI.** Three situations warrant a default of **not using AI at all**, rather than using it
with heavier controls: (1) where no governed tool exists for a genuine business need and the alternative is
informal, ungoverned ("shadow") use — the gap should be closed by procuring a governed tool, not filled by
tolerating ungoverned use (section 13.6.11's root cause); (2) where a use case would require processing data a
contract explicitly prohibits sharing with any third party, regardless of that party's data-handling
commitments; and (3) where the decision or output is safety-critical, or otherwise sits in a domain (a Tier 4
use case, section 13.6.7) for which Meridian's Executive Risk Committee has not granted risk acceptance — silence or
delay in that approval means "not yet," not "proceed cautiously."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assuming a governed enterprise AI tool's own vendor contract automatically flows down every confidentiality
  obligation Meridian owes to its own clients — the two contracts must be checked against each other, not
  assumed compatible.
- Treating "we checked the regulatory frameworks" as sufficient without also checking the specific client and
  employer contract terms that may be stricter than any general regulation.
- Filling a genuine capability gap with tolerated shadow AI rather than escalating the gap to the AI Governance
  Owner as a resourcing/procurement need (section 13.6.11's central lesson).

**AI in this topic.** Contract-analytics tools (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 7.2) can scan a contract for
confidentiality and AI-disclosure clauses faster than a manual read-through, flagging relevant terms for
review. They cannot determine whether a *specific proposed AI use* falls within or outside a flagged clause's
scope — that interpretation is a legal judgement, and, consistent with every other tool described in this KA,
the flagged clause is a starting point for Legal's review, not a cleared-or-blocked determination in itself.

**Cross-references.** section 13.6.5 (the frameworks this topic operationalises); KA 7.1, KA 7.2 (contractual
confidentiality and risk allocation this topic depends on); section 13.6.7 (Tier 4's executive-approval gate); section 13.6.11
(the mini-case this topic's "when not to use AI" guidance would have prevented).

### 13.6.11 Governance mini-case — an ungoverned AI disclosure on Pipeline Segment 4B

**Definition & purpose.** This mini-case closes the KA by walking a single governance failure — the AI-5 use
case flagged as "not permitted" in Example 13.6.3 — through a full incident-response cycle: detection,
containment, impact assessment, notification, root-cause analysis, corrective action and the lessons-learned
update to the AI use register. It is deliberately built around confidentiality and data leakage (section 13.6.3)
rather than hallucination, since section 13.6.9 already worked a hallucination-adjacent failure in full; a governance
programme has to be ready for more than one risk type at once.

**Mini-case — the incident.** Two weeks after a difficult negotiation over Pipeline Segment 4B's WP2 pipe and
valve replacement supply agreement (KA 12.2.7), Meridian's Regional Commercial Manager — the same role that
correctly applies Fig 11.3.4's authorisation matrix elsewhere in this book — is drafting a same-day commercial
email to a prospective alternative supplier. Working under time pressure, the manager pastes an extract of
Pipeline Segment 4B's current unit rates and the incumbent subcontractor's confidential quoted pricing into a
free, public consumer-grade GenAI chatbot, asking it to "rewrite this more persuasively." The chatbot is not a
Meridian-governed tool: it carries no signed data-processing agreement with Meridian, and its standard consumer
terms permit the vendor to retain submitted text. The pricing data is confidential under Meridian's supply
agreement with the incumbent subcontractor (KA 7.1). This is Example 13.6.3's AI-5 in its actual, unregistered,
Tier-4 state.

**Detection.** A colleague copied on the resulting email notices phrasing that reads like generic AI-generated
marketing copy and asks about it informally. Separately and independently, Meridian's Information Security
function — running its routine review of network egress logs (section 13.6.8's monitoring cadence) — flags outbound
traffic from the manager's device to the chatbot's domain, carrying a data payload matching the confidential
pricing schedule's structure.

**Containment.** Information Security immediately blocks further network access to the ungoverned tool
(technically enforcing a policy restriction that existed on paper but had not previously been enforced), and
the manager is asked to stop using it for any Meridian business pending review.

**Impact assessment.** The AI Governance Owner and Meridian's legal counsel jointly assess: (a) whether the
pricing data was actually retained or used to train the vendor's model — assessed as **indeterminate**, given
the tool's own terms permit retention and Meridian has no contractual visibility into what the vendor actually
does with submitted data, a materially worse position than a governed tool's contractual no-training commitment
would have provided; (b) whether the incumbent subcontractor's confidentiality clause was breached — assessed
**yes, in substance**, regardless of whether onward disclosure is ever proven, because the data left Meridian's
control without the vendor's data-handling status having been checked; and (c) that no client-facing figures,
personal data or safety-related information were involved — a mitigating factor that limits, but does not
eliminate, the exposure.

**Notification.** Legal counsel concludes that proactively notifying the incumbent subcontractor of the
incident is the lower-risk course, compared with staying silent and risking a worse-received disclosure later
if the issue surfaced independently. The incumbent is notified in writing; Meridian's commercial leadership and
the AI Governance Owner are briefed in parallel.

**Root-cause analysis.** Investigation identifies three compounding causes, not a single act of misconduct: (1)
Meridian's AI use policy (section 13.6.6) existed but had not been proactively communicated to commercial staff — an
awareness gap; (2) **no governed, Meridian-provisioned AI tool was readily available** for everyday drafting
tasks of exactly this kind, so staff had, in practice, been filling a genuine capability gap with an ungoverned
public tool — the "shadow AI" pattern named in section 13.6.10; and (3) the network-level technical block on public AI
tools existed as a stated policy but had never actually been enforced — a control that was designed but not
operated (KA 11.3.7).

**Corrective action.** Meridian: (i) procures a governed enterprise GenAI tool, with a contractual
no-training/no-retention data commitment, specifically to close the everyday-drafting capability gap that drove
the shadow-AI use — addressing the root cause, not merely the symptom; (ii) technically enforces the
network-level block on ungoverned public AI tools, closing the gap between designed and operated policy; (iii)
requires all commercial and project controls staff to complete a mandatory briefing on the AI use policy and
register before using any AI tool for Meridian business; and (iv) adds an explicit, mandatory
"confidential/third-party data involved (Y/N)" field to every AI use register entry (section 13.6.6), closing a design
gap the register itself had.

**Fig 13.6.11 — the incident-response timeline for the Pipeline Segment 4B AI disclosure.**
Chart type: a horizontal timeline with seven sequential nodes, mirroring KA 11.3.7's audit-trail flow diagram:
Detection (two independent triggers) → Containment (network block; use suspended) → Impact assessment (data
retention, confidentiality breach, materiality) → Notification (subcontractor; internal leadership) →
Root-cause analysis (three compounding causes) → Corrective action (four numbered actions) → Register &

*Underlying data:* the seven-stage narrative above.

**Animation 13.6.11 — "Two signals, one incident" [digital-only].** Frame 1: two separate icons — a
colleague's raised-hand query and an Information-Security log-alert icon — appear on opposite sides of the

**Interpretation.** The organisational lesson is not "an individual made a mistake" — though the manager's
action was, in the moment, a policy breach — it is that **the policy existed without the capability and the
enforcement needed to make following it the easy, obvious choice**. A governance programme that relies on staff
never being under time pressure is not a functioning governance programme; section 13.6.10's "when not to use AI"
guidance, and section 13.6.6's register design, exist to close exactly this kind of gap before it produces an
incident, not only to respond well once one occurs.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Closing an incident investigation once individual behaviour is corrected, without asking whether an
  organisational gap (capability, enforcement) made that behaviour likely in the first place.
- Treating "no proof the data was misused" as equivalent to "no incident occurred" — the confidentiality breach
  is real the moment the data leaves the organisation's control without the receiving party's data-handling
  status being verified, regardless of what happens to it afterwards.
- Failing to close the loop back into the AI use register and Fig 13.6.6's operating model — an incident
  investigated but not fed back into the governance artefacts that should have prevented it will very plausibly
  recur.

**AI in this topic.** As throughout this KA, network- and content-monitoring tools were a genuine part of
*detecting* this incident (section 13.6.1, section 13.6.8) — a real, demonstrated governance benefit, not a hypothetical one.
No tool assessed materiality, decided on notification, or diagnosed the root cause; those remained legal,
commercial and governance judgements throughout, precisely the closing statement this entire KA has been
building toward: **AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable — and this Knowledge Area is what makes that
disposal accountable, auditable and real.**

### Key terms

- **AI governance** — the policies, roles, processes and controls ensuring AI use is identified, risk-assessed,
  humanly overseen and traceable after the fact.
- **Hallucination** — a confident, fluent AI output that is factually wrong, including fabricated citations,
  clauses or figures.
- **Bias (AI)** — a model reproducing an unjustified, systematic disadvantage present in its training data.
- **Drift** — degradation in an AI model's accuracy over time or in a new context, without a visible change in
  how confidently it presents its output.
- **Data leakage** — confidential or client data leaving an organisation's control via an ungoverned AI tool
  with no data-processing agreement.
- **Prompt injection** — hidden instructions embedded in third-party content an AI processes, designed to
  manipulate its behaviour (awareness level).
- **Automation bias** — the tendency to trust and under-scrutinise an automated system's output, increasing
  over time as familiarity builds.
- **AI use register** — the living log of every AI use case, its data sensitivity, owner, risk tier and
  controls; the AI-governance equivalent of a risk register.
- **Risk tiering (AI)** — scoring a use case on impact (if wrong and unreviewed) and human oversight, to assign
  one of four proportionate governance tiers.
- **AI-output assurance checklist** — the seven-point structured check (source data, arithmetic, assumptions,
  omissions, certainty, confidentiality, sign-off) applied before a higher-tier AI output is relied upon.
- **ISO/IEC 42001** — the international standard specifying requirements for an organisation's AI management
  system (AIMS).
- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)** — the US NIST's voluntary framework, organised around Govern,
  Map, Measure and Manage.
- **OECD AI Principles** — the intergovernmental values-based principles for trustworthy AI (inclusive
  well-being; human-centred values and fairness; transparency; robustness, security and safety; accountability).
- **EU AI Act risk tiers** — the EU's binding risk-based classification: unacceptable, high-risk, limited risk,
  minimal/no risk.
- **Governed vs ungoverned AI tool** — an enterprise-licensed tool with a contractual data-handling commitment,
  versus a public, consumer-grade tool without one.
- **Shadow AI** — unregistered, informal AI use outside the AI use register and its controls.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 13.6.1 [topic 13.6.2, level: recall]**
An AI-drafted contract summary confidently cites "clause 14.3" for a retention term. On checking the actual
signed contract, no clause 14.3 exists. What is this an example of?
A. Model drift
B. **Hallucination** ✔
C. Automation bias
D. Prompt injection
*Rationale:* A fabricated, non-existent citation presented with full confidence is the defining feature of a
hallucination. Drift concerns declining accuracy over time in a previously reliable tool; automation bias
concerns the *reviewer's* under-scrutiny, not the AI's error itself; prompt injection concerns manipulated
instructions from processed content, not a fabricated fact.

**MCQ 13.6.2 [topic 13.6.5, level: recall]**
Which of the following best describes the NIST AI Risk Management Framework's four core functions?
A. Identify, Analyse, Respond, Monitor
B. **Govern, Map, Measure, Manage** ✔
C. Plan, Do, Check, Act
D. Avoid, Transfer, Mitigate, Accept
*Rationale:* The NIST AI RMF is organised around Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. A is the general ISO
31000-style risk process (KA 12.1–12.2) in different words; C is the generic management-system improvement
cycle referenced in relation to ISO/IEC 42001, not the NIST AI RMF specifically; D is the risk-response
strategy vocabulary from KA 12.2.7.

**MCQ 13.6.3 [topic 13.6.7, level: application]**
An AI use case produces output that, if wrong, would feed a client-facing commercial position (High impact),
and its output is currently used with only occasional, unstructured review (Light review). Under the tiering
grid in section 13.6.7, what tier does this use case fall into?
A. Tier 1
B. Tier 2
C. **Tier 3** ✔
D. Tier 4
*Rationale:* High impact combined with Light review maps to Tier 3 on the grid (mandatory checklist, dual
sign-off, monthly monitoring). Tier 1 and Tier 2 require materially stronger oversight for this impact level;
Tier 4 requires either Autonomous oversight at High impact or any oversight level at Critical impact with
weak review, neither of which applies here.

**MCQ 13.6.4 [topic 13.6.9, level: analysis]**
Applying the AI-output assurance checklist to an AI-drafted EAC, a reviewer confirms the arithmetic is
correct and the source EVM data is accurate, but does not check whether the AI's stated assumption (that a
cost shortfall is a resolved, one-off event) is consistent with a recent schedule update confirming the
shortfall lies on the critical path. What checklist failure does this best illustrate?
A. Confidentiality failure
B. **Assumption-verification failure** ✔
C. Arithmetic failure
D. Sign-off failure
*Rationale:* The reviewer verified data and arithmetic (checks 1–2) but failed to verify the AI's stated
assumption against ground truth the AI was never given (check 3) — exactly Example 13.6.4's central lesson.
No confidential data handling is described (A); the arithmetic was, in fact, correct (C); a sign-off did
occur, it was simply based on an incomplete check (D).

**MCQ 13.6.5 [topic 13.6.11, level: analysis]**
In the Pipeline Segment 4B mini-case, root-cause analysis identifies three compounding causes behind the
confidentiality incident. Which of the following was NOT one of them?
A. The AI use policy existed but had not been proactively communicated to commercial staff
B. No governed AI tool was readily available for the everyday drafting task involved
C. **The AI tool itself fabricated confidential data that did not previously exist** ✔
D. The network-level technical block on public AI tools existed on paper but had not been enforced
*Rationale:* The incident was a data-leakage/confidentiality failure — genuine Meridian pricing data left the
organisation's control via an ungoverned tool — not a hallucination; no data was fabricated. A, B and D are
the three root causes actually identified in the mini-case.

**MCQ 13.6.6 [topic 13.6.4, level: recall]**
A project controls function ensures every AI-assisted client deliverable carries a named, qualified
professional's sign-off, regardless of how much of the drafting the AI performed. Which of the five ethical
principles in section 13.6.4 does this most directly satisfy?
A. Transparency
B. Fairness
C. **Accountability** ✔
D. Privacy
*Rationale:* Accountability requires a named human to remain answerable for the output regardless of AI
involvement — exactly what a mandatory sign-off establishes. Transparency concerns disclosing that AI was
used, not who is answerable for the result; fairness concerns unbiased treatment of affected parties; privacy
concerns lawful handling of personal/confidential data.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using section 13.6.7's impact × oversight grid, explain why the same underlying AI tool (a general-purpose GenAI
   assistant) can sit at Tier 1 for one Meridian use case and Tier 3 for another.
2. In Example 13.6.4, the AI's arithmetic was entirely correct, yet the reviewing Project Controls Manager
   still rejected its recommended EAC method. Explain in two or three sentences why "the maths checks out" was
   not sufficient assurance in this case.
3. Name the three compounding root causes identified in section 13.6.11's mini-case, and explain why "the manager
   should not have done that" is an incomplete account of what went wrong.
4. Explain the difference between the NIST AI RMF and the EU AI Act's risk tiers in one or two sentences each,
   and why treating them as interchangeable would misstate an organisation's actual obligations.

**Answers**

1. Tiering depends on the *use case's* impact (what happens if the output is wrong and unreviewed) and the
   oversight actually applied, not on which product is used. An internal draft fully reviewed before anyone
   else sees it (Low/Medium impact, Full review) can sit at Tier 1, while the same tool used to draft a
   client-facing commercial position under only light review (High impact, Light review) sits at Tier 3 — Fig
   13.6.7's AI-1 and AI-2 illustrate exactly this contrast using the same category of tool.
2. The assurance checklist distinguishes verifying that a number is *computed correctly* from verifying that
   the *assumption behind the computation* is actually supported by the facts. The AI's Method 1 recommendation
   assumed a resolved, one-off cause, but the confirmed schedule update showing the shortfall on the critical
   path directly contradicted that assumption — a fact the AI was never given and so could not have accounted
   for, meaning a mathematically correct answer was built on an unverified and, in this case, wrong premise.
3. The three causes: (i) the AI use policy existed but had not been proactively communicated to commercial
   staff; (ii) no governed AI tool was readily available for the everyday drafting task, so staff filled the
   gap with an ungoverned public tool; (iii) the network-level technical block on public AI tools existed on
   paper but had never been enforced. "The manager should not have done that" addresses only the individual
   action and ignores that the organisation had not made the compliant path either available or the easier
   choice — corrective action therefore had to close the capability and enforcement gaps, not only counsel the
   individual.
4. The NIST AI RMF is a voluntary framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) that any organisation can choose to
   apply as a risk-management reference architecture, with no legal force of its own. The EU AI Act is binding
   law within its jurisdictional scope (and extraterritorially in some circumstances), classifying AI systems
   into unacceptable/high-risk/limited-risk/minimal-risk tiers with mandatory obligations attached to
   higher-risk classifications. Treating them as interchangeable would understate that non-compliance with the
   EU AI Act carries legal consequences a voluntary framework's non-adoption does not.
