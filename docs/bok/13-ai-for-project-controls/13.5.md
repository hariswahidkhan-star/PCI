# Domain 13 — AI for Project Controls & Project Management: Concepts, Tools & Practice

## KA 13.5 — AI applied across the project-controls lifecycle

data quality and governance; effective, verified prompting), and KA 13.4 catalogued the categories of AI

### 13.5.1 Estimating and budgeting: parametric and analogous ML-assisted estimating

**Definition & purpose.** Parametric and analogous estimating (KA 3.2) derive a cost from a rate applied to a
quantity, or by scaling a comparable historical project. AI-assisted estimating tools extend both techniques
by widening the pool of historical comparators a human could realistically review, fitting a statistical or
machine-learning model across many attributes at once (ground/soil type, region, crew composition, season,
contract type — not just a single "similar description" match), and producing a predicted rate or cost range
rather than a single point figure. KA 3.4.2 already introduced this capability in general forecasting terms;
this topic applies it specifically to the pre-project estimating stage, where AI's ability to search a large
historical base is most valuable because so little project-specific data yet exists.

**How AI helps, and its limits.**

| AI genuinely helps by… | …but AI cannot |
|---|---|
| Searching many more historical projects/rates than a person could review manually, matching on several attributes simultaneously | Judge whether a matched historical project is a *genuinely fair* comparator for this one — the single most important estimating judgement |
| Producing a predicted rate or range quickly, with the contributing historical data points shown | Know about a site-specific condition not yet reflected in any historical data (a fresh geotechnical finding, a new access constraint) |
| Flagging when a new package's attributes fall outside the range the model was trained on (an "out of sample" warning) | Decide how much weight a specific, localised risk (e.g. a rock reach within an otherwise average alignment) should carry in the final adopted rate |

**Example 13.5.1 — a parametric ML estimate for Pipeline Segment 4C, and why the professional does not adopt
it unadjusted.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's rail-signalling programme includes four segments (4A–4D, cross-reference KA 4.3.1's
reporting-pyramid figure). Segment 4C is now entering early (AACE Class 4) estimating: 2,400 metres of
trenching, not yet started. Meridian's estimating team runs an AI-assisted parametric tool trained on
completed Meridian trenching packages and comparable regional projects:

| Historical project | Metres | Rate ($/m) | Ground condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Segment 4A (Meridian) | 1,600 | 92 | Favourable |
| Segment 4B, WP3 (Meridian; KA 6.1.4) | 2,000 | 100 | Average |
| Coastal Crossing Segment 2 (comparator) | 1,200 | 118 | Difficult |
| Northgate Extension (comparator; cross-ref KA 3.1.6, KA 12.2.4) | 900 | 121 | Difficult |

The tool fits a simple rate-versus-ground-condition model across these and other historical points and
predicts, for Segment 4C's stated (mostly average) ground profile, a mean rate of **$108/m**, with a
predicted range of **$98–$118/m** reflecting the spread in the historical data it was trained on.

*Required.* (a) The ML-suggested total cost if the mean rate is applied uniformly to the full 2,400 m; (b)
the estimator's adjusted total once a targeted geotechnical survey — commissioned specifically because the
tool's predicted range is wide enough to warrant one — finds a 600 m sub-reach of markedly harder, rockier
ground, historically associated with a $140/m rate; (c) the difference between the two, and what it means.

*Working.*
(a) Uniform ML mean: `2,400 m × $108/m = $259,200`.
(b) Blended, survey-informed estimate: normal ground `1,800 m × $108/m = $194,400`; rock reach
`600 m × $140/m = $84,000`; total `= 194,400 + 84,000 = $278,400`.
(c) `Difference = 278,400 − 259,200 = $19,200` (`19,200 / 259,200 = 7.4%` understatement in the unadjusted
ML figure).

*Answer.* **The unadjusted ML estimate ($259,200) understates the survey-informed estimate ($278,400) by
$19,200 (7.4%).**

*Interpretation.* The ML model's $108/m mean rate and $98–$118/m range were genuinely useful — they matched
Segment 4C's *average* profile correctly and, crucially, their width was itself a signal worth acting on: a
$20/m spread on a project not yet started is wide enough to justify commissioning a targeted survey before
finalising the estimate (echoing the decision-tree logic of KA 12.2.5, applied here at the estimating rather
than the response-planning stage). What the model could not do is know, in advance, that a specific 600 m
sub-reach would prove rockier than the alignment's average — that came from a physical survey, not from
pattern-matching historical projects. Adopting the model's blended mean uniformly across all 2,400 m would
have quietly built a $19,200 shortfall into Segment 4C's base estimate before a single metre was excavated.

**Mini-case — rejecting a poor AI-suggested analogue.** For a separate, smaller valve-chamber package on
Segment 4C, the same tool suggests scaling directly from the Northgate Extension comparator ($121/m,
KA 3.1.6, KA 12.2.4), on the basis that both involve "difficult ground." The estimator rejects the suggestion:
Northgate's rate reflects hand-excavation around a congested urban utility corridor, while Segment 4C's
difficult ground is open-country rock requiring mechanical breaking — a materially different cost driver
that happens to share only the word "difficult" in the tool's description field. A better-matched comparator
(a rock-breaking package from an unrelated Meridian earthworks contract, at $132/m) is used instead. This is
the comparability judgement KA 3.4.2 already names as the one thing an analogous-estimating tool cannot make
reliably on its own: two projects can score as "similar" on a shallow text match while differing completely
on the specific cost driver that actually matters.

**A brief note on AI-assisted budget-scenario generation.** A related capability, more common at the
budgeting than the estimating stage, is scenario generation: a tool that takes a quantified risk register
(KA 12.2.4's EMV method) and rapidly produces several alternative contingency scenarios (e.g. funding to the
EMV total, to a P50, or to a P80 Monte Carlo output, KA 12.2.6) for comparison. This speeds up assembling the
comparison table a professional would otherwise build by hand; it does not decide which funding level the
organisation should actually adopt — that remains a risk-appetite decision (KA 12.1) for the professional and
sponsor, not the tool.

**Common pitfalls.** Adopting a model's mean or median output as the estimate without reviewing which
historical projects actually drove it (KA 3.4.2's comparability judgement, applied here). Treating a wide
predicted range as a reason to distrust the tool, rather than as a signal to investigate further (as this
Example does). Feeding a parametric model stale or since-superseded unit rates without first checking they
are still current (cross-reference KA 3.2's estimate-basis discipline).

**Honest AI note.** A parametric/analogous ML tool is only as good as the historical data it was trained on
— a small or unrepresentative training set (as with any of the "garbage in, garbage out" risks introduced in
KA 13.2) produces a confident-looking but poorly supported range. The tool also has no way to know about a
condition that has not yet been surveyed or documented anywhere; it extrapolates the past, it does not observe
the present. Every AI-suggested rate is a starting hypothesis for the estimator to test against current,
project-specific evidence — never a substitute for the physical survey, the estimator's own site knowledge,
or the basis-of-estimate sign-off KA 3.2 requires. **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2 (estimate classes and methods); KA 3.4.2 (analogous/driver-based forecasting and

historical input); KA 12.2.5 (the decision-tree logic behind commissioning a survey); KA 13.2 (data quality,

### 13.5.2 Scheduling: AI schedule generation and QA, risk-based durations, and delay prediction

**Definition & purpose.** Three distinct AI-assisted scheduling capabilities are in practical use: (i)
**schedule generation and logic QA** — proposing an activity sequence from a WBS and a library of comparable
historical networks, and automatically checking a built schedule for logic errors (missing predecessors,
dangling activities, illogical constraints); (ii) **risk-based duration estimation** — suggesting three-point
(optimistic/most-likely/pessimistic) duration ranges from historical productivity data, feeding schedule
Monte Carlo simulation (cross-reference KA 10.3, KA 12.2.6); and (iii) **delay prediction** — a classification
or scoring model that combines leading indicators (resourcing levels, RFI response times, inspection
backlogs, weather, subcontractor performance elsewhere in the portfolio) into a probability that a given
activity or project will finish later than planned, *before* that lateness has fully worked its way into a
formal EVM report.

**How AI helps, and its limits.**

| AI genuinely helps by… | …but AI cannot |
|---|---|
| Proposing a plausible activity sequence and durations from historical templates, and flagging logic errors (open ends, missing ties) far faster than a manual review | Confirm that a proposed logic link or duration is realistic for *this* site's actual conditions |
| Combining many leading indicators into a single early-warning score, surfacing a risk before it appears in a monthly EVM cycle | Explain the underlying *cause* of a flagged risk, or confirm the flagged pattern is not coincidental |
| Recomputing a delay-risk score continuously as new indicator data arrives, at a scale no manual review can match | Decide what action, if any, the flagged probability warrants — that remains a resourcing, commercial and schedule judgement |

**Example 13.5.2 — a delay-prediction flag on Pipeline Segment 4B's WP4, one reporting cycle ahead of the
Month-5 EVM report.**

*Scenario.* At the Month-4 status update — a full cycle before the Month-5 EVM data set (KA 6.3.2: SPI 0.75,
critical-path slip requiring paid acceleration, KA 6.3.4) formally confirms the problem — Meridian's
delay-prediction tool scores WP4 (pipe-laying & welding) against four leading indicators. The scoring shown
below is a simplified, illustrative points system (0–25 per indicator, out of 100 overall) built for teaching
purposes; production tools typically use more sophisticated statistical or machine-learning classifiers, not
a manually weighted points table — the *type* of input and output is realistic even though this specific
scoring mechanism is illustrative, not a reproduction of any named vendor's algorithm.

| Leading indicator | Observed value | Baseline/normal | Illustrative score (0–25) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Welding subcontractor crew utilisation across Meridian's active segments | 95% | < 80% | 22 |
| RFI response time (rolling average) | 9 days | 4 days | 20 |
| Weld-inspection backlog | 12 welds awaiting inspection | 3–4 (steady state) | 23 |
| Weather days lost, trailing 4 weeks | 2 days | ≈ 2 days (seasonal norm) | 3 |

*Required.* (a) The total illustrative score and the mapped delay-risk band; (b) the professional's response
before the Month-5 EVM report crystallises the position.

*Working.*
(a) `Total score = 22 + 20 + 23 + 3 = 68 / 100`. On the tool's illustrative banding (60–79 = "High"), this maps
to an output probability of **≈72%** that WP4 finishes more than two weeks beyond its Month-6 window.

(b) The flag names *what* is elevated (crew utilisation, RFI turnaround, inspection backlog) but not *why*,
nor what to do about it. The site manager and commercial team investigate directly: the welding subcontractor
confirms it is simultaneously stretched across two other Meridian packages (a resourcing pressure last
scored, at register level, as R5 — "welding subcontractor resource shortage" — a **watchlist** item at Month
3, KA 12.2.1, score 4, not among the four risks (R1–R4) carried into full quantitative treatment); the
inspection backlog corroborates the trend register's own TR-06 ("potential re-sequencing of WP4, weld-
inspection backlog," logged Month 4, KA 5.4.5, "still open, monitoring"). Consistent with KA 12.2.9's rule
that new information should promote a watchlist risk, the team promotes R5 to active management at Month 4:
they open a resourcing conversation with the subcontractor and expedite a second weld-inspector to clear the
backlog, and log the AI flag itself against TR-06 as corroborating evidence.

*Answer.* **Illustrative delay-risk score 68/100 (≈72% probability of a >2-week slip); R5 promoted from
watchlist to active management one full reporting cycle ahead of the Month-5 EVM confirmation.**

*Interpretation.* The Month-5 data that follows (SPI 0.75, a confirmed critical-path slip requiring paid
acceleration, KA 6.3.4) is consistent with — though not solely explained by — the resourcing and inspection
pressure this flag surfaced a month earlier; KA 6.3.2's separately noted equipment failure is a second,
compounding driver of the same critical-path pressure, not replaced by this one. The value the AI flag added
was **timing**, not diagnosis: it gave the team a month's head start on a problem the formal EVM cycle would
otherwise have surfaced only once SPI and CPI had already deteriorated — directly analogous to the timing
argument KA 5.4.1 makes for catching a change early rather than late.

**Mini-case — AI schedule generation and logic QA, before delay prediction has anything to work with.**
Before any of the above can run, a schedule must exist. When Segment 4C's outline schedule is first built, an
AI schedule-generation tool proposes an initial six-activity network from a WBS and a library of comparable
Meridian networks (design, procurement, trenching, pipe-laying, testing, commissioning), pre-populated with
durations drawn from historical analogues. A logic-QA pass over the proposed network — run automatically, not
manually — flags one issue immediately: the "testing" activity has no successor logic tying it to
"commissioning," leaving it a dangling end that the forward pass would silently treat as unconstrained. The
scheduler adds the missing finish-to-start tie before the network is baselined. **A second AI-suggested
input** — a three-point duration range for the new segment's trenching activity (optimistic 4.5 weeks,
most likely 5 weeks, pessimistic 7 weeks, drawn from the historical rate spread already used in 13.5.1) — is
accepted as a starting point for the schedule-risk model but is not itself baselined as the deterministic
duration; the scheduler retains the 5-week most-likely figure for the baseline and reserves the full range for
the Monte Carlo schedule-risk analysis (KA 10.3, KA 12.2.6), exactly as that quantitative technique already
requires.

*Interpretation.* Logic QA is a genuinely low-risk, high-value automation: a missing successor tie is an
objective, mechanical defect a tool can catch with certainty, unlike a delay-prediction probability. Duration
*suggestion* is a different matter — the AI proposed a plausible range from historical data, but the decision
of which figure becomes the baseline, and which feeds only the risk model, remained the scheduler's.

**Common pitfalls.** Treating a delay-prediction probability as a certainty rather than a probabilistic
early-warning signal (the same caution KA 12.2.6 gives for a Monte Carlo percentile). Escalating every flagged
score with equal urgency, rather than triaging by materiality (echoing KA 12.2.3's qualitative-screening
funnel). Failing to close the loop — logging the flag against the trend register (TR-06) so it becomes part
of the auditable record, not an ephemeral dashboard alert that disappears once the crisis passes.

**Honest AI note.** A delay-prediction score is a correlation-based output: it identifies that *this*
combination of indicators has historically preceded lateness on comparable work, not that it *causes*
lateness on this specific package. A model trained on a small or unrepresentative historical base (a common
constraint for many project controls functions, cross-reference KA 13.2 once written) can produce a
confident-sounding percentage with a much weaker evidential basis than the number implies. The model also
cannot distinguish a genuine resourcing shortage from a short-term, already-resolving blip unless it is
retrained or corrected with fresh outcome data — false positives that go uninvestigated erode trust in the
tool exactly as an unmaintained trend register erodes trust in KA 5.4.5's mini-case. **AI proposes; the
professional disposes.**

### 13.5.3 Cost control and EVM: anomaly detection, automated variance narratives and AI-assisted EAC/forecasting

**Definition & purpose.** Three linked AI-assisted workflows sit inside the cost-control-and-EVM cycle:
**anomaly detection** scans the cost ledger for postings that are statistically unusual (duplicates, outliers
against a historical run-rate, entries breaking an expected coding pattern) before they distort AC and
everything computed from it; **automated variance narratives** draft the prose that explains a CV/SV/CPI/SPI
movement (KA 4.3.8 already gives the flagship worked example of this — cross-referenced, not repeated, below);
and **AI-assisted EAC/forecasting** computes the full EAC family (KA 6.3) mechanically and drafts a
first-pass recommendation and narrative, which the professional must then verify and complete. Because AC,
CPI and EAC are so tightly coupled (KA 6.1.3, KA 6.2.3, KA 6.3.3), an error caught — or missed — at the
ledger stage propagates directly into the headline forecast a client or board reads.

**How AI helps, and its limits.**

| AI genuinely helps by… | …but AI cannot |
|---|---|
| Flagging a ledger posting that is statistically unusual (duplicate ID/amount, outlier vs run-rate) far faster and more consistently than a manual line-by-line review | Confirm *why* a flagged posting is unusual, or that it is actually wrong rather than a genuine, unusual-but-correct transaction |
| Recomputing CV/SV/CPI/SPI/EAC continuously as new data lands, and drafting a first-pass narrative and method recommendation in seconds | Know the site-specific driver behind a variance, make the one-off-versus-trend judgement (KA 6.3.6), or recognise a cross-domain implication (e.g. a statutory disclosure trigger) outside its own data feed |
| Applying a consistent driver/trend/action commentary template across many accounts at once | Assign an accountable owner and a credible date to an action, or decide which of several mechanically correct EACs to defend to a sponsor |

**Example 13.5.3a — an anomaly-detection catch on Pipeline Segment 4B's ledger, and the CPI/EAC swing it
prevented.**

*Scenario.* Ahead of finalising the Month-5 AC extract already used throughout KA 6.3 ($506,250), Meridian's
ERP-integrated anomaly-detection tool flags a $22,000 subcontract posting against WP4's control account: the
invoice ID and amount exactly match a payment already posted three weeks earlier, a pattern consistent with a
duplicate caused by an interface retry. The cost controller investigates with accounts payable, confirms it is
indeed a duplicate, and reverses the posting **before** the Month-5 AC extract is finalised — which is why
KA 6.3's Month-5 AC of $506,250 is already the *corrected* figure.

*Required.* The counterfactual CPI and EAC (Method 2) had the duplicate **not** been caught, and the size of
the resulting distortion.

*Working.*
Counterfactual AC: `506,250 + 22,000 = 528,250`.
Counterfactual CV: `EV − AC = 405,000 − 528,250 = (123,250)`, against the actual, corrected CV of `(101,250)`
— a $22,000 gap, exactly the size of the duplicate, confirming the arithmetic is internally consistent.
Counterfactual CPI: `EV / AC = 405,000 / 528,250 = 0.77` (2 d.p.), against the actual, corrected CPI of 0.80.
Counterfactual EAC (Method 2, `BAC × AC/EV`, avoiding a compounding rounding step):
`EAC = 600,000 × 528,250 / 405,000 = 782,593` (rounded to the nearest dollar), against the actual, reported
EAC (Method 2) of $750,000.

*Answer.* **The uncaught duplicate would have overstated EAC (Method 2) by $32,593 — 5.4% of BAC, 4.3% of the
reported EAC — from a single $22,000 posting error.**

*Interpretation.* This is the sharpest possible illustration of why anomaly detection at the ledger stage
matters specifically to a controls professional: EVM's forecasting formulae (KA 6.3) are highly sensitive to
AC's accuracy, so a small, uncaught data-quality error near the ledger does not stay small — it is
mechanically amplified by `BAC/CPI` into a materially wrong headline forecast, exactly the concern KA 6.1.3's
accrual-adjustment mini-case raised in the opposite direction (an *under*-stated AC). The tool did not know
the posting was definitely wrong; it only knew the pattern was statistically unusual. Confirming it with
accounts payable — the human step — is what actually established the fact before it reached the report.

**Mini-case — automated cost extraction and reconciliation catching a miscoding, not a duplication.** A
related but distinct RPA/automated-extraction capability (cross-reference KA 5.2's commitment→accrual→actual
cycle) pulls labour, materials, equipment and subcontract postings directly from Meridian's ERP into each
control account, replacing a manual ledger pull. Reconciling WP4's extracted postings against KA 6.1.3's
cost-element structure, the tool flags a $9,400 equipment posting coded to WP4 (pipe-laying & welding) that,
on the description field, actually reads "trenching backhoe hire" — equipment associated with WP3, not WP4.
The cost controller confirms with the site team: the invoice was miscoded at data entry. Correcting the
coding does not change total project AC, but it does matter for control-account-level accuracy: WP4's AC
falls by $9,400 and WP3's rises by the same amount, each control account's own CPI shifting slightly as a
result, and the driver/trend narrative for each account (KA 4.3.8) being attributed to the correct cause.
This is a different failure mode from Example 13.5.3a's duplicate — a **misclassification**, not a
**duplication** — and illustrates that ledger-level AI assistance catches more than one type of data-quality
risk, each requiring its own kind of human confirmation (accounts payable for a duplicate; the site team for
a miscoding).

**A brief note on adaptive delivery.** Where scope is deliberately variable (Domain 9's agile/adaptive
delivery), AI-assisted forecasting applies the identical EAC-family logic to AgileEVM's velocity- and
burnup-derived EV (cross-reference KA 9.5, KA 9.6) — the same "AI proposes; the professional disposes"
discipline governs an AI-drafted sprint-forecast narrative exactly as it governs Example 13.5.3b's EAC
narrative here, with the added complication that scope itself, not just performance, is expected to move.

**Automated variance narratives.** A GenAI tool can turn a raw EVM table into draft prose in seconds and
apply a house driver/trend/action template consistently across many control accounts (cross-reference KA
13.3, once written). KA 4.3.8 already works this through in full — a fluent but empty AI first draft on
Pipeline Segment 4B's Month-5 CPI 0.80/SPI 0.75 position, set against the professional's verified version
that adds the specific driver, the trend/isolated diagnosis and named, dated actions — and is not repeated
here. Two related capabilities extend that pattern: **threshold-triggered drafting**, where a narrative draft
is generated automatically the moment a metric crosses a RAG boundary (KA 4.3.4, KA 4.3.10), removing the lag
between a breach occurring and a first-pass explanation being available for review; and **natural-language
querying**, covered in depth in 13.5.6 below, which lets a reader ask a plain-language question of the
underlying, reconciled data model rather than waiting for a scheduled report.

**Example 13.5.3b — an AI-assisted EAC narrative for the Month-5 exception report, and what the professional
adds before it is issued.**

*Scenario.* At the Month-5 data date, an AI forecasting assistant is given Pipeline Segment 4B's EVM data set
(BAC $600,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250; CPI 0.80; SPI 0.75), the three-period CPI trend (KA 6.3.3) and the
schedule float report confirming the shortfall sits on the critical path (KA 6.3.4), and is asked to compute
the EAC family and draft the exception-report narrative.

*AI first draft (verbatim, for illustration).*
> "EAC ranges from $701,250 (Method 1) to $831,250 (Method 3). Given the stable three-period CPI trend and
> the confirmed critical-path schedule pressure, Method 3 ($831,250, VAC adverse $231,250) is the
> best-supported figure. The project will very likely finish around $831,250. Recommend reporting this figure
> to the client this cycle."

*Required.* Assess the AI draft's mechanical accuracy and identify what a professional must add before this
reaches an exception report.

*Working.* The three EAC figures and the Method 3 recommendation are mechanically correct and match KA
6.3.6–6.3.7's own worked diagnostic exactly — this part of the task is genuinely low-risk to automate, since
the underlying arithmetic is fully deterministic (as KA 6.3.7's own "AI in this topic" note already
observes). Four things are missing or wrong:

1. **Overstated certainty.** "Will very likely finish around $831,250" asserts a confidence the underlying
   analysis does not support — Method 3 is the *best-evidenced current forecast*, not a prediction stated with
   that degree of assurance; KA 6.3.7 itself recommends disclosing the full method range, not one number
   presented as near-certain.
2. **No currency check on the critical-path finding.** The AI used the float report it was given, but did not
   — and could not — confirm that report is still current as of the Month-5 data date; float positions can
   shift (KA 6.4.5).
3. **No statutory cross-reference.** The AI has no visibility into financial-reporting standards: it does not
   flag that a forecast loss on this fixed-price contract (contract price $750,000; forecast margin
   `750,000 − 831,250 = (81,250)`, KA 4.3.10) is the trigger for testing an IAS 37 onerous-contract provision
   (cross-reference KA 1.4, KA 2.4, KA 2.5.4) — an omission entirely explained by the tool operating on EVM
   data alone, with no connection to Domain 2's standards.
4. **No named, dated actions.** The draft recommends "reporting this figure" but assigns no owner or date for
   confirming the acceleration cost, reviewing contingency, or notifying finance (KA 4.3.10's three actions).

*Answer.* **The AI draft's arithmetic and method selection are correct and reusable; its certainty language,
currency check, statutory cross-reference and accountable actions are not — and each of the four gaps
requires professional knowledge the tool's own data feed could not supply.**

*Interpretation.* This is the fullest illustration in this KA of *input → AI step → professional verification
→ output*: the input was the reconciled EVM data set and float report; the AI step produced a mechanically
correct comparison and an overconfident, incomplete narrative in seconds; the professional's verification
re-scoped the certainty language, re-confirmed the float report's currency, added the IAS 37 flag and named
the actions — producing exactly the exception report already built in full in KA 4.3.10. The AI draft was
fast and directionally right; it was not, on its own, a report a professional could issue.

### 13.5.4 Risk management: AI risk identification from documents, Monte Carlo-assisted analysis and early-warning signals

**Definition & purpose.** KA 12.2 already gives, topic by topic, a thorough "AI in this topic" treatment
across identification, qualitative screening, EMV, decision trees, Monte Carlo simulation and monitoring —
not repeated here. This topic adds the cross-lifecycle view the manifest for this KA specifically calls for:
**document-based risk identification** (NLP tools scanning correspondence, RFIs and meeting minutes for
candidate risk signals), **Monte Carlo-assisted analysis** (AI-suggested three-point ranges and correlation
detection, cross-referenced to KA 12.2.6's existing worked treatment), and **early-warning signals** (the
risk-exposure trend indicator of KA 12.2.9, computed continuously rather than only at each scheduled review).

**How AI helps, and its limits.**

| AI genuinely helps by… | …but AI cannot |
|---|---|
| Scanning a volume of correspondence no team could read line by line, surfacing candidate risk signals and duplicate entries | Determine whether a candidate signal is a genuine, register-worthy risk or a false positive from keyword matching |
| Suggesting three-point ranges and correlations between risk-affected elements from historical project data (KA 12.2.6) | Confirm those ranges or correlations actually apply to *this* project's specific conditions |
| Computing a portfolio-wide risk-exposure trend continuously, rather than only at scheduled reviews | Decide whether a trend movement is material enough to escalate, or authorise a resulting contingency change |

**Example 13.5.4 — a document-scanning pass on Pipeline Segment 4B's correspondence, Month 4.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's NLP-based correspondence-scanning tool processes a batch of RFIs and meeting minutes
and surfaces three candidate risk signals.

| # | Candidate signal | Outcome after professional review |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Design team notes a possible clash with the third-party utility crossing near chainage 1+400" | **Duplicate** — matches the already-logged R2 (utility clash rework, KA 12.2.2); tool's flag is discarded, no new entry created |
| 2 | "Subcontractor's public-liability insurance certificate expires in six weeks; renewal not yet confirmed" | **Genuine new signal** — not previously logged; raised as a new register entry (commercial/compliance risk) and scored at qualitative screening |
| 3 | "Site meeting started 20 minutes late due to a delay collecting attendees from the gate" | **False positive** — the word "delay" matched the tool's keyword pattern, but the sentence describes an administrative scheduling matter with no project risk content |

*Required.* Assess the value of the scanning pass, and the triage effort it still requires.

*Working/Answer.* Of three flagged items, one **(#1)** correctly duplicates an already-registered risk and is
discarded without further work — a genuine time saving, since a human reviewer confirms the duplicate in
seconds rather than re-investigating it from scratch. One **(#2)** is a genuine catch: an insurance-expiry
risk that had not yet been logged, now correctly entered and scored. One **(#3)** is a false positive requiring
a human's few seconds of judgement to dismiss.

*Interpretation.* A one-in-three false-positive rate in this illustrative batch is not a failure of the tool —
it is the expected, manageable cost of casting a wide enough net to catch genuine signal #2, exactly as
KA 12.2.2's manual brainstorming technique also produces candidates that do not survive triage. The value is
in the *volume* the tool can screen, not in a claim that every flag is correct.

**Mini-case — early warning from a continuous, rather than monthly, risk-exposure trend.** KA 12.2.9's
monitoring mini-case computed a revised quantified EMV total of $37,500 at the Month-5 scheduled review, down
from $50,000 (a fall of $12,500, largely driven by R1's favourable survey result). An AI-computed exposure
trend, refreshed weekly rather than only at each scheduled review, shows that fall did not happen evenly: it
occurred almost entirely in the single week the geotechnical survey report was filed, with the register's
other components essentially flat either side of it. A continuously computed trend surfaces this **as an
event**, not merely as a before/after difference read a month apart — useful because it lets the risk owner
confirm the movement traces to one specific, verifiable cause (the survey) rather than a general, harder-to-
explain drift, and lets R6 (permit delay), which the KA 12.2.9 mini-case separately promotes onto the
quantified register that same month, be tracked from the moment its own trigger (the permitting authority's
confirmed six-week backlog) occurred, not only once the monthly review catches up with it.

### 13.5.5 Document and contract analysis: extraction, obligation tracking and claim support

**Definition & purpose.** Contract-analytics tools apply extraction and classification techniques to
contracts and correspondence for three related purposes: **extraction** (pulling key terms — price, dates,
milestones, liquidated-damages rates, retention percentages, payment terms — into a structured record);
**obligation tracking** (building a compliance calendar of notice periods, deliverable dates and renewal
deadlines from the contract's own clauses); and **claim support** (assembling the correspondence timeline and
relevant clause references that substantiate — or undermine — a claim's entitlement). The full contractual
mechanics of variations and claims belong to KA 7.2 (once written); this topic covers only the
project-controls-facing AI workflow around them.

**How AI helps, and its limits.**

| AI genuinely helps by… | …but AI cannot |
|---|---|
| Extracting key terms and dates from a long contract far faster than a manual read-through | Confirm the extracted clause means what a human reader, in context, would conclude it means |
| Assembling a correspondence timeline and flagging the relevant clause for a claim | Determine entitlement — whether the contract's actual wording, applied to the facts, supports the claim |
| Building an obligation/compliance calendar from a contract's notice and renewal provisions | Know that a deadline has been informally varied by conduct or a side agreement not captured in the document set |

**Example 13.5.5 — AI-assisted claim support for Pipeline Segment 4B's CL-01.**

*Scenario.* CL-01 (KA 5.4.2's change register: "10 days EOT + prolongation, late tie-in permit release,"
$18,000 claimed, "under review... pending substantiation") requires Meridian to demonstrate the client-caused
delay. A contract-analytics tool is given the correspondence file and the contract's illustrative,
FIDIC-style permit-related notice/EOT clause, and asked to assemble a draft evidence pack.

*AI-assembled draft (structured output).*

| Item | AI-extracted detail |
|---|---|
| Contract clause identified | Illustrative Clause 8.4-style extension-of-time provision, permit delays attributable to the Employer/Engineer |
| Permit application submitted | Month 4, week 1 (per Meridian's permit tracker) |
| Contractually anticipated turnaround | 3 weeks (per the illustrative clause's referenced schedule) |
| Actual permit release | Month 4, week 6 — **3 weeks beyond the anticipated turnaround** |
| Corroborating correspondence | Two RFIs referencing the pending permit; one email from the permitting authority confirming a general processing backlog (cross-reference R6, KA 12.2.1, 12.2.9 — a confirmed six-week backlog) |
| Draft entitlement conclusion | "The delay appears attributable to the permitting authority, supporting a 10-day EOT claim" |

*Required.* What the professional must do before this draft evidence pack is submitted.

*Working/Answer.* The professional (a) **verifies every extracted date** against the primary correspondence
directly, rather than trusting the extraction; (b) **confirms the clause reading** — an illustrative,
AI-summarised clause description is not the same as the actual, negotiated contract wording, which only a
person with commercial/legal authority should rely on to assert entitlement; (c) **checks the causal chain**
— the AI has correctly linked R6's confirmed backlog to the correspondence, but has not established that
*Meridian's own* permit application was submitted correctly and on time (a precondition for entitlement the
tool has no visibility into); and (d) **quantifies the commercial ask** using KA 5.4.4's methodology (direct
cost plus schedule-driven indirect cost), not merely repeating the AI's draft $18,000 figure without an
independent build-up.

*Interpretation.* The AI-assembled pack materially speeds up the mechanical work of locating and dating
correspondence — work that would otherwise consume hours of a commercial team's time — but it drafts a
*conclusion* about entitlement that only a person with authority over the contract can actually stand behind.
This is the same "AI proposes; the professional disposes" pattern as Example 13.5.3b's EAC narrative, applied
to a commercial rather than a cost-forecasting judgement.

**Structured example — an AI-built obligation-tracking calendar following VO-01.** Once VO-01 (the isolation
valve chamber variation, KA 5.4.4–5.4.7) is approved, a document-analysis tool scans the contract and VO-01's
own supplemental terms and builds a compliance calendar of upcoming obligations:

| Obligation | Source clause (illustrative) | Due date | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notify client's Engineer of VO-01 completion | Illustrative variation-notice clause | 5 working days after chamber commissioning | Not yet due |
| Renew the trenching subcontractor's insurance certificate | Subcontract Schedule 4 | 2 weeks before Segment 4C mobilisation (cross-ref 13.5.4's genuinely new signal #2) | **Flagged — renewal not yet confirmed** |
| Submit as-built P&ID drawing (Rev C) | Illustrative technical-submittal clause | 10 working days after chamber commissioning | Not yet due |

*Interpretation.* The calendar itself is a mechanical extraction from clause and date data; the flagged
insurance-renewal item is the same signal 13.5.4's document-scanning example surfaced independently, now
reaching the compliance calendar through a different tool and workflow — a reasonable illustration of how
overlapping AI tools can, and should, corroborate rather than replace each other. Confirming the renewal
itself is chased and closed remains a human task; the calendar only makes the deadline visible in time to act
on it.

*Interpretation.* Natural-language querying removes the friction of waiting for a scheduled report to ask an

### 13.5.7 Progress measurement: computer vision

**Definition & purpose.** Computer-vision (CV) progress measurement uses drone photogrammetry, fixed site
cameras or periodic 360° walkthrough imagery, processed by an image-recognition model — often compared
against a BIM or 3D reference model — to estimate physical percentage complete automatically, without a
manual site walk or quantity survey. It is a distinct, genuinely new capability among the applications in this
KA, extending KA 6.1.4's percentage-complete-by-units measurement method with an automated, photographic data
source.

**How AI helps, and its limits.**

| AI genuinely helps by… | …but AI cannot |
|---|---|
| Covering a large site or long linear alignment (a pipeline route) far faster than a manual walk or survey | Verify that visually "complete-looking" work actually meets specification — quality is invisible to a camera exactly as it is invisible to EV itself (KA 6.4.7) |
| Producing a dated, photographic evidence trail useful for both EV substantiation and, where needed, dispute evidence (cross-reference 13.5.5) | Reliably distinguish visually similar states (e.g. shored-but-unexcavated trench versus completed excavation) without a well-modelled reference to compare against |
| Flagging visual anomalies as a byproduct (an unexpected excavation reach, a missing barrier) | Substitute for the quantity survey or milestone sign-off that remains the authoritative EV input once a discrepancy is found |

**Example 13.5.7 — a drone/CV progress estimate for WP3, checked against the verified quantity survey.**

*Scenario.* At the Month-3 data date, WP3's verified progress — 900 m of 2,000 m excavated, EV $90,000 — is
already established in KA 6.1.4 as the authoritative figure. Independently, a drone photogrammetry survey,
processed by a CV model comparing captured imagery against the alignment's reference model, estimates **870 m**
excavated.

*Required.* (a) The CV-estimated EV; (b) the size of the discrepancy against the verified figure; (c) the
likely cause.

*Working.*
(a) `CV-estimated % complete = 870 / 2,000 = 43.5%`; `CV-estimated EV = 43.5% × 200,000 = 87,000`.
(b) `Discrepancy = 900 − 870 = 30 m` (`30/900 = 3.3%` of the surveyed length; `EV gap = 90,000 − 87,000 =
3,000`).
(c) Investigation finds a short, shored-but-not-yet-fully-excavated stretch that, viewed from the drone's
aerial angle, visually resembles completed trenching — a known CV limitation where shoring or formwork can be
misclassified against imagery alone.

*Answer.* **CV-estimated EV $87,000 versus the verified $90,000 — a $3,000 (3.3%) shortfall, traced to a
misclassified shored section.**

*Interpretation.* The CV survey provided a fast, largely independent cross-check spanning the *entire*
alignment in a fraction of the time a manual walk would take — genuinely valuable, and the discrepancy it
produced was itself useful, prompting a targeted check of the flagged stretch. It was not, however,
automatically more accurate than the ground-truth quantity survey already treated as authoritative throughout
KA 6.1.4 and KA 6.1.6; the correct response is to reconcile the two, not to substitute the CV figure for the
verified one, echoing KA 6.4.4's mini-case on duration-elapsed versus verified quantity progress.

**Mini-case — a visual-anomaly byproduct: an unexpected excavation reach.** The same Month-3 drone survey that
produced the 870 m progress estimate also flags, as a byproduct of its image comparison against the reference
alignment, a roughly 25 m stretch of excavation that falls **outside** WP3's approved trench corridor,
adjacent to chainage 1+250. The site team investigates: the excavation was authorised verbally by a site
supervisor to accommodate an access ramp for plant movement, was never logged as a scope change, and sits
squarely inside KA 5.4.1's "unlogged verbal instruction" pattern — the same failure mode illustrated there for
a different work package, caught here not by a register check but by a computer-vision model comparing
imagery against the approved corridor. The finding is raised as a new change-register entry, assessed under
KA 5.4.4's methodology, and either regularised as a minor scope item or reinstated, rather than left as an
unexplained variance discovered only once the cost report shows it.

*Interpretation.* This is a genuinely useful, unplanned side-benefit of CV progress surveys: because the
model compares imagery against an approved reference, it can flag work that falls outside approved boundaries
just as readily as it estimates how much approved work is complete — but, as with every other AI application
in this KA, the finding is a prompt to investigate, not a conclusion about what actually happened on site.

**Fig 13.5.7 — CV-estimated versus verified progress: WP3, Month 3.**
Chart type: a paired bar chart. Two bars: "Verified quantity survey" (900 m, EV $90,000) and "CV drone
survey" (870 m, EV $87,000), brand blue `#1D4ED8` for the verified bar, a lighter tone for the CV estimate,

**Common pitfalls.** Treating a CV-derived percentage complete as automatically authoritative because it is

confidently wrong comparison (a specific case of "garbage in, garbage out"). Using CV output directly as EV
without the same measurement-method discipline (front-loading risk, KA 6.1.4) applied to any other progress
input.

**Honest AI note.** Computer-vision progress measurement is a genuinely current, increasingly used capability
in construction and linear-infrastructure projects, and it materially speeds up progress assessment at scale.
It remains an imperfect visual-classification technology: occlusion, weather, lighting and visually similar
states (as in this Example) all degrade its accuracy, and it has no way to assess conformance to
specification. Every CV-derived percentage complete requires the same verification a manual measurement
would, before it earns value. **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

### 13.5.8 Portfolio-level analytics

**Definition & purpose.** Portfolio-level analytics aggregate the AI-assisted signals from 13.5.1–13.5.7 —
RAG status, forecast movement (ΔEAC, KA 3.4.4), delay-prediction scores (13.5.2), risk-exposure trends
(KA 12.2.9) — across many projects or control accounts simultaneously, at a scale a human reviewer cannot
manually replicate every reporting cycle. This is the AI use case that scales specifically *because* it
operates at the programme/portfolio altitude of KA 4.3.1's reporting pyramid, where a human's attention is the
genuinely scarce resource.

**How AI helps, and its limits.**

| AI genuinely helps by… | …but AI cannot |
|---|---|
| Combining several indicators into one composite score per project, ranking a whole portfolio for where limited human attention should go this cycle | Know about a qualitative risk (a counterparty issue, a reputational concern) that has not yet produced any quantitative signal |
| Recomputing the ranking continuously as new data lands across every project, without manual re-tabulation | Decide that a low-ranked item still warrants review this cycle, for reasons outside the model's inputs |
| Preserving drill-down to the underlying project/control-account detail behind any portfolio figure (KA 4.3.7's "one truth, many views") | Guarantee that every project's underlying data is coded and structured consistently enough for a fair cross-project comparison (KA 1.5) |

**Example 13.5.8 — an attention-priority ranking across Meridian's rail-signalling programme, Month 5.**

*Scenario.* A portfolio dashboard combines three signals into an illustrative composite "attention score"
(0–100), weighted 40% RAG severity, 30% ΔEAC-trend severity, 30% delay-prediction probability — a teaching
illustration of the *type* of weighting such a tool applies, not a specific vendor's proprietary formula.

| Segment | RAG (severity, 0–100) | ΔEAC-trend severity (0–100) | Delay probability (%) | Composite score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4A | Green (10) | 5 | 8 | `0.4×10 + 0.3×5 + 0.3×8 = 7.9 ≈ 8` |
| 4B | Red (100) | 85 | 72 (13.5.2) | `0.4×100 + 0.3×85 + 0.3×72 = 87.1 ≈ 87` |
| 4C | n/a, not yet baselined (10) | 0 | 0 | `0.4×10 + 0 + 0 = 4` |
| 4D | Amber (50) | 30 | 25 | `0.4×50 + 0.3×30 + 0.3×25 = 36.5 ≈ 37` |

*Required.* (a) The resulting attention-priority ranking; (b) whether the ranking alone should determine which
segments the programme director reviews this cycle.

*Working.* Ranking by composite score, highest first: **4B (87) → 4D (37) → 4A (8) → 4C (4).**

*Answer.* **Segment 4B — the same Month-5 crisis built up throughout this chapter — is confirmed as the
programme's top priority this cycle; Segment 4C ranks lowest, consistent with it being an early-stage estimate
with no execution data yet (13.5.1).**

*Interpretation.* The ranking correctly surfaces Segment 4B, and the composite score gives the director a
single, defensible reason why (RAG severity, forecast deterioration and a corroborated delay-prediction signal
all point the same way). However, the programme director separately knows Segment 4C — despite its lowest
score — has a newly identified counterparty risk (a prospective subcontractor's pre-qualification lapsing)
that no execution-stage indicator could yet reflect, and elects to review it this cycle regardless of its
ranking. A low composite score means "no signal has yet appeared," not "nothing here needs a professional's
attention."

**Mini-case — a training-data bias surfaced during model review.** During a periodic review of the composite-
score model (good governance practice in its own right, cross-reference KA 3.4.6's forecast-accuracy-tracking
discussion and forward to KA 13.6, once written), Meridian's controls team notices the model was trained
almost entirely on Meridian's own historical rail-signalling segments (4A and 4B), with only a handful of
external comparator projects (Coastal Crossing, Northgate) in the training set. On review, the model
systematically under-scores delay probability for projects using external subcontractors unfamiliar to
Meridian's own historical base — precisely the profile of a new, not-yet-mobilised contractor being
considered for Segment 4D's next phase — because the model has simply seen too few comparable examples to
score that profile with any real confidence, not because such projects are genuinely lower-risk. The team
documents this as a known model limitation, applies a manual uplift to Segment 4D's delay-probability input
until the model can be retrained on a wider, more representative data set, and flags the issue for the
organisation's AI-governance process rather than quietly trusting the model's output.

*Interpretation.* This is the same "garbage in, garbage out" and organisation-specific-bias risk KA 3.4.6
raises for forecast-accuracy tracking, now shown at the point where it most matters in this KA: a portfolio-
level triage tool that systematically under-flags a whole category of project does not merely produce a wrong
number for one segment — it can quietly starve exactly the projects that most need scrutiny of the human
attention the entire tool exists to direct toward them.

**Common pitfalls.** Treating the composite score as a complete picture of portfolio risk rather than a
triage aid. Losing drill-down to project-level detail, repeating KA 4.3.7's under-aggregation/over-
aggregation trap at portfolio scale. Comparing projects whose underlying cost coding or EVM measurement
methods are inconsistent (KA 1.5, KA 6.1.4) as if their scores were directly comparable.

**Honest AI note.** Portfolio analytics genuinely solve a real capacity problem — no controls function can
manually re-review every project, every account, every cycle — but a composite score is only as trustworthy
as its weighting choices and its underlying data's cross-project consistency, and it inherits every limit
already identified in 13.5.1–13.5.7 for the signals it combines. It is, ultimately, a prioritisation aid for
a professional's finite attention, never a replacement for it. **AI proposes; the professional disposes** —
across a single control account, and across an entire portfolio, alike.

### Synthesis: one lifecycle, one governing rule

Walking Pipeline Segment 4B's lifecycle from an early parametric estimate for its sibling Segment 4C
(13.5.1), through schedule generation and a delay-prediction flag on WP4 (13.5.2), a ledger anomaly caught
before it corrupted a headline EAC and an AI-drafted exception-report narrative completed by a professional's
review (13.5.3), a document-scanning pass and a continuous risk-exposure trend (13.5.4), AI-assisted claim
support for CL-01 and an obligation-tracking calendar (13.5.5), audience-tailored automated reporting
(13.5.6), a computer-vision progress cross-check (13.5.7) and a portfolio-wide attention ranking with its own
training-data bias caught on review (13.5.8) — one pattern recurs in every single case, regardless of
domain, data type or technique. AI is genuinely strong at **scale, speed and pattern-matching**: reading more
correspondence, recomputing more ratios, screening more transactions, and covering more site or more
projects than a human team can manually process every reporting cycle. It is consistently weak at the same
three things every time: **causal judgement** (why did this happen, and will it continue), **contextual
knowledge outside its own data feed** (a statutory disclosure trigger an EVM tool cannot see; a counterparty
risk no execution-stage indicator yet reflects; a site condition no historical project ever encountered), and
**accountability** (an AI tool cannot sign a report, approve a change, or stand behind a forecast to a
sponsor). This is true whether the workflow sits in Domain 3's estimating, Domain 5's cost control, Domain
6's EVM, Domain 7's contracts, Domain 9's adaptive delivery, Domain 10's scheduling, Domain 12's risk, or
Domain 4's reporting — the domain changes; the governance pattern does not. Every application in this KA
followed the same *input → AI step → professional verification/decision → output* shape precisely because
that shape is the practical, working definition of **"AI proposes; the professional disposes"** — not a
slogan repeated once per chapter, but the operating discipline a project controls professional applies, by
habit, to every AI-assisted task on every project, every cycle.

### Key terms

- **Parametric/analogous ML estimating** — statistical or machine-learning models that predict a rate or cost
  by matching a new package against many historical comparators simultaneously, producing a predicted range
  rather than a single point figure.
- **Schedule logic QA** — automated checking of a CPM network for missing predecessors, dangling activities or
  illogical constraints.
- **Delay-prediction model** — a classification or scoring model combining leading indicators (resourcing,
  RFI turnaround, inspection backlog, weather, portfolio-wide subcontractor performance) into a probability of
  lateness, ahead of formal EVM confirmation.
- **Leading indicator** — a metric that moves ahead of, and is correlated with, a later performance outcome
  (e.g. CPI/SPI deterioration), used as an AI model's input.
- **Anomaly detection (cost ledger)** — statistical/pattern-based flagging of unusual postings (duplicates,
  outliers against run-rate) in the cost ledger, before they distort AC and downstream EVM figures.
- **Automated variance narrative** — AI-drafted prose explaining a CV/SV/CPI/SPI movement from a raw data
  table, requiring the professional's driver/trend/action review before issue (KA 4.3.8).
- **AI-assisted EAC/forecasting** — automated computation of the EAC family (KA 6.3) and a first-pass method
  recommendation and narrative, always requiring professional verification of certainty language, currency of
  inputs, cross-domain implications and named actions.
- **Natural-language querying** — asking a plain-language question of a reconciled underlying data model and
  receiving a direct answer, rather than waiting for a scheduled report.
- **Contract data extraction** — pulling key terms (price, dates, milestones, LDs, retention, payment terms)
  from a contract into a structured record.
- **Obligation tracking** — an AI-assisted compliance calendar of notice periods, deliverables and renewal
  deadlines derived from a contract's own clauses.
- **AI-assisted claim support** — assembling a correspondence timeline and relevant clause references to
  substantiate a claim, without determining entitlement.
- **Computer-vision (CV) progress measurement** — drone/camera imagery, processed by an image-recognition
  model and typically compared against a BIM/3D reference model, to estimate physical percentage complete
  automatically.
- **Portfolio-level analytics / composite attention score** — a weighted combination of several AI-assisted
  signals (RAG, forecast movement, delay probability) used to rank many projects for where limited human
  review attention should go this cycle.
- **"AI proposes; the professional disposes"** — the governing rule of the AI domain, applied throughout this
  KA to every lifecycle application: an AI output is a draft requiring human verification, judgement and
  accountable sign-off before it is relied upon.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 13.5.1 [topic 13.5.1, level: application — numerical]**
An AI parametric estimating tool predicts a mean rate of $108/m for a 2,400 m trenching package. A subsequent
geotechnical survey finds 600 m of that alignment needs a $140/m rate, with the remaining 1,800 m confirmed at
the model's $108/m mean. What is the survey-informed total estimate?
A. $259,200
B. **$278,400** ✔
C. $336,000
D. $19,200
*Rationale:* `(1,800 × 108) + (600 × 140) = 194,400 + 84,000 = 278,400`. A is the *unadjusted* uniform-rate
estimate (Example 13.5.1); C wrongly applies $140/m to the full 2,400 m; D is the difference between the two
totals, not either total itself.

**MCQ 13.5.2 [topic 13.5.3, level: application — numerical]**
A duplicate $22,000 subcontract posting is included in a project's actual cost of $528,250 before being
caught and reversed by an anomaly-detection tool. Earned value is $405,000. What is the corrected
(post-reversal) Cost Performance Index?
A. 0.77
B. **0.80** ✔
C. 1.25
D. 1.30
*Rationale:* Corrected AC `= 528,250 − 22,000 = 506,250`; `CPI = 405,000/506,250 = 0.80`. A is the
*counterfactual* CPI had the duplicate not been caught (Example 13.5.3a); C and D invert or misapply the
ratio.

**MCQ 13.5.3 [topic 13.5.2, level: analysis]**
An AI delay-prediction model reports a 72% probability that a work package will finish more than two weeks
late, based on leading indicators including subcontractor crew utilisation and an inspection backlog. What is
the correct professional interpretation of this output?
A. The work package is certain to finish late, and the finish date should be revised immediately
B. **The output is a probabilistic early-warning signal that should prompt investigation of the underlying
   drivers before any decision is made** ✔
C. The figure can be ignored until it is confirmed by the next formal EVM report
D. The model has determined the root cause of the delay and no further investigation is needed
*Rationale:* A delay-prediction score is correlation-based and probabilistic, not a certainty (13.5.2's
Honest AI note); it should trigger investigation, not either an automatic schedule change (A) or dismissal
until later confirmed (C); the model identifies elevated indicators, not root cause (D).

**MCQ 13.5.4 [topic 13.5.3, level: analysis]**
An AI forecasting assistant computes the correct EAC family for a project and drafts an exception-report
narrative recommending the highest of the three methods, stating the project "will very likely finish" at that
figure. Which of the following is the most significant gap a professional must close before this narrative is
issued?
A. The AI's arithmetic is likely wrong and must be recalculated from scratch
B. **The AI's certainty language overstates what a "best-evidenced" forecast assumption actually supports, and
   the draft omits any cross-domain (e.g. statutory) implications outside the AI's own data feed** ✔
C. AI tools cannot be used for EAC calculations under any circumstances
D. The narrative should be issued as drafted, since the arithmetic is correct
*Rationale:* As Example 13.5.3b shows, the EAC arithmetic and method selection can be genuinely correct and
reusable; the professional's essential additions are toning down overstated certainty and supplying
cross-domain checks (e.g. IAS 37) and named actions the AI cannot see — not redoing correct arithmetic (A) or
issuing the draft unreviewed (D); C overstates the limitation.

**MCQ 13.5.5 [topic 13.5.7, level: application — numerical]**
A computer-vision drone survey estimates 870 m of a 2,000 m trenching scope excavated, where the verified
quantity survey confirms 900 m and the work package is budgeted at $200,000. What is the earned-value gap
between the CV estimate and the verified figure?
A. $1,500
B. **$3,000** ✔
C. $6,000
D. $30,000
*Rationale:* CV-estimated EV `= (870/2,000) × 200,000 = 87,000`; verified EV `= (900/2,000) × 200,000 =
90,000`; gap `= 90,000 − 87,000 = 3,000` (Example 13.5.7). D confuses the 30 m quantity gap with a dollar
figure.

**MCQ 13.5.6 [topic 13.5.8, level: analysis]**
A portfolio analytics tool ranks four projects by a composite attention score and places Project X lowest.
The programme director nonetheless schedules a review of Project X this cycle, citing a newly identified
counterparty risk not reflected in any of the model's inputs. Is this a correct application of "AI proposes;
the professional disposes"?
A. No — the model's ranking should always be followed without override
B. **Yes — a low composite score reflects the absence of a quantitative signal in the model's inputs, not the
   absence of anything requiring a professional's attention** ✔
C. No — composite scores should never be used if they can be overridden
D. Yes, but only because the model made a calculation error
*Rationale:* 13.5.8 explicitly makes this point: the model's ranking is a triage aid built from the signals it
was given, not a complete picture of portfolio risk; overriding it for a reason outside the model's inputs is
the correct, intended use of professional judgement, not a sign the model is broken (D) or that scores should
never be overridable (C).

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using Example 13.5.3a's figures, recompute the counterfactual CPI and Method 2 EAC if the uncaught
   duplicate posting had been $15,000 instead of $22,000 (all other Month-5 figures unchanged). Is the
   resulting distortion larger or smaller than in the worked example, and why?
2. Explain, in your own words, why the delay-prediction flag in Example 13.5.2 was valuable specifically
   because of its *timing*, not because it told the team anything the Month-5 EVM report would not eventually
   reveal on its own.
3. A colleague argues that because an AI tool computed Pipeline Segment 4B's EAC family correctly and
   selected the same method (Method 3) the professional would have chosen, the AI-drafted exception-report
   narrative could have been issued without further review. Using Example 13.5.3b, explain why this is
   incorrect.
4. Using the composite-score weighting in Example 13.5.8 (40% RAG severity, 30% ΔEAC-trend severity, 30%
   delay probability), calculate the composite attention score for a hypothetical Segment 4E with RAG
   severity 70, ΔEAC-trend severity 40 and delay probability 50%.

**Answers**

1. Counterfactual AC `= 506,250 + 15,000 = 521,250`; counterfactual CPI `= 405,000/521,250 = 0.777` (rounds to
   0.78); counterfactual EAC (Method 2) `= 600,000 × 521,250/405,000 = 772,222` (rounded). The distortion
   (`772,222 − 750,000 = 22,222`) is **smaller** than the $32,593 distortion in the worked example, because a
   smaller duplicate posting produces a proportionally smaller overstatement of AC, and therefore a smaller
   understatement of CPI and overstatement of EAC — the relationship is directional (a larger uncaught error
   produces a larger EAC distortion), not fixed.
2. The Month-5 EVM report would eventually have surfaced the schedule pressure regardless of the AI flag —
   nothing about the underlying physical reality changed because of it. The flag's value was giving the team a
   full reporting cycle's head start to investigate and respond (opening the resourcing conversation,
   expediting inspection) *before* the position had fully deteriorated into the confirmed Month-5 numbers,
   exactly mirroring KA 5.4.1's argument that the same change costs far less to address when caught early
   rather than late.
3. Correct method selection and correct arithmetic are only part of what an exception-report narrative needs.
   As Example 13.5.3b shows, the AI draft still overstated certainty ("will very likely finish"), did not
   re-confirm the float report's currency, omitted the cross-domain IAS 37 onerous-contract flag entirely
   (something an EVM-only tool has no visibility into), and assigned no named, dated actions — each requiring
   professional knowledge outside the AI's own data feed. A mechanically correct EAC is not the same as a
   report ready to issue.
4. `Composite score = 0.4×70 + 0.3×40 + 0.3×50 = 28 + 12 + 15 = 55`.
