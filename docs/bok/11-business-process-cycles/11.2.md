# Domain 11 — Business Process Cycles (O2C, P2P & the Control Environment)

## KA 11.2 — Procure-to-Pay (P2P)

Every dollar a project spends on materials, plant and subcontracted work reaches the cost ledger through one
disciplined cycle: **Procure-to-Pay (P2P)**. Where Order-to-Cash (O2C, KA 11.1) governs the cash coming *in*
against work Meridian delivers to its client, P2P governs the cash going *out* against goods and services
Meridian receives from its suppliers and subcontractors — together the two cycles form the project's
complete cash-to-cash loop. P2P matters to a project controls professional for two connected reasons: it is
the operational source of the Actual Cost (AC) figure at the centre of every EVM calculation (KA 6.1–6.4),
and it is one of the highest fraud-risk cycles in any organisation, because it authorises real money to leave
the business on the strength of documents that can be falsified, duplicated or manipulated if controls are
weak. This KA follows the cycle end to end — requisition, purchase order, goods/services receipt, the
three-way match, invoice approval and exceptions, the accrual for goods received but not invoiced, the
payment run, and subcontractor payments and retention — continuing **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**'s
**Pipeline Segment 4B** control account (KA 6.1) as the running illustration, treated here as a $600,000
control account within Meridian's $10,000,000 rail-signalling contract (KA 1.1, KA 1.4), with **Al-Noor Site
Services** (KA 1.1, Example 1.1.6b) as the WP4 welding subcontractor — a synthesis flagged for SME
confirmation at the end of this KA. The chapter closes with the cycle's key controls: segregation of duties,
approval limits, vendor master integrity and duplicate-payment prevention.

### 11.2.1 The Procure-to-Pay cycle: purpose and the end-to-end process flow

**Definition and purpose.** Procure-to-Pay is the sequence of controlled steps by which an organisation
identifies a need, commits funds against it, receives the goods or services ordered, verifies and pays the
resulting invoice. Its purpose is twofold: **operational** — get the right goods and services to site, on
time, at an agreed price — and **financial-control** — ensure nothing is paid for that was not properly
authorised, was not actually received, or has already been paid.

**The underlying principle.** No single document in the cycle is trusted on its own. A purchase order alone
only proves an *intention* to buy; a supplier invoice alone only proves the supplier's *claim* to be paid;
neither proves that anything was actually delivered. The cycle's design principle is **independent,
sequential evidence**: a different function generates each of the three core documents (Procurement raises
the PO, an independent receiving function raises the goods/services receipt note, the supplier raises the
invoice), and payment is released only once all three agree — the **three-way match** developed fully in
§11.2.4. This is the same internal-control logic (segregation of custody, authorisation and recording) that
underpins every well-controlled business cycle, formalised further in KA 11.3.

**Formula.** No calculation attaches to the process overview itself; the cycle is defined by its sequence of
control gates, summarised in Fig 11.2.1.

**AI in this topic.** Process-mining tools reconstruct the *actual* sequence of events from system

a deviation is not automatically a failure (an emergency purchase may legitimately need an expedited path),
so a professional must review each flag before concluding whether it needs tightening or is a legitimate
fast-track route.

### 11.2.2 Purchase requisition, budgetary control and the purchase order

**Definition and purpose.** A **purchase requisition** is an internal request to buy specified goods or
services, raised by the party with the need (typically a site or project team) and routed for approval
*before* any commitment is made externally. Once approved, it converts into a **purchase order (PO)** — a
formal, authorised offer to a named supplier, stating price, quantity, specification, delivery terms and
payment terms, which becomes a binding commitment once accepted. The requisition-then-PO split exists so
that budgetary control is exercised *before* the organisation is contractually committed, not after.

**The underlying principle.** Issuing a PO creates a **commitment**: a real, forward financial exposure that
is not yet an actual cost, because nothing has yet been received. Commitment accounting exists precisely so
that a control account's *remaining spending capacity* reflects orders already placed, not only cash already
spent — otherwise two people could each, in good faith, commit the same uncommitted-looking budget twice.
This is the first stage of the commitment → accrual → actual cost-control cycle developed fully in KA 5.2.

**Formula.**

`Available (uncommitted) budget = Approved budget − Actual costs posted to date − Open commitments (unreceived PO balances) − Accruals not yet converted to a PO`

**Example 11.2.2 — checking available budget before approving a subcontract requisition.**

*Scenario.* Work Package 4 (pipe-laying & welding) on Pipeline Segment 4B has an approved budget of
$120,000 (KA 6.1 §6.1.4). By the requisition date, Meridian's own labour and plant have completed the Prep
and Lay milestones at an actual cost of $70,000. No other commitments are currently open against WP4. The
site team raises Purchase Requisition **PR-4B-021** for the remaining Weld and Test scope, to be
subcontracted, at an estimated value of **$50,000**.

*Required.* Determine the available budget before the requisition, and whether it can be approved.

*Working.*

`Available budget = 120,000 − 70,000 (actual) − 0 (open commitments) − 0 (accruals) = 50,000`

Requested requisition value = $50,000 ≤ available budget $50,000 → **passes the budgetary check**, exactly
at the limit.

*Answer.* PR-4B-021 is approved for **$50,000**, exhausting WP4's remaining budget entirely.

*Interpretation.* The check passes, but leaves **zero headroom** for further growth on WP4 without a formal
change or a contingency draw (KA 3.1) — "passes the budget check" and "has spare capacity" are not the same
thing. On approval, PR-4B-021 converts into **Purchase Order SC-PO-4B-014**, issued to Al-Noor Site Services
for **1,000 linear metres of pipe welding at $50/m = $50,000** (SAR 187,500 at an illustrative
SAR 3.75 = USD 1), with a 10% retention clause (§11.2.8). The negotiated PO price need not equal the internal
EV milestone weights it replaces (Weld $36,000 + Test $24,000 = $60,000, KA 6.1 §6.1.4) — a subcontract price
is a commercial outcome; a control-account weighting is a planning estimate.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Approving a requisition against gross budget without netting off open commitments,
double-spending budget already earmarked. (2) Confusing an approved PO with an actual cost — it is exposure,
not expenditure, until received (§11.2.3–11.2.4). (3) Splitting a requirement into smaller requisitions to
stay under an approval limit (§11.2.9).

**AI in this topic.** Budget-checking is readily automated within an ERP workflow, blocking a requisition
that would breach available budget — a reliable rules-based control, not really an AI application. AI adds
genuine value in *forecasting* whether a control account is trending toward exhausted headroom before it
happens, from historical commitment and burn-rate patterns (KA 13.5) — a signal to investigate, not a
substitute for the budget-check control itself.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 (management reserve and contingency); KA 5.2 (the full commitment → accrual →
actual cycle); KA 6.1 §6.1.4 (WP4's milestone weighting); §11.2.9 (approval limits and order-splitting).

### 11.2.3 Goods and services receipt (the GRN): the receiving control

**Definition and purpose.** A **goods/services receipt note (GRN)** is the record, raised by a function
independent of both procurement and accounts payable, confirming that specified goods or services have
actually been physically received, inspected and (where relevant) quality-verified against the PO. For
services and subcontracted work, the equivalent is often called a **service entry sheet** or **work
verification**, but it serves the identical control purpose. A GRN may be **full** (the entire PO quantity)
or **partial** (a portion, with the balance remaining open on the PO).

**The underlying principle.** The GRN is the cycle's evidentiary anchor: it is the one document generated by
someone with direct, independent sight of the goods or work itself, rather than of the paperwork describing
it. Without an independent GRN, the organisation would be relying on the supplier's own invoice as proof
that the supplier delivered what it is billing for — the receiving control exists specifically so that never
happens.

**Formula.** `GRN value (this receipt) = Quantity/extent verified this event × PO unit rate`, with
`Cumulative GRN value = Σ (GRN values, all receipt events to date)`, capped at the PO's total value.

**Example 11.2.3 — a partial GRN on the Al-Noor welding subcontract.**

*Scenario.* Under PO SC-PO-4B-014 (1,000 m of pipe welding at $50/m, $50,000 total), Meridian's site
engineer inspects and certifies **500 m** of welding as physically complete and passed non-destructive
testing at the Month-3 data date, raising **GRN-4B-142**.

*Required.* The GRN value and the PO balance still open.

*Working.* `GRN-4B-142 value = 500 m × $50/m = $25,000`

`Remaining open PO balance = 50,000 − 25,000 = $25,000` (the remaining 500 m, not yet welded/received)

*Answer.* Partial GRN of **$25,000**; **$25,000** of PO SC-PO-4B-014 remains open.

*Interpretation.* This is the same $25,000 of verified welding work referenced as the "accrual-lag pitfall"
in KA 6.1 §6.1.3 — this KA now shows the underlying P2P mechanics that produce that figure, developed further
into an accrual in §11.2.6.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Recording a GRN for the full PO quantity when only part has been received — the
single most damaging receiving failure, since it removes the very check the GRN exists to provide.
(2) Delaying GRN entry until the invoice arrives and copying its quantity — this collapses two independent
documents into one, defeating the match before it starts (§11.2.5). (3) A "rubber-stamp" GRN with no genuine
inspection — a particular risk for services, where "receipt" is a judgement, not a countable delivery.

**AI in this topic.** Computer-vision and IoT-linked tools (site cameras, GPS/timestamped progress photos)
can corroborate a claimed physical receipt, and document-intelligence tools can auto-populate a draft GRN
from a delivery note. These speed up receiving but do not replace human inspection judgement — for welded or
buried work in particular, the point of receipt may be the only chance to catch a defect before it is
covered up, so an AI-populated GRN still requires the inspecting engineer's sign-off.

**Cross-references.** §11.2.2 (the PO this GRN is measured against); §11.2.4 (the three-way match); §11.2.6
(the GRNI accrual this partial GRN generates); KA 6.1 §6.1.3 (the accrual-lag pitfall, resolved here).

### 11.2.4 The three-way match: PO ↔ GRN ↔ invoice

**Definition and purpose.** The **three-way match** compares the purchase order, the goods/services receipt
note and the supplier invoice on price, quantity and value before an invoice is approved for payment. A
simpler **two-way match** (PO and invoice only, without a GRN) is sometimes used for low-value or
non-inventory purchases where a separate receiving step would add more control cost than benefit; the
three-way match is the standard for materials and subcontracted works, where the sums and the risk of
disputed receipt both justify the extra step.

**The underlying principle.** Each of the three documents is independently generated and independently
capable of being wrong or fraudulent; requiring all three to agree, within a defined tolerance, makes it
very difficult for a single error or a single act of manipulation to result in an incorrect payment, because
it would have to be replicated consistently across documents raised by different functions.

**Formula.**

`Match passes ⇔ (|Invoice unit price − PO unit price| / PO unit price) ≤ price tolerance AND (Invoice quantity ≤ cumulative GRN quantity received) AND (Invoice value ≤ remaining PO value)`

Price tolerance is a policy parameter (commonly 1–2%, to absorb legitimate indexed or escalation-clause
rounding); quantity tolerance is commonly **zero** — an invoice for more than has been verified received is
a hold, not a rounding matter.

**Example 11.2.4 — a clean three-way match on a materials order.**

*Scenario.* Under WP2 (pipe & valve procurement, $150,000 budget, KA 6.1 §6.1.4), Meridian issues
**PO MAT-PO-4B-009** to **Al Rashid Valve & Fittings Trading** for 200 gate valves at $250/unit = $50,000.
Receiving records **GRN-4B-098** for all 200 valves, inspected and accepted. Al Rashid submits
**Invoice INV-2216** for 200 valves at $250/unit = $50,000.

*Required.* Perform the three-way match and state the outcome.

*Working.*

| Check | PO | GRN | Invoice | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unit price | $250 | — | $250 | 0% variance ✓ |
| Quantity | 200 | 200 | 200 | Invoice qty ≤ GRN qty ✓ |
| Value | $50,000 | $50,000 | $50,000 | Invoice value ≤ remaining PO value ✓ |

*Answer.* All three checks pass with **zero variance** — the invoice is approved automatically, without
manual intervention ("touchless" processing).

*Interpretation.* A clean match like this is what a well-designed process should deliver for the large
majority of routine, low-risk purchases, freeing AP's attention for the exceptions that genuinely need it
(§11.2.5).

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Matching on value alone without checking price and quantity independently — two

tolerances so wide "to reduce exceptions" that the match stops being a genuine control. (3) Matching against
the *original* PO price when an approved amendment exists — the match must run against the *current* PO.

**AI in this topic.** Invoice/PO matching is one of the most mature, widely deployed RPA/AI applications in
finance operations: OCR and document-intelligence tools extract invoice data, and rules- or ML-based matching
engines run the comparison automatically, materially reducing manual effort for the clean-match majority. The
professional's role shifts rather than disappears: someone must set and periodically review the tolerance
thresholds, and a mis-configured engine (tolerances too wide, a document type silently ignored) fails
silently and at scale — which is exactly why the matching configuration itself needs periodic control testing
(KA 11.3).

**Cross-references.** §11.2.2 (the PO); §11.2.3 (the GRN); §11.2.5 (what happens when the match fails);
§11.2.9 (segregation of duties underpinning why three *independent* documents matter).

### 11.2.5 Invoice approval, match exceptions and approval limits

**Definition and purpose.** Where the three-way match fails any of its checks, the invoice is placed on a
**hold** (an exception) rather than approved, and routed for investigation and resolution before payment can
proceed. Separately, every invoice and PO — matched or not — must be approved within a **delegation of
authority (DOA)**: a table of pre-agreed value thresholds stating who may approve spending of what size.

**The underlying principle.** A held invoice is the match doing exactly what it is designed to do — it is
not itself a process failure, provided exceptions are resolved promptly and on proper evidence. The DOA
exists for a distinct reason: the three-way match confirms *what was ordered was received and billed
correctly*; it says nothing about whether the *original* spending decision was itself properly authorised at
an appropriate level. Both controls are needed together.

**Formula.** No single formula; a representative illustrative DOA structure:

| Value band (USD) | Approver |
|---|---|
| Up to $10,000 | Site engineer / procurement officer |
| $10,001–$100,000 | Project controls manager / procurement lead |
| $100,001–$500,000 | Project director |
| Above $500,000 | Regional finance director **and** project director (dual approval) |

**Example 11.2.5 — a quantity-variance exception and its resolution.**

*Scenario.* A follow-on order, **PO MAT-PO-4B-011**, is issued to Al Rashid Valve & Fittings Trading for 100
further valves at $250/unit = $25,000. **GRN-4B-101** records only **90** valves physically received (10
short-shipped). Al Rashid nonetheless submits **Invoice INV-2241** billing all **100** valves = $25,000.
Meridian applies a zero-tolerance quantity policy.

*Required.* State the match outcome and the resolution path.

*Working.* Invoiced quantity (100) > GRN quantity (90) → variance of 10 units / $2,500 (10% of invoice
value) → **breaches the zero-quantity-tolerance policy** → invoice held as a **quantity-variance exception**,
routed to procurement and receiving for investigation. Two weeks later, the outstanding 10 valves are
delivered; receiving raises a second GRN line for 10 units, bringing cumulative GRN to 100 units = $25,000,
which now matches the invoice exactly → the hold is released and the invoice is approved.

*Answer.* Invoice **held**, then **released for the full $25,000** once the GRN was completed to match actual
receipt.

*Interpretation.* The correct control response was to hold the invoice and update the GRN only once the
remaining goods genuinely arrived — never to adjust the GRN merely to agree with the invoice, which would
convert an independent control into a rubber stamp.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) "Force-matching" — overriding a genuine variance to clear a backlog, defeating the
control's purpose. (2) Paying a disputed invoice in full "to keep the supplier happy" instead of paying the
matched portion and holding the rest. (3) A DOA that exists on paper but is not enforced as a hard system
control — a policy with no system enforcement is not a control.

**AI in this topic.** ML-based triage tools can classify held invoices by likely root cause (price
escalation, short-shipment, duplicate) and prioritise the queue, learning routine, recurring resolution
patterns. This speeds up resolution but should never auto-clear an exception without human sign-off — the
exception queue is, by definition, where the highest-risk items concentrate, and that is precisely where an
automated override carries the most risk if it is wrong.

**Cross-references.** §11.2.4 (the match this exception failed); §11.2.9 (approval-limit breaches through
order-splitting); KA 11.3 (the fuller control-testing treatment of exception handling).

### 11.2.6 Accruals for goods and services received not invoiced (GRNI)

**Definition and purpose.** Where a GRN exists but the matching supplier invoice has not yet arrived at a
reporting period-end, the value of goods or services received must still be recognised as a cost, through a
**goods-received-not-invoiced (GRNI) accrual** — known in many ERP systems as a **GR/IR (goods
receipt/invoice receipt) clearing account**. This is the P2P cycle's specific application of the accrual
concept introduced generally in KA 1.3 §1.3.3.

**The underlying principle.** The matching concept (KA 1.3 §1.3.2) requires a cost to be recognised in the
period the related work was performed, not the later period in which an invoice happens to arrive. A GRNI
accrual is normally an ordinary **accrued expense**, not an IAS 37 **provision** — its amount is reliably
known from the GRN's measured quantity at the agreed PO rate, and its timing (an imminent supplier invoice)
is not genuinely in doubt, so it does not need to clear IAS 37's three-part recognition test (KA 1.4 §1.4.1).
A common misconception is treating every unbilled cost as requiring provision-style judgement; a GRNI
accrual from a measured GRN and a firm PO rate is normally the more straightforward, high-confidence case.

**Formula.**

`GRNI accrual required (period-end, per open PO line) = Cumulative GRN value to date − Cumulative invoice value matched/booked to date`

Posted using the same reversing-entry mechanism as any other accrual (KA 1.3 §1.3.3, §1.3.5): the accrual is
raised at period-end, reversed at the start of the following period, and the actual invoice is then booked
in full when it arrives.

**Example 11.2.6 — the Al-Noor welding GRNI accrual at the Month-3 close.**

*Scenario.* Continuing Example 11.2.3: GRN-4B-142 has certified 500 m ($25,000) of welding under
PO SC-PO-4B-014. At the Month-3 close, **no invoice has yet been received** — Al-Noor's typical invoice lag
is 3–4 weeks (KA 6.1 §6.1.3).

*Required.* The GRNI accrual required, and its journal entry.

*Working.*

`GRNI accrual = 25,000 (cumulative GRN) − 0 (invoice booked) = 25,000`

Month-3 close: `Dr WP4 Weld cost 25,000 / Cr GRNI accrual (accrued expenses) 25,000`

*Answer.* A **$25,000** GRNI accrual is raised at the Month-3 close.

*Interpretation.* This is precisely the $25,000 accrual referenced in KA 6.1 §6.1.3, which lifts the
ledger-only AC from $280,000 to the accrual-adjusted **$305,000** that the professional should actually use
in KA 6.2's CV and CPI calculations — this KA has now shown the underlying P2P document trail (a specific
PO, a specific partial GRN) that produces that number, rather than treating it as an abstract adjustment.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Forgetting to reverse the accrual before the actual invoice is booked, double

invoice for months is itself a control signal that warrants investigation, not indefinite carry-forward.

**AI in this topic.** Anomaly-detection tools can scan the GR/IR clearing account portfolio-wide and flag
balances open longer than a defined threshold — a useful completeness and ageing check across hundreds of
lines that manual review would struggle to cover as thoroughly. It cannot determine *why* a balance is stuck
(a dispute, a lost invoice, a data error); that diagnosis, and whether to write off or keep chasing, remains a
professional judgement.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.3 §1.3.3, §1.3.5 (the general accrual and reversing-entry mechanism this applies);

§11.2.8 (the invoice that clears this accrual).

### 11.2.7 The payment run: scheduling and duplicate-payment prevention

**Definition and purpose.** A **payment run** is a scheduled, batched release of approved invoices for
payment, executed by a treasury function independent of accounts payable's invoice-processing role.
Payment terms (commonly stated as a number of days from invoice or certification date, e.g. "net 30")
determine when each invoice becomes due; the payment run groups due invoices into a single, controlled
release rather than paying invoices individually and continuously.

**The underlying principle.** Batching payments and separating the *approval* of an invoice from the
*release* of funds against it are both control devices, not merely administrative convenience: batching
allows a final, independent review of the whole payment file before money moves, and separating approval
from release prevents whoever approved an invoice from also being the one who can send the cash.
**Duplicate-payment prevention** is a specific control within this step: the payment system checks each
invoice for payment against a history of vendor, PO, GRN and invoice-amount combinations already paid,
blocking a second payment against the same underlying transaction.

**Formula.** No calculation; the duplicate check is a matching-key comparison:

`Flag as possible duplicate ⇔ (same vendor) AND (same PO and/or GRN reference) AND (same or near-identical invoice amount), regardless of invoice number`

Matching on amount and reference rather than invoice number alone is essential, because a genuine duplicate
often arrives under a *different* invoice number.

**Example 11.2.7 — a blocked duplicate payment.**

*Scenario.* Al Rashid Valve & Fittings Trading's Invoice INV-2216 ($50,000, against PO MAT-PO-4B-009 and
GRN-4B-098, Example 11.2.4) is paid in full in the Week-6 payment run. Six weeks later, due to a system
re-export error at the supplier, the same shipment is re-submitted as **Invoice INV-2298**, referencing the
same PO and GRN, for the same $50,000.

*Required.* State how the duplicate-payment control responds.

*Working.* The payment system's duplicate check matches INV-2298 against the vendor + PO + GRN + amount
combination already paid under INV-2216, despite the different invoice number, and blocks it from entering
the payment run. Accounts payable investigates, confirms the duplication with the supplier, and cancels
INV-2298.

*Answer.* A **$50,000 duplicate payment is prevented**.

*Interpretation.* Duplicate submissions are usually innocent (system errors, re-sent PDFs) rather than
fraudulent, but the control must catch both causes equally — the check does not need to know *why* a second
invoice arrived to do its job.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) A duplicate check keyed only on invoice number — the single most common gap, since
it misses exactly the re-submitted-under-a-new-number case above. (2) No independent review of the payment
*file* immediately before release, relying solely on earlier invoice-level approvals. (3) Manual payment runs
outside the normal batch/schedule "to help a supplier out," bypassing the review step batching enforces.

**AI in this topic.** ML duplicate-detection models catch near-matches an exact-key rule would miss — a
slightly different amount, a transposed reference, a resubmission under a related invoice number — learned
from historical confirmed-duplicate patterns. False positives are a real cost of a sensitive model, so a
flagged item should delay payment for review, not silently auto-reject; the professional confirms genuine
duplication before cancelling.

**Cross-references.** §11.2.5 (the DOA and match controls upstream of payment); §11.2.8 (subcontractor
payment and retention within the payment run); §11.2.9 (segregation between payment release and the rest of
the cycle).

### 11.2.8 Subcontractor payments, retention and the integrated mini-case

**Definition and purpose.** Subcontractor payments follow the same PO/GRN/invoice/match/payment mechanics as
any other P2P transaction, with one addition specific to construction and engineering subcontracts:
**retention** — a percentage of each certified payment withheld by the main contractor as security against
defective or incomplete work, released later in agreed tranches (commonly at practical completion and at the
end of a defects-liability period). This mini-case brings §11.2.2–11.2.7 together on a single transaction.

**The underlying principle.** Retention gives the paying party continuing financial leverage over a
subcontractor after most of the value has already changed hands — without it, a subcontractor with no
further work outstanding would have little practical incentive to return and fix defects found after
handover. Retention terms are contract-specific (commonly 5–10% of certified value, illustrative here and
flagged for SME confirmation) and are developed fully alongside contract administration in the forthcoming
KA 7.2 and KA 7.4.

**Formula.**

`Retention withheld (this certificate) = Retention % × Certified/invoiced value (this certificate)`
`Net cash payable (this certificate) = Certified/invoiced value − Retention withheld`
`Cumulative retention held = Retention % × Cumulative certified value to date`, subject to any contractual cap.

**Example 11.2.8 — from partial GRN to payable: the Al-Noor welding subcontract, Month 4.**

*Scenario.* Continuing Examples 11.2.3 and 11.2.6 (PO SC-PO-4B-014, 1,000 m at $50/m; GRN-4B-142 for 500 m =
$25,000; $25,000 GRNI accrual raised at the Month-3 close and reversed at the start of Month 4 per
§11.2.6). In Month 4, Al-Noor Site Services submits **Invoice AN-0233** for the 500 m already received:
500 m × $50/m = **$25,000**. The subcontract carries a **10% retention** clause.

*Required.* (a) Perform the three-way match; (b) journalise the invoice; (c) compute the payment split; (d)
state the PO balance remaining open.

*Working.*

(a) *Three-way match:* price $50/m (PO) = $50/m (invoice) ✓; invoice quantity 500 m ≤ cumulative GRN
quantity 500 m ✓; invoice value $25,000 ≤ remaining PO value $50,000 ✓ — **match passes** with zero
variance.

(b) *Journal entry, Month 4 (actual invoice booked, following the reversal already made per §11.2.6):*
`Dr WP4 Weld cost 25,000 / Cr Trade payable — Al-Noor Site Services 25,000`

Net cost recognised across Months 3–4: $25,000 (Month 3 accrual) − $25,000 (Month 4 reversal) + $25,000
(Month 4 actual) = **$25,000 in total, recognised once**, in the period the work was actually performed.

(c) *Retention and payment:*
`Retention withheld = 10% × 25,000 = 2,500`
`Net cash payable = 25,000 − 2,500 = 22,500`

Payment-run entry: `Dr Trade payable — Al-Noor 25,000 / Cr Cash 22,500 / Cr Retention payable — Al-Noor 2,500`

Check: `22,500 + 2,500 = 25,000` ✓ — agrees with the invoiced/payable amount.

(d) *Remaining PO balance:* `50,000 − 25,000 (invoiced to date) = 25,000` still open, covering the remaining
500 m of welding to be received, invoiced and paid on the same cycle.

*Answer.* Al-Noor Site Services is paid **$22,500 in cash**; **$2,500** is carried forward as retention
payable; **$25,000** of PO SC-PO-4B-014 remains open.

*Interpretation.* The full chain — partial GRN, GRNI accrual, invoice, three-way match, payable, retention,
cash — reconciles exactly at every step, and the $25,000 accrual that first appeared as an EVM adjustment in
KA 6.1 §6.1.3 has now been traced through to an actual cash payment and a retention balance on Meridian's
books.

**Example 11.2.8b — retention release (illustrative).** If the full $50,000 subcontract is eventually
invoiced in total, cumulative retention withheld = 10% × 50,000 = **$5,000**. Under a common (illustrative,
contract-specific) release convention, 50% is released at practical completion (**$2,500**) and the
remaining 50% at the end of a 12-month defects-liability period (**$2,500**), assuming no defects are
outstanding at that point.

### 11.2.9 Key controls across the P2P cycle: segregation of duties and vendor master integrity

**Definition and purpose.** This topic draws together the P2P-specific key controls threaded through
§11.2.2–11.2.8 — **segregation of duties**, **approval limits**, **vendor master file integrity** and
**duplicate-payment prevention** — into a single control view, applying internal-control concepts (of the
kind formalised in frameworks such as **COSO's Internal Control – Integrated Framework**, at an awareness
level) specifically to this cycle. The fuller, cross-cycle treatment of internal control and the audit trail
(covering both O2C and P2P together) is developed in KA 11.3; this topic keeps to what is specific to P2P.

**The underlying principle.** No single control in this KA is sufficient on its own; each addresses a
different failure mode, and they are designed to work as a layered set:

- **Segregation of duties (SoD).** No one individual should hold two or more of: *raising* a requisition,
  *approving* a PO/invoice, *receiving* goods or services (the GRN), and *releasing* payment. Combining any
  two of these removes the independent check the other provides.
- **Approval limits (DOA, §11.2.5).** Ensures spending decisions are made at a level of seniority
  proportionate to their size, independently of whether the transaction later matches correctly.
- **Vendor master file integrity.** The supplier master file (names, addresses, and critically, **bank
  account details**) is a high-value fraud target: a fraudulent change to a genuine supplier's bank details
  redirects an otherwise entirely legitimate, correctly matched payment. Controls include independent
  verification of any bank-detail change (a callback to a previously known contact number, never a number
  supplied with the change request itself), dual approval for master-file changes, restricted write-access,
  and periodic audits for duplicate or look-alike vendor records.
- **Duplicate-payment prevention (§11.2.7).** Catches the same underlying transaction being paid twice,
  regardless of cause.

**Formula.** No calculation; Fig 11.2.9 sets out an illustrative segregation-of-duties matrix.

**Example 11.2.9 — a control-failure vignette: order-splitting.**

*Scenario.* A procurement officer holds a personal PO-approval limit of $25,000 (§11.2.5's DOA). A genuine
requirement for $60,000 of pipe fittings arises. Rather than route it to the project controls manager (whose
authority covers $10,001–$100,000), the officer raises **three** separate purchase orders of $20,000 each,
to three near-identical vendor codes for the same underlying supplier, each individually within their own
$25,000 limit.

*Required.* Identify the control failure and a detective response.

*Working/Answer.* This is **order-splitting** (or "structuring"): a deliberate breach of the approval-limit
control by fragmenting a requirement to stay under an individual's own authority, often paired with a
vendor-master weakness (near-identical codes for what is really one supplier). A standard detective test —
screening for multiple same-week POs to one supplier or group, each just under a threshold — is designed to
surface exactly this pattern, which rarely shows up from reviewing any one PO alone.

*Interpretation.* This is why approval-limit testing routinely looks at *patterns across* transactions, not
only individual compliance — a control can be technically satisfied on every document and still be
circumvented in substance.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) A DOA/SoD policy that exists on paper but is not enforced as a hard system control
(§11.2.5's pitfall, restated cycle-wide). (2) Vendor master-file changes approved by the same person who also
raises or approves POs to that vendor. (3) Assuming a strong three-way match makes segregation of duties less
important — the match checks whether a *specific transaction* is priced and quantified correctly; it does
nothing to stop a fabricated transaction where PO, GRN and invoice are all generated by the same colluding
individual.

**AI in this topic.** Process-mining and network-analysis tools can screen vendor master and transaction data
at scale for the patterns above — near-duplicate vendor records, clusters of same-week POs just under a
threshold, an employee's own bank details matching a vendor record — far faster than manual sample testing.
These generate leads requiring investigation, not proof; a false positive is common enough that findings must
be corroborated before any disciplinary action follows — **AI proposes; the professional (with HR/legal/audit
as appropriate) disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 11.3 (the full cross-cycle internal-control and audit-trail treatment); §11.2.5
(DOA thresholds); §11.2.7 (duplicate-payment prevention); §11.2.1 (why independent documents are the cycle's
foundational design principle).

### Key terms

- **Purchase requisition** — an internal request to buy, approved before external commitment is made.
- **Purchase order (PO)** — an authorised offer to a named supplier stating price, quantity and terms;
  creates a commitment once accepted.
- **Commitment accounting** — recognising an issued, unreceived PO as a forward exposure against available
  budget, distinct from an actual cost.
- **Goods/services receipt note (GRN)** — independent evidence of physical/verified receipt; full or
  partial. Also called a service entry sheet for services/subcontract work.
- **Three-way / two-way match** — comparing PO, GRN and invoice (three-way) or PO and invoice only
  (two-way, no GRN, for low-value purchases) before approving payment.
- **Match tolerance / exception (hold)** — the policy-set variance within which an invoice auto-approves;
  a failed match is held and investigated before payment.
- **Delegation of authority (DOA)** — the table of value-based approval thresholds for requisitions, POs and
  invoices.
- **GRNI accrual / GR-IR clearing account** — an accrued liability for goods/services received but not yet
  invoiced; an ordinary accrued expense (KA 1.3, KA 1.4), distinct from an IAS 37 provision.
- **Payment run** — a scheduled, batched, independently released set of approved payments.
- **Duplicate-payment prevention** — matching-key controls blocking a second payment against an
  already-paid transaction.
- **Retention** — a percentage of certified subcontractor payment withheld as security, released in later
  tranches (commonly at practical completion and the end of the defects-liability period; KA 7.2).
- **Vendor/supplier master file** — the system record of approved suppliers, including bank details; a key
  fraud-control focus.
- **Segregation of duties (SoD)** — no individual holds two or more of requisitioning, approving, receiving
  and paying.
- **Order-splitting (structuring)** — fragmenting a requirement into smaller transactions to evade an
  approval limit.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 11.2.1 [level: recall]**
In the Procure-to-Pay cycle, what does the "three-way match" specifically compare?
A. The purchase requisition, the budget and the general ledger
B. The purchase order, the goods/services receipt (GRN) and the supplier invoice ✔
C. The supplier invoice, the payment run and the bank statement
D. The vendor master file, the purchase order and the contract

*Rationale:* the three-way match compares PO, GRN and invoice (§11.2.4). A, C and D each substitute a
plausible-sounding but incorrect set of documents.

**MCQ 11.2.2 [level: application — numerical]**
A subcontractor PO is for 1,000 m of pipe welding at $50/m. At the period-end data date, a GRN records 500 m
certified complete; no invoice has yet been received. What accrual for goods/services received not invoiced
(GRNI) should be raised?
A. $50,000
B. $0 — no accrual until an invoice is received
C. $25,000 ✔
D. $12,500

*Rationale:* GRNI accrual = cumulative GRN value − cumulative invoice value booked = (500 × $50) − $0 =
$25,000 (§11.2.6). A wrongly uses the full PO value; B contradicts the matching/accrual concept (KA 1.3);
D incorrectly halves the GRN value.

**MCQ 11.2.3 [level: application]**
An invoice is received for 100 units against a PO priced at $250/unit, but the GRN on file shows only 90
units received. Under a standard P2P matching policy with zero tolerance on quantity, what is the correct
next step?
A. Pay the invoice in full because the unit price matches the PO
B. Hold the invoice as a quantity-variance exception until the discrepancy is investigated and resolved ✔
C. Automatically re-issue a new PO for the extra 10 units
D. Post the full invoice and amend the GRN retrospectively so it agrees with the invoice

*Rationale:* a quantity variance beyond tolerance must be held, not paid or force-matched (§11.2.5). A
ignores the unmatched 10 units; C does not follow from an unresolved variance; D destroys the GRN's purpose
as independent evidence of receipt.

**MCQ 11.2.4 [level: analysis]**
A procurement officer with a personal PO-approval limit of $25,000 splits a genuine $60,000 requirement into
three separate $20,000 purchase orders to three near-identical vendor codes, avoiding the higher-level
approval a single $60,000 PO would require. Which control failure does this best illustrate?
A. A vendor master file integrity failure only
B. A breach of the approval-limit (delegation of authority) control through order-splitting, with a
   related vendor-master weakness ✔
C. A three-way match failure
D. A duplicate-payment control failure

*Rationale:* this is the order-splitting/structuring pattern that circumvents delegation-of-authority limits
(§11.2.9); it is not primarily a matching or duplicate-payment issue (C, D), though the near-identical vendor
codes also point to a vendor-master weakness — B captures both correctly, A alone is incomplete.

**MCQ 11.2.5 [level: application — numerical]**
A subcontractor invoice of $25,000 is approved for payment under a subcontract with 10% retention. What net
cash amount is paid to the subcontractor, and what is credited to retention payable?
A. $22,500 cash; $2,500 retention ✔
B. $25,000 cash; $0 retention
C. $20,000 cash; $5,000 retention
D. $2,500 cash; $22,500 retention

*Rationale:* retention withheld = 10% × 25,000 = $2,500; net cash = 25,000 − 2,500 = $22,500 (§11.2.8). B
ignores retention entirely; C and D misapply the percentage.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using the Al-Noor Site Services welding subcontract mini-case, explain why the $25,000 GRNI accrual
   raised in Month 3 required no additional cost entry when the matching invoice was processed in Month 4.
2. Explain, with reference to KA 1.4's recognition boundary, why a routine GRNI accrual is normally treated
   as an accrued expense rather than an IAS 37 provision.
3. A quantity-variance exception is discovered where an invoiced quantity exceeds the GRN quantity. Explain
   why the correct control response is to hold the invoice and investigate, rather than retrospectively
   amending the GRN to match the invoice.
4. Describe two distinct purposes served by segregating "who approves a purchase order" from "who releases
   payment," and state one specific fraud or error each separation is designed to prevent.

**Answers**

1. At the GRN, the $25,000 of verified welding work was already recognised as a cost (Dr WP4 Weld cost 25,000
   / Cr GRNI accrual 25,000). Once the accrual was reversed at the start of Month 4 and the matching $25,000
   invoice was then booked in full, the net cost recognised across the two months was $25,000 in total —
   the cost was not repeated, only reclassified from an accrual to a trade payable (§11.2.6, §11.2.8).
2. IAS 37 governs liabilities of *uncertain* timing or amount. A GRNI accrual's amount is reliably known from
   a GRN reflecting a specific, measured quantity received at an agreed PO rate, and its timing (an imminent
   supplier invoice) is not genuinely in doubt — it therefore sits in KA 1.4's "accrued expense" category,
   not the provision category, and does not need IAS 37's three-part recognition test (§11.2.6, cross-ref
   KA 1.4 §1.4.1).
3. The GRN is independent evidence, generated by a function separate from the one that will pay the invoice,
   that goods or services were actually received. Amending the GRN to match the invoice would remove that
   independence entirely, converting the receiving control into a rubber stamp for whatever the supplier
   chooses to bill (§11.2.5).
4. Separating PO/invoice approval from payment release prevents a single individual both committing the
   organisation to a purchase (or approving an invoice against it) and then also being able to release funds
   against it — for example, approving a fabricated or inflated invoice and then also authorising its
   payment, with no independent check at any point in the chain (§11.2.9).
