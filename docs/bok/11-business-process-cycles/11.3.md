# Domain 11 — Business Process Cycles (O2C, P2P & the Control Environment)

## KA 11.3 — Internal control & segregation of duties

KA 11.1 and KA 11.2 walk the Order-to-Cash (O2C) and Procure-to-Pay (P2P) cycles end-to-end, from a customer
order or purchase requisition through to cash receipt or payment, noting the principal control points along
each cycle. This Knowledge Area steps back from any single cycle to examine the **control environment** that
makes those cycle-level controls trustworthy: why internal control exists, what a control is trying to
achieve (its objectives), how controls act (preventive, detective, corrective), the specific controls a
project-controls professional relies on most in project finance (approval limits, authorisation matrices,
reconciliations), and the single most important structural safeguard against error and fraud going
undetected — **segregation of duties (SoD)** across the four functions of authorisation, recording, custody
and reconciliation. It closes with the practical reality that small teams and remote site offices often
cannot achieve textbook SoD, how to design compensating controls when they cannot, and how a control's
existence is proven after the fact through the audit trail and controls assurance testing. The chapter
continues **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC** and its **Pipeline Segment 4B** project (Domain 1, Domain 6; BAC
$600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1), applying these concepts to Meridian's own
procurement and site organisation.

### 11.3.1 The purpose of internal control and the control environment

**Definition & purpose.** Internal control is the process, designed and operated by an organisation's people
and management, that provides *reasonable* — not absolute — assurance that the organisation will achieve its
objectives across three categories: effective and efficient operations, reliable reporting, and compliance
with applicable laws and regulations. This definition follows the internationally recognised **COSO Internal
Control – Integrated Framework**, published by the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway
Commission (first issued in 1992 and most recently updated in 2013), which remains the standard reference
framework for internal control in professional accounting and audit practice.

**The underlying principle.** COSO organises internal control into five interrelated components: the
**control environment** (the foundation — governance, ethics, competence and assigned authority);
**risk assessment**; **control activities** (approval limits, SoD, reconciliations — the main subject of this
KA); **information and communication**; and **monitoring activities** (§11.3.7) — underpinned by seventeen
supporting principles (e.g. a demonstrated commitment to integrity and ethical values, and clear structures,
reporting lines and authorities). Two limitations matter from the outset: internal control gives
*reasonable*, not absolute, assurance (cost is weighed against benefit, and collusion can defeat almost any
design), and no combination of control activities compensates for a weak control environment — a
well-designed authorisation matrix that nobody is held to is a control in name only.

**Matrix.** No calculation attaches to this topic; the mapping below shows how Meridian's actual control
activities sit within COSO's five components, previewing where the rest of this KA goes:

| COSO component | Meridian example |
|---|---|
| Control environment | Board-approved code of conduct; a documented delegation of authority (DoA); appropriately qualified commercial and finance staff |
| Risk assessment | Annual review of project-level control risk, including the small-site-team SoD risk examined in §11.3.6 |
| Control activities | The authorisation matrix (§11.3.4), the three-way match and other reconciliations (§11.3.4), and segregation of the four incompatible functions (§11.3.5) |
| Information & communication | ERP workflow logs, weekly exception reports, a confidential whistleblowing channel |
| Monitoring activities | The internal audit programme and the controls-testing cycle (§11.3.7) |

**Example 11.3.1 — classifying three Meridian control failures by objective category.** *Scenario.* An

*objective* affected is the first step in deciding who within Meridian's governance structure (operations

### 11.3.2 Control objectives for transactions: existence, completeness, accuracy, authorisation and cut-off

**Definition & purpose.** A **control objective** states, for a class of transaction, the specific risk a
control is designed to address. Five control objectives recur throughout the O2C and P2P cycles and mirror
the transaction-level assertions used in external audit practice (the International Standards on Auditing
(ISA) framework applies closely related assertions when testing financial statement line items):
**existence** (a recorded transaction genuinely occurred — nothing fictitious is on the books);
**completeness** (every transaction that occurred *has* been recorded — nothing real is missing);
**accuracy** (recorded amounts, quantities and account coding are correct); **authorisation** (the
transaction was approved by someone with the appropriate delegated authority before it was committed); and
**cut-off** (the transaction is recorded in the correct accounting period).

**The underlying principle.** Existence and completeness are mirror-image risks — one guards against
recording too much (fictitious items), the other against recording too little (real items omitted) — and a
control for one does not automatically address the other. Authorisation is the only objective that looks
*forward*, at the point of commitment, and is therefore usually a **preventive** control (§11.3.3); the other
four are more often tested **detectively**, after recording. Cut-off connects to the matching concept (KA
1.3): a transaction can satisfy every other objective perfectly and still misstate the period if recorded in
the wrong month.

**Example 11.3.2 — testing five control objectives on one Pipeline Segment 4B transaction.** *Scenario.*
Purchase order 4B-0091 is raised for 400 units of steel pipe at $120/unit ($48,000 total), for delivery to
the Pipeline Segment 4B site in the current reporting month. *Required.* State, for each control objective,
the specific question a controls professional would ask and the evidence that answers it. *Working/Answer.*

### Fig 11.3.2 — Control-objective table: PO 4B-0091

| Control objective | Question asked | Evidence / control used |
|---|---|---|
| Existence | Did the 400 units genuinely arrive at site? | A signed goods received note (GRN) evidencing physical receipt |
| Completeness | Has every unit actually received been captured in the GRN and cost ledger, with none omitted? | Reconciliation of the GRN log to site delivery/gate records |
| Accuracy | Do the recorded quantity, unit price and cost-code match the PO and the supplier's invoice? | Three-way match of PO, GRN and invoice (§11.3.4) |
| Authorisation | Was the $48,000 commitment approved by someone within Meridian's delegated authority *before* the PO was issued to the supplier? | Workflow approval log, tested against the authorisation matrix (§11.3.4) |
| Cut-off | Is the cost recorded in the month the goods were actually received, not the month the invoice happened to arrive? | Comparison of GRN date to the cost ledger posting period (KA 1.3 matching concept) |

**Common pitfalls.** Assuming a single control (e.g. the three-way match) tests every objective equally well
— it strongly tests accuracy and existence, but does little for completeness (an invoice never raised leaves
no trail to catch) or cut-off; treating "authorised" as equivalent to "accurate," when a properly authorised
transaction can still carry a coding or quantity error.

**AI in this topic.** Document-intelligence (OCR/NLP) tools can extract PO, GRN and invoice data and flag
quantity, price or coding mismatches at a speed no manual review can match — a genuine gain against the
accuracy and existence objectives. They are only as reliable as the data fed in (a poorly scanned GRN defeats
completeness regardless of model sophistication), and none can judge whether an unusual but genuine
transaction was authorised *in substance*, versus merely logged as "approved" — that stays with the
professional.

underlying cut-off); KA 11.1, KA 11.2 (objectives applied across the full cycles); §11.3.4 (the controls that
address them in practice).

### 11.3.3 Preventive, detective and corrective controls

**Definition & purpose.** Controls act at three different points in a transaction's life. A **preventive
control** stops an error or an unauthorised transaction *before* it occurs or is committed (an approval
workflow that blocks a PO exceeding a requester's authority). A **detective control** identifies an error or
irregularity *after* it has occurred, so it can be investigated and corrected (a bank reconciliation that
surfaces an unexplained payment). A **corrective control** addresses the *root cause* once a detective
control has found a problem, so the same failure is less likely to recur (tightening a workflow rule after a
bypass is discovered, or a formal write-off and process change following a loss).

**The underlying principle.** No single control type is sufficient alone. Preventive controls are the
cheapest failure to have, but no design is perfect — outages, emergencies and determined circumvention all
create gaps. Detective controls provide a second, independent line of defence that catches what prevention
missed, at the cost of the error existing, however briefly, before discovery. Corrective action closes the
loop — without it, an organisation simply detects the same failure repeatedly. A mature control environment
(§11.3.1) deliberately layers all three.

### Fig 11.3.3 — Preventive, detective and corrective controls across the O2C and P2P cycles

| Control type | Example in P2P (Meridian procurement) | Example in O2C |
|---|---|---|
| Preventive | System-enforced authorisation matrix blocking a PO above the requester's DoA limit (§11.3.4) | Credit-limit check blocking a new sales order for a customer already over their approved credit terms |
| Detective | Monthly three-way match exception report; bank reconciliation | Accounts receivable ageing review; customer statement reconciliation |
| Corrective | Retrospective approval plus a tightened workflow rule following a bypass (Example 11.3.3 below) | Formal bad-debt write-off, following credit-control escalation, plus a revised credit-approval process |

Chart type: a 3×2 reference grid rendered as coloured cells (preventive in dark brand blue `#1D4ED8`,
detective in mid-tone blue, corrective in pale blue), each cell containing the example text above.

*Underlying data:* the table above.

**Example 11.3.3 — a control failure worked through all three control types.** *Scenario.* A site engineer at
Pipeline Segment 4B issues an urgent $18,000 PO for emergency scaffolding directly to a supplier, bypassing
the ERP workflow because the approval screen was down during a system outage — a **preventive control
failure**, since the authorisation matrix (§11.3.4) requires Project Manager approval above $5,000, obtained
here only after the fact. *Required.* Identify the detective control that surfaces this, and a corrective
action. *Working/Answer.* The **detective control** is the weekly exception report (§11.3.6), which flags the
scaffolding invoice as having no matching pre-approved PO; investigation confirms a genuine emergency, and
retrospective approval is obtained. The **corrective action** adds a documented emergency-procurement
procedure to Meridian's DoA policy — a logged verbal/email approval confirmed in the ERP within 24 hours of
system restoration — so a genuine emergency has a controlled path rather than an informal workflow bypass.
*Interpretation.* The fix is not to weaken the authorisation matrix but to give it a controlled fallback,
itself a preventive control for the *next* outage.

**Common pitfalls.** Relying solely on detective controls because they are cheaper than reworking a
preventive process, treating "catch and fix" as normal rather than a signal prevention needs strengthening;
confusing "the report exists" with "the report is reviewed" — an unread exception report is not a functioning
detective control.

**AI in this topic.** Anomaly-detection models trained on historical transaction data can act as a powerful
detective control, flagging patterns (an unusually large petty-cash disbursement, a supplier paid on unusual
terms) faster and across higher volumes than manual review. Detection is not prevention: a model flagging a
payment already released has only shortened the time to discovery, and an unmonitored AI alert queue is no
better a control than an unmonitored manual one.

**Cross-references.** §11.3.4 (the authorisation matrix, Meridian's principal preventive control); §11.3.6
(exception reporting in the compensating-control design); KA 4.2 (variance analysis as a broader detective
control); KA 5.4 (change control, a corrective response); KA 8.4 (Monitoring & Controlling).

### 11.3.4 Key controls in project finance: approval limits, authorisation matrices and reconciliations

**Definition & purpose.** An **authorisation matrix** (or delegation of authority, DoA) sets out, for each
type and value band of transaction, exactly who holds the authority to approve it. A **reconciliation**
independently compares two sets of records that should agree (a subledger to the general ledger, a bank
statement to the cash book, or — in procurement — a purchase order, goods receipt and supplier invoice to
each other) and investigates any difference. Together they are the two most frequently relied-upon controls
in project finance: the authorisation matrix is the primary **preventive** control over commitment (§11.3.3),
and reconciliation is the primary **detective** control over accuracy and completeness (§11.3.2).

**The underlying principle.** Authority should be commensurate with risk: the larger the commitment, the more
senior and independent the required approver, escalating rather than leaving one individual with unlimited
discretion. A reconciliation only has value if performed by someone independent of both the recording and
custody of the item concerned (§11.3.5) — one prepared by the person who posted the entries mainly checks
their own arithmetic, not whether the entries are legitimate.

### Fig 11.3.4 — Meridian's authorisation matrix (approval limits, commitment value)

| Value band (USD) | Value band (SAR, illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) | Approving role |
|---|---|---|
| Up to $5,000 | Up to SAR 18,750 | Site Quantity Surveyor / Site Engineer |
| $5,001 – $25,000 | SAR 18,754 – SAR 93,750 | Project Manager |
| $25,001 – $100,000 | SAR 93,754 – SAR 375,000 | Regional Commercial Manager |
| $100,001 – $500,000 | SAR 375,004 – SAR 1,875,000 | Regional Director, countersigned by the Financial Controller above $250,000 |
| Above $500,000 | Above SAR 1,875,000 | CEO / Board approval |

For a reconciliation-type control, the **three-way match** used in Meridian's P2P cycle is a logical test
rather than a numerical formula:

`Invoice cleared for payment ⇔ (Invoice quantity = GRN quantity) AND (Invoice quantity ≤ PO quantity) AND (Invoice unit price = PO unit price)`

**Example 11.3.4a — applying the authorisation matrix to a variation order.** *Scenario.* A $75,000
pipe-fitting variation order is raised on Pipeline Segment 4B. *Required.* Identify the correct approver.
*Working.* $75,000 falls within the $25,001–$100,000 band ($75,000 × SAR 3.75 = SAR 281,250, also within the
equivalent SAR band). *Answer.* The **Regional Commercial Manager** must approve — not the Site Quantity
Surveyor or the Project Manager, both below the required threshold, and not the Regional Director, whose
authority begins above $100,000. *Interpretation.* Escalating a $75,000 variation to the correct level
matters commercially as well as as a control matter: it also engages KA 7.2's contract-management process
for evaluating and pricing the variation before commitment.

**Example 11.3.4b — the three-way match catching a quantity mismatch.** *Scenario.* Continuing PO 4B-0091
(Example 11.3.2): PO for 400 units of steel pipe at $120/unit ($48,000); the supplier's invoice bills for 400
units at $120/unit ($48,000); but the GRN, signed at site, records only **380 units** actually received.
*Required.* State the outcome of the three-way match and its financial effect. *Working.* Invoice quantity
(400) ≠ GRN quantity (380) → the logical test fails → the invoice is **held**, not cleared for payment,
pending resolution with the supplier. The unit shortfall is `400 − 380 = 20 units`; at $120/unit this
represents `20 × 120 = $2,400` of potential overpayment. *Answer.* The three-way match **prevents a $2,400
overpayment** until the supplier either delivers the missing 20 units or issues a credit note.
*Interpretation.* This is accuracy and existence working together (§11.3.2): the invoice is accurate against
the PO's price, but the *quantity* fails against independent physical evidence (the GRN) — exactly the
discipline of controlling three independent sources rather than trusting any single document.

**Common pitfalls.** Setting authorisation thresholds so high or so rarely reviewed that they no longer
reflect current project risk; letting a "temporary" manual override become routine (Example 11.3.3);
treating a reconciling item as resolved once it is *explained*, without confirming it is actually *corrected*
in the underlying records.

**AI in this topic.** Robotic process automation (RPA) and workflow engines can enforce the authorisation
matrix as a hard system control — a PO cannot be released above a requester's configured limit — and perform
three-way matching at a volume no manual team could sustain, the classic "invoice/PO matching" use case. The
control is only as good as its configuration: a mis-set threshold or an over-loose matching tolerance fails
silently, and periodically testing the *configuration itself*, not just its outputs, remains a professional
responsibility (§11.3.7).

**Cross-references.** §11.3.3 (preventive vs detective, embodied by the matrix and the match); §11.3.5 (why
the reconciler must be independent of recording and custody); KA 7.2 (contract management — variation
approval); KA 5.4 (change control and cost impact); KA 1.5 (cost coding relevant to invoice-matching
accuracy).

### 11.3.5 Segregation of duties: the four incompatible functions and the SoD conflict matrix

**Definition & purpose.** **Segregation of duties (SoD)** is the internal control principle that no single
individual should be able to both carry out an error or irregularity *and* conceal it in the normal course of
their own duties. It is achieved by splitting a transaction's life cycle across four functions, ideally held
by four different people: **authorise** (approve that the transaction should happen, within the authorisation
matrix, §11.3.4); **record** (enter the transaction into the accounting/ERP system); **custody** (physical or
system control over the related asset — cash, cheques, inventory, banking credentials, a payment run); and
**reconcile** (independently compare the recorded transaction to external, independent evidence — a bank
statement, a supplier statement, a physical count).

**The underlying principle.** Each pairwise combination of these functions, if held by one person, removes a
check that would otherwise exist. The risk is not uniform: some pairings let one individual both commit and
hide the error in the same act (the highest-risk combinations), while others create a narrower "self-review"
risk without direct asset access. SoD is a **control activity** within COSO's framework (§11.3.1), and its
rigour depends on having enough independent people to hold the four functions separately — the limiting case
for a small team is examined in §11.3.6.

### Fig 11.3.5 — The SoD conflict matrix: the four incompatible functions

| | Authorise | Record | Custody | Reconcile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Authorise** | — | **HIGH** — can approve, then record, a fictitious transaction | **HIGH** — can approve a disbursement, then take the asset | **MEDIUM** — self-review risk: may conceal errors in transactions of their own approving |
| **Record** | (see above) | — | **HIGH** — the classic theft-and-conceal combination: take the asset, post a covering entry | **HIGH** — the recorder checking their own postings is not an independent check |
| **Custody** | (see above) | (see above) | — | **HIGH** — can take the asset and adjust the reconciliation to hide the shortfall |
| **Reconcile** | (see above) | (see above) | (see above) | — |

**Example 11.3.5 — a compliant, fully segregated P2P role structure at Meridian's head office.** *Scenario.*
Meridian's head-office P2P process for a routine supplier invoice involves four roles: the **Procurement
Manager**, who approves the PO within the authorisation matrix (Authorise); the **AP Clerk**, who enters the
approved invoice into the ERP once received (Record); the **Treasury Officer**, who holds the banking
credentials and executes the weekly payment run (Custody); and the **Financial Controller**, who reconciles
the AP subledger to the general ledger and the bank statement each month (Reconcile). *Required.* Assess this
structure against the conflict matrix. *Working/Answer.* Each of the four functions sits with a different
individual — **no conflict** exists on any of the six pairings in Fig 11.3.5. *Interpretation.* This is the
textbook-compliant structure the matrix is built to describe; §11.3.6 examines what happens when headcount
does not permit it.

**Animation 11.3.5 — "Building the SoD conflict matrix" [digital-only].** Frame 1: four nodes (Authorise,

**Common pitfalls.** Assuming SoD is satisfied because job *titles* differ, without checking whether one
individual holds two of the four *functions* in system-access terms; treating Authorise + Reconcile as
risk-free rather than MEDIUM, particularly for large or unusual transactions; designing SoD around the formal
organisation chart while ignoring informal practice — a shared login "to help out" quietly recreates every
conflict the chart was built to prevent.

**AI in this topic.** **Process mining** tools reconstruct the *as-performed* process directly from ERP
system logs (who actually clicked "approve," who posted the entry, using which login) rather than the
*as-designed* org chart — a powerful way to surface real SoD violations, including shared-login workarounds,
that a manual review would never catch. It needs complete, reliable log data (an offline step is invisible to
it), and identifies *that* a conflict occurred, not *whether* it was ever exploited or already mitigated by a
compensating control (§11.3.6) — that risk assessment stays with the professional.

**Cross-references.** §11.3.1 (SoD as a control activity); §11.3.4 (the authorisation matrix and

### 11.3.6 Compensating controls where SoD is impractical: the Pipeline Segment 4B site-office mini-case

**Definition & purpose.** A **compensating control** is an alternative control activity that reduces risk to
an acceptable level when the ideal — full structural segregation of the four incompatible functions — cannot
be achieved, most commonly because a small team, a remote site or a new entity simply does not have enough
independent people to hold four separate functions. Compensating controls do not eliminate the underlying
conflict; they substitute additional, usually **detective**, strength (independent review, increased
oversight frequency, system-enforced limits) for the **preventive** strength that structural segregation
would otherwise provide.

**The underlying principle.** Risk cannot always be designed away by adding headcount. A controls
professional needs to recognise an unavoidable SoD conflict, size the residual risk honestly, and design a
proportionate response — neither ignoring it nor refusing to operate at all. The response typically layers
several compensating controls, since no single substitute fully replaces a missing structural segregation.

**Mini-case — the Pipeline Segment 4B site office.** *Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's site office has only
two Meridian staff: the **Site Quantity Surveyor (SQS)**, the sole commercial resource on site, and the
**Project Manager (PM)**, focused on technical and schedule delivery. To keep small, urgent site purchases
(consumables, minor plant hire, fuel) moving without waiting for head office, the SQS currently: raises and
approves local site purchase orders up to the $5,000 site-level limit (Authorise, per Fig 11.3.4); records the
corresponding goods receipts directly in the ERP (Record); and holds signing authority over a SAR-denominated
site petty-cash imprest account used for the smallest incidental purchases (Custody). No one is based at site
to independently reconcile the petty-cash account or the site's local commitments to head office's records.

*Required.* (a) Identify the SoD conflict using Fig 11.3.5; (b) assess the risk; (c) design compensating
controls.

*Working — (a) Identify the conflict.* The SQS holds **Authorise + Record + Custody** simultaneously — all
three of the highest-severity pairings in Fig 11.3.5 stacked in one role, with no on-site Reconcile function
at all.

*Working — (b) Assess the risk.* Unchecked, the SQS could raise a PO for consumables never delivered, record
a false goods receipt, and disburse petty cash against it — creating and concealing the irregularity in one
self-contained sequence, with no one at site to catch it before it reaches head office's monthly cost report.

*Working — (c) Design compensating controls.*

| Compensating control | What it substitutes for |
|---|---|
| 1. Petty-cash imprest capped at a low value (e.g. SAR 3,750 / $1,000 equivalent) and independently reconciled monthly by the head-office Financial Controller against original receipts couriered or scanned to head office | Restores an independent **Reconcile** function, physically removed from the SQS's control, and limits the maximum single-period exposure |
| 2. Every site-raised PO and GRN is provisional in the ERP until electronically countersigned by the PM before it posts | A partial maker-checker step within the small team — does not create full independence (the PM is not finance-trained) but breaks the SQS's unilateral Authorise + Record combination |
| 3. A weekly exception report — every GRN without a matching approved PO, and every petty-cash disbursement without an original receipt — reviewed by the Regional Commercial Manager at head office | An independent, off-site **detective** control operating far more frequently than the monthly reconciliation alone |
| 4. Mandatory annual leave for the SQS, with duties temporarily covered by a head-office-appointed relief officer | A classic detective compensating control: a concealed irregularity is more likely to surface when the regular custodian is briefly replaced |
| 5. Unannounced quarterly site audits (physical materials count, petty-cash count) performed by internal audit, independent of both site and regional commercial management | Independent, surprise verification that does not rely on the SQS's own records at all |

*Answer.* No single control fully replaces the missing structural segregation, but the five layered together
— independent off-site reconciliation, a partial maker-checker step, frequent exception reporting, mandatory
leave and unannounced audit — reduce the residual risk from **HIGH** (an unmonitored triple-function stack)
to what Meridian's internal audit assesses as **LOW-to-MODERATE**: a proportionate response given the site's
transaction values are capped at $5,000 (Fig 11.3.4), relative to the cost of a second permanent site
finance resource.

*Interpretation.* The compensating controls do not pretend the conflict is resolved — Fig 11.3.5 would still
show Authorise + Record + Custody held by one person — they demonstrate that the *residual risk* has been
consciously identified, sized and mitigated to a level management accepts, which is what a controls
professional, not just a control-design template, is expected to do.

*Underlying data:* the five-row compensating-control table above.

**Common pitfalls.** Treating "we're too small to segregate" as a reason to do nothing, rather than the
trigger to design compensating controls; designing them once and never revisiting them as site transaction
volumes grow; relying on a single compensating control alone, so one missed review leaves the conflict fully
unmitigated for a whole period.

**AI in this topic.** Lightweight anomaly-detection tools can flag unusual patterns in a small site's
petty-cash and local-PO activity (e.g. a high count of just-under-threshold purchases, a known structuring
red flag) — a useful early-warning layer on top of the human controls above. A single small site rarely
generates enough volume for a model to be highly reliable alone; AI augments, rather than substitutes for,
the layered human controls here, and the weekly exception review remains the control of record.

### 11.3.7 Audit trails and controls assurance & testing

**Definition & purpose.** An **audit trail** is the retained, chronological record connecting a transaction
from origination through recording to reporting — system logs, timestamps, user or approver identifiers, and
retained source documents — that lets an independent party trace and verify a transaction end to end after
the fact. **Controls assurance** is the broader discipline of testing whether controls actually work: a
**test of design** (a walkthrough asking whether the control, if operated as described, would actually
address the risk) and a **test of operating effectiveness** (sampling actual transactions to confirm the
control operated consistently throughout a period) are distinct and both matter — a well-designed control
that is not consistently *operated* provides no real assurance.

**The underlying principle.** Without an audit trail, neither management nor an auditor can distinguish a
control that worked from one that happened to produce the right answer by coincidence — the trail converts a
control from a paper design into something testable. Assurance is typically layered by role, following the
**Three Lines Model** (Institute of Internal Auditors, superseding "three lines of defense"): the **first
line** is operational/site management, owning risk and controls day to day (the PM's countersigning role,
§11.3.6); the **second line** is oversight functions — project controls, finance, compliance; and the
**third line** is internal audit, assuring the governing body directly — alongside external audit and, for an
outsourced service organisation (e.g. a payroll bureau), an ISAE 3402 or SOC 1 report.

**Formula.**

`Sample deviation rate = number of exceptions found in the sample / sample size`

The result is compared to an organisation's **tolerable deviation rate** for that control — an internal
policy threshold, not a fixed external standard — to conclude whether the control is assessed as operating
effectively.

**Example 11.3.7 — testing the "PO approved before commitment" control.** *Scenario.* Meridian's internal
audit function tests the authorisation control objective (§11.3.2, §11.3.4) for Pipeline Segment 4B. From a
population of 140 POs raised in the quarter, a sample of **25** is selected. For each, the tester compares
the ERP's approval timestamp and approver identity to the date the PO was issued to the supplier, and to the
authorisation matrix (Fig 11.3.4). Twenty-four of the 25 show a valid approval recorded before commitment;
one — the emergency scaffolding PO from Example 11.3.3 — shows approval recorded two days *after*
commitment. Meridian's internal audit policy sets a tolerable deviation rate of **5%** for this key control.
*Required.* Compute the sample deviation rate and state the conclusion. *Working.* `Sample deviation rate =
1 / 25 = 0.040 = 4.0%`. *Answer.* At **4.0%**, the deviation rate is **within** the 5% tolerable rate — the
control is assessed as **operating effectively** for the quarter, on the evidence sampled.
*Interpretation.* "Within tolerance" is not "no follow-up required": the single exception is investigated
(root cause: the system outage in Example 11.3.3), and because it exposed a genuine gap — no controlled
fallback existed for emergency procurement — Meridian's corrective action (adding a documented
emergency-procurement procedure, §11.3.3) proceeds regardless of the sample passing overall. A passing sample
tells the professional the control worked *often enough*; it does not tell them the one failure was
immaterial.

**Common pitfalls.** Testing only design (a walkthrough) and reporting a control "effective" without also
testing operating effectiveness on a sample; setting a tolerable deviation rate without reference to how
material the underlying risk is; treating every exception as simply pass/fail without root-cause analysis —
Example 11.3.7's single exception was low-risk (a quickly regularised emergency), but an identical rate from
repeated deliberate workarounds would warrant a very different conclusion.

**AI in this topic.** **Continuous controls monitoring (CCM)** tools can test 100% of a transaction
population against a defined control rule continuously, rather than a periodic sample of 25 out of 140 — a
substantial gain in assurance coverage and speed. Full-population testing shows *how many* deviations exist
far more precisely than sampling, but it does not assess *materiality* or *root cause*, and a poorly
specified rule will confidently produce the wrong answer at full scale rather than a sampling error at 25
transactions. Every AI-assisted controls-testing output in this KA remains a proposal for the professional's
review and sign-off, never a substitute for it: **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

### Key terms

- **Internal control** — a process, effected by an entity's people and management, providing reasonable
  assurance of achieving objectives across operations, reporting and compliance (COSO).
- **COSO Internal Control – Integrated Framework** — the standard reference framework: five components
  (control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information & communication, monitoring
  activities) across three objective categories.
- **Control environment** — the foundational COSO component: governance, ethics, competence and assigned
  authority that set the tone for all other controls.
- **Control objective** — the specific risk a control addresses for a class of transaction: existence,
  completeness, accuracy, authorisation, cut-off.
- **Preventive control** — stops an error or unauthorised transaction before it occurs.
- **Detective control** — identifies an error or irregularity after it has occurred.
- **Corrective control** — addresses the root cause once a detective control has found a problem.
- **Authorisation matrix / delegation of authority (DoA)** — the table of who may approve which transaction
  type and value band.
- **Reconciliation** — an independent comparison of two record sets that should agree, investigating any
  difference.
- **Three-way match** — the P2P control comparing PO, goods receipt and invoice for quantity and price
  agreement before an invoice is cleared for payment.
- **Segregation of duties (SoD)** — no individual should be able to both commit and conceal an error or
  irregularity in the normal course of their duties.
- **The four incompatible functions** — authorise, record, custody, reconcile; ideally held by four
  different people.
- **SoD conflict matrix** — the pairwise mapping of the four functions showing which combinations, if held
  by one person, create HIGH or MEDIUM risk.
- **Compensating control** — an alternative control substituted where full structural SoD cannot be
  achieved, reducing residual risk to an acceptable level.
- **Audit trail** — the retained, chronological evidence connecting a transaction from origination to
  reporting.
- **Three Lines Model** — the IIA's model of layered assurance: first line (operational management), second
  line (oversight functions), third line (internal audit), alongside external audit.
- **Test of design / test of operating effectiveness** — assessing whether a control would work if operated
  as described, versus whether it actually operated consistently.
- **Tolerable deviation rate** — the organisation-set threshold deviation rate below which a sampled control
  is assessed as operating effectively.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 11.3.1 [topic 11.3.2, level: recall]**
Which control objective is tested by confirming that a recorded purchase invoice relates to goods or
services that were genuinely received, rather than a fictitious transaction?
A. Completeness
B. **Existence** ✔
C. Cut-off
D. Authorisation
*Rationale:* Existence tests that a recorded transaction genuinely occurred. Completeness is the
mirror-image risk (something real omitted from the records); cut-off concerns period allocation;
authorisation concerns approval, not occurrence.

**MCQ 11.3.2 [topic 11.3.3, level: application]**
A monthly bank reconciliation identifies a payment that was released without proper authorisation. The
reconciliation itself is best classified as which type of control?
A. Preventive
B. **Detective** ✔
C. Corrective
D. Compensating
*Rationale:* The reconciliation identifies the irregularity after the payment has already occurred — it
detects, rather than prevents (before the fact) or fixes the root cause (corrective, which may follow once
the issue is found).

**MCQ 11.3.3 [topic 11.3.4, level: application — numerical]**
Under Meridian's authorisation matrix (site level up to $5,000; Project Manager $5,001–$25,000; Regional
Commercial Manager $25,001–$100,000; Regional Director $100,001–$500,000), which role must approve a $75,000
site variation order?
A. Site Quantity Surveyor
B. Project Manager
C. **Regional Commercial Manager** ✔
D. Regional Director
*Rationale:* $75,000 falls within the $25,001–$100,000 band. A and B are below the required threshold; D's
authority begins only above $100,000.

**MCQ 11.3.4 [topic 11.3.5, level: analysis]**
Which pairing of the four incompatible functions is most associated with an individual being able to
misappropriate an asset and conceal the shortfall in a single, self-contained act?
A. Authorise + Reconcile
B. **Record + Custody** ✔
C. Two people each independently holding Record only
D. Authorise + Custody, where a second person independently records every transaction
*Rationale:* Combining custody of an asset with recording the related entries lets one person take the asset
and post a covering or fictitious entry in the same act — the classic theft-and-conceal combination (Fig
11.3.5). A carries a self-review risk (MEDIUM) but no direct asset access; C describes no conflict; D
describes Authorise + Custody as a genuine HIGH-risk pairing, but with recording independently controlled,
concealment through a covering entry is not available to the same individual.

**MCQ 11.3.5 [topic 11.3.7, level: application — numerical]**
A controls test samples 40 purchase orders for evidence of authorisation before commitment and finds 3
exceptions. What is the sample deviation rate?
A. 3.0%
B. **7.5%** ✔
C. 12.0%
D. 37.0%
*Rationale:* `3 / 40 = 0.075 = 7.5%`. A misstates the numerator as a percentage of a different base; C and D
are arithmetic errors (e.g. mis-based or misplaced-decimal calculations).

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using the four incompatible functions, explain why Record + Custody is generally considered a higher-risk
   combination than Authorise + Reconcile.
2. Explain, in your own words, why COSO's control environment component matters even where an individual
   control activity such as an authorisation matrix is well designed.
3. In the Pipeline Segment 4B site-office mini-case (§11.3.6), name two compensating controls addressing the
   Site Quantity Surveyor's stacked conflict and state what each substitutes for.
4. Why does a reliable audit trail matter to the outcome of a controls test such as Example 11.3.7 — what
   would happen to the test's conclusion if the ERP kept no approval timestamp?

**Answers**

1. Record + Custody lets one person both take or control an asset and post the entry that conceals the
   shortfall, in a single, self-contained act (Fig 11.3.5) — the highest-risk combination, because it removes
   both the opportunity barrier and the concealment barrier at once. Authorise + Reconcile creates a genuine
   self-review risk (the approver checking their own approved transactions) but does not, by itself, give
   that person physical or system control of the asset — rated MEDIUM rather than HIGH.
2. A control activity such as an authorisation matrix only functions if people actually follow it and
   breaching it carries real consequences; the control environment (tone at the top, ethics, competence,
   clearly assigned authority) is what creates that context. A perfectly designed matrix operated within an
   organisation where overrides are casually tolerated is, in practice, not an effective control at all
   (§11.3.1).
3. Any two from Fig 11.3.6, for example: (i) the independent monthly petty-cash reconciliation performed by
   the head-office Financial Controller, which substitutes for the missing on-site Reconcile function; (ii)
   the weekly exception report reviewed by the Regional Commercial Manager, which substitutes a frequent,
   independent detective review for the missing structural segregation.
4. The approval timestamp is the specific evidence that let the tester determine whether each of the 25
   sampled POs was approved *before* commitment. Without a reliable timestamp, the tester could not
   distinguish a properly preventive approval from a retrospective one for any PO in the sample — the test of
   operating effectiveness could not be performed at all, and the control could be neither confirmed as
   effective nor shown to have failed; the population would, in effect, be untestable.
