# Domain 5 — Cost Management & Cost Control

## KA 5.1 — Cost management framework

Domain 3 showed how a base estimate becomes a budget, a cost baseline and a total project budget (KA 3.1);
Domain 6 showed how that baseline becomes Planned Value and is compared against Earned Value and Actual Cost
to measure performance (KA 6.1–6.4). Both domains took the underlying cost *structure* — what a "cost"
actually is, how it is classified, how it is broken down into reportable pieces, and who is accountable for
each figure in it — largely as given. This Knowledge Area is where that structure is built. It opens Domain
5 (Cost Management & Cost Control) by setting out the **cost management framework**: the total cost
management concept that AACE International and PMI's PMBOK Guide both organise cost work around; the
**cost management plan** that fixes the rules every later estimate, budget and control report must follow;
the **life-cycle cost view** that looks beyond a single capital number to an asset's whole-life cost; the
basic **cost types** (direct/indirect, fixed/variable, recurring/non-recurring) that every subsequent cost
classification in this book depends on; the **cost breakdown structure (CBS)** that links cost elements to
the WBS and the chart of accounts; the precise relationship between the **cost baseline and the budget**,
including where contingency and management reserve sit (cross-reference KA 3.1, KA 12.3 — not repeated
here); and the **roles and governance** that make all of the above accountable rather than theoretical. The
rest of Domain 5 builds directly on this KA: KA 5.2 (once written) develops the operational cost-control
cycle — commitment, accrual, actual — that consumes the structure set out here; KA 5.3 (once written) deepens
control-account mechanics already introduced in KA 1.5; KA 5.4 (once written) covers change control and its
cost impact. The continuing worked entity is **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**, delivering **Pipeline
Segment 4B** — the $600,000 (SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) control account already
built, estimated and risk-assessed in KA 3.2, KA 6.1 and KA 12.2–12.3. This KA does not re-derive those
figures; it shows, for the first time, how they connect into one coherent cost management framework.

### 5.1.1 Total cost management: the discipline and its place in the framework

**Definition & purpose.** **Total Cost Management (TCM)** is AACE International's name for the integrated
discipline that spans cost and schedule work across the full asset life cycle — from strategic asset
planning, through project cost estimating and budgeting, to cost engineering, performance measurement and
forecasting during execution. PMI's PMBOK Guide organises the same territory, at project level, into four
**Cost Management** processes: **Plan Cost Management** (deciding the rules — §5.1.2), **Estimate Costs**
(KA 3.2), **Determine Budget** (KA 3.1), and **Control Costs** (KA 5.2, once written, and Domain 6's EVM in
depth). The two frameworks describe the same underlying work from different vantage points — AACE's is
broader (a portfolio/asset view including strategic asset management), PMBOK's is project-scoped — and this
BoK uses both names interchangeably where their content overlaps, naming whichever is the more precise
source for a given topic.

**The underlying principle.** Cost management is not a single technique but a *sequence of dependent
decisions*, each of which constrains what is possible at the next: a cost management plan (§5.1.2) fixes the
rules; a cost breakdown structure (§5.1.5) fixes the reporting structure; an estimate (KA 3.2) produces a
number; a budget (KA 3.1) authorises it; and cost control (KA 5.2, KA 6.1–6.4) measures performance against
it. Skipping or under-specifying an early step does not save effort — it defers cost and confusion to a later
step, where it is more expensive to fix (the same lesson KA 3.1.5 drew about baselining without proper
reconciliation, now generalised across the whole framework).

**Formula.** No single arithmetic formula describes a framework; what follows is the process map itself,
stated once here so every later cross-reference in this BoK points back to a common structure.

*The cost management process map, and where each step is treated in this BoK:*

| Step | AACE TCM Framework | PMBOK Cost Management process | Treated in |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (governing) | Cost management planning | Plan Cost Management | §5.1.2 (this KA) |
| 1 | Cost estimating | Estimate Costs | KA 3.2 |
| 2 | Budgeting / cost baseline | Determine Budget | KA 3.1, §5.1.6 (this KA) |
| 3 | Cost engineering / project cost control | Control Costs | KA 5.2 (once written), KA 5.4 (once written) |
| 4 | Performance measurement & forecasting | Control Costs (via EVM) | KA 6.1–6.4 |

**Mini-case — treating "cost control" as a single, undifferentiated activity.** A newly formed project team,
without a cost management plan, uses the phrase "we're controlling cost" to describe everything from
estimating a new work package, to approving a supplier invoice, to producing a monthly variance report — with
no team member able to say which of the four process-map steps a given task actually belongs to. When a
$40,000 estimating error surfaces late in execution, the team's first instinct is to look for it in the
monthly control reports (Step 4), because that is where "cost control" is assumed to live — wasting a full
reporting cycle before someone traces the error back to Step 1, where it originated. Naming the steps
explicitly, as the process map does, is what lets a team route a problem to the right stage of the framework
immediately rather than searching the wrong one first.

### 5.1.2 The cost management plan

**Definition & purpose.** The **cost management plan** is the subsidiary component of the overall project
management plan (PMBOK's Plan Cost Management process, cross-reference KA 8.2) that documents, in advance
of estimating, how project costs will be structured, estimated, budgeted, executed and controlled. AACE's
TCM Framework treats this planning step as the necessary precursor to every later step in §5.1.1's process
map, precisely because it fixes decisions — units, precision, thresholds, methods, roles — that later steps
would otherwise have to make inconsistently, one work package at a time.

**The underlying principle.** A cost management plan trades a small amount of up-front effort for
consistency across the whole project (and, if templated properly, across a portfolio). Without one, two
control-account owners can legitimately make different, individually reasonable choices — one measuring
earned value by weighted milestones, another by a simple 50/50 split for similar-natured work (cross-
reference KA 6.1.4's front-loading pitfall) — that make the resulting reports impossible to compare or roll
up reliably. The plan does not do any cost management itself; it makes every later cost management decision
answerable to a documented rule rather than to whoever happens to be doing the work that week.

**Structure — key components of a cost management plan.**

| Component | Purpose | Pipeline Segment 4B (Meridian) |
|---|---|---|
| Units of measure & currency | Fixes the reporting currency and units | USD primary; illustrative SAR at 3.75 = USD 1 (never a live rate) |
| Level of precision | How finely figures are stated in reports | Nearest $100 in reports; exact $ in the ledger |
| Level of accuracy expected | Which AACE estimate class applies at each stage | Per KA 3.2.1's class progression |
| Cost breakdown structure & coding | The CBS/cost-code structure cost is captured against | Structure built in §5.1.5; coding mechanics in KA 1.5 |
| Control thresholds | The variance % that triggers formal review | ±5.0% variance at control-account level (worked below) |
| EVM measurement methods | Which method values EV for each work-package type | Per KA 6.1.4 (0/100, 50/50, % complete, weighted milestones, LOE) |
| Reporting formats & frequency | Standard reports and cadence | Monthly EVM summary plus exception reporting (cross-ref KA 4.3) |
| Reserve & change-control rules | Who may authorise contingency vs management reserve access | Per §5.1.6, KA 3.1.6, KA 12.3 |
| Roles & responsibilities | Who owns which decision | §5.1.7 |

**Formula.** The control-threshold rule, the one genuinely arithmetic component of the plan:

`Variance % = (Actual − Budget) / Budget × 100%`; escalate to formal review if `|Variance %| > threshold`
stated in the plan.

**Example 5.1.2 — applying Pipeline Segment 4B's ±5.0% control threshold.**

*Scenario.* WP2 (Pipe & valve procurement)'s control-account budget, built up from cost elements in §5.1.5,
is $150,000. By Month 5, its actual cost is reported at $158,000.

*Required.* The variance percentage, and whether it breaches the plan's ±5.0% threshold.

*Working.*
`Variance = 158,000 − 150,000 = 8,000`
`Variance % = 8,000 / 150,000 × 100% = 5.3%`

*Answer.* **Variance = +$8,000 (5.3%), which exceeds the ±5.0% threshold.**

*Interpretation.* Because the plan states the threshold in advance, the escalation is a mechanical trigger,
not a judgement call made under pressure after the fact — the control-account owner and the project controls
manager already know, before the overrun occurred, exactly what percentage would require a formal review.
This is the practical value of §5.1.2's exercise: the number is small, but the governance behaviour it
switches on is not.

**Mini-case — a gap in the plan, not a breach of it.** Two control-account owners on a comparable Meridian
project, in the absence of a documented EVM-method table, each measure similar short-duration procurement
milestones differently — one using 50/50, the other using 0/100 — producing a materially different reported
% complete for physically identical purchase-order status (exactly the distortion KA 6.1.4's mini-case
quantifies). No individual decision was wrong; the plan simply never said which method applied to which type
of work package. The fix is not retraining either owner — it is adding the missing method table to the cost
management plan before the next control account is opened, so the choice is made once, centrally, rather
than repeatedly and inconsistently at the point of use.

either extreme defeats the purpose of having a threshold. (3) Leaving a component silent (the mini-case

review" — silence is read differently by different people, which is exactly what the plan exists to prevent.

mode at the plan level instead of the work-package level.

### 5.1.3 The life-cycle cost view: capex, opex and whole-life cost

**Definition & purpose.** **Capital expenditure (capex)** is cost that meets the recognition criteria for an
asset — under IAS 16, cost directly attributable to bringing an asset to the location and condition necessary
for it to operate as intended (cross-reference KA 2.4, not repeated here) — and is capitalised onto the
statement of financial position rather than expensed immediately. **Operating expenditure (opex)** is cost
that does not meet those criteria and is instead expensed as incurred. **Whole-life cost** (also called
**total cost of ownership, TCO**) is the sum of an asset's capex and the present value of all its opex over
its operating life — a life-cycle view that both AACE's TCM Framework (under its strategic asset management
scope) and PMBOK recognise as a legitimate input to a cost decision, even though neither capex nor opex alone
tells the full story.

**The underlying principle.** A lower capex option is not automatically the better decision if it carries
materially higher ongoing opex — the two must be compared on the *same* basis, over the *same* period, in
*present-value* terms, because a dollar of opex saved five years from now is worth less today than a dollar
of capex spent now. Ignoring this discounting step, or ignoring opex altogether and comparing capex figures
in isolation, is a common and costly project-controls error, particularly on assets with a long operating
life relative to their construction period.

**Formula.**

`Whole-life cost = Capex + Σ [Opex_t / (1 + r)^t]`, summed over `t = 1` to `n` (the operating/design life in
years), where `r` is the discount rate.

**Example 5.1.3 — comparing two valve-actuator options for Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* During early procurement planning, Meridian evaluates two actuator technologies for Pipeline
Segment 4B's isolation valves, over a 5-year design-review horizon, at an illustrative discount rate of
10.0%:

| Option | Capex ($) | Opex ($/year) |
|---|---|---|
| A — pneumatic actuators | 40,000 | 8,000 |
| B — electric actuators | 52,000 | 3,000 |

*Required.* The undiscounted whole-life cost and the discounted whole-life cost of each option, and which
option Meridian should prefer on a whole-life basis.

*Working.*

*Undiscounted:*
`Whole-life (A) = 40,000 + (8,000 × 5) = 40,000 + 40,000 = 80,000`
`Whole-life (B) = 52,000 + (3,000 × 5) = 52,000 + 15,000 = 67,000`

*Discounted, using the five-year discount factors at 10.0%:*

| Year | Discount factor (10.0%) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0.909 |
| 2 | 0.826 |
| 3 | 0.751 |
| 4 | 0.683 |
| 5 | 0.621 |
| **Sum (cumulative annuity factor)** | **3.790** |

`PV(opex A) = 8,000 × 3.790 = 30,320`
`PV(opex B) = 3,000 × 3.790 = 11,370`
`Whole-life cost (A) = 40,000 + 30,320 = 70,320`
`Whole-life cost (B) = 52,000 + 11,370 = 63,370`

*Answer.* **Undiscounted: Option A = $80,000; Option B = $67,000 (B cheaper by $13,000). Discounted at
10.0%: Option A = $70,320; Option B = $63,370 (B cheaper by $6,950).**

*Interpretation.* Option B costs $12,000 more up front but is cheaper on a whole-life basis either way —
the correct decision does not change. What *does* change is the size of B's advantage: $13,000 undiscounted
narrows to $6,950 once discounted, because B's entire advantage lies in future opex savings, and discounting
reduces the present value of savings that arrive later rather than now. A professional comparing only capex
would choose A and be wrong; a professional comparing undiscounted whole-life cost would still choose B but
overstate the margin; only the discounted comparison states the decision correctly *and* sizes it correctly.

**Mini-case — whole-life cost is not the only decision criterion.** Suppose Option A's lower capex were the
deciding factor for a cash-constrained client facing a hard funding-release limit in the current year (cross-
reference KA 3.1.5's funding-limit mini-case) — Meridian might still recommend Option A despite its higher
whole-life cost, provided the trade-off is made *explicitly and knowingly*, not by default because opex was
never compared. Whole-life costing does not remove judgement from the decision; it ensures the judgement is
made with the full cost picture in view, including which fund (capital budget vs operating budget, often
governed and approved separately by a client organisation) each option actually draws from.

incurred now is already at present value and should not itself be discounted a second time.

**AI in this topic.** Life-cycle-costing tools can model whole-life cost across many design options and
sensitivity-test the result against a range of discount rates and opex assumptions far faster than a manual
comparison, and can flag which input (discount rate, opex escalation, operating life) the conclusion is most
sensitive to. They cannot supply a defensible discount rate or opex forecast on their own authority — those
remain finance/engineering judgements, informed by the organisation's cost of capital and operating
experience, and must be stated and justified, not merely accepted from a tool default.

### 5.1.4 Cost types: direct/indirect, fixed/variable, recurring/non-recurring

**Definition & purpose.** Three independent classification questions apply to every cost in the framework,
and a professional should be able to answer all three for any given cost without confusing them. **Direct vs
indirect** asks whether a cost is traceable to a specific **cost object** (a project, work package or control
account) or must instead be allocated to it via a cost driver because it is shared across several cost
objects. **Fixed vs variable** asks how a cost *behaves* as activity volume changes — fixed costs stay
constant in total regardless of volume; variable costs move roughly in proportion to volume (a cost with both
elements is **mixed** or **semi-variable**). **Recurring vs non-recurring** asks whether a cost is incurred
repeatedly, period after period, for as long as the related activity continues, or as a single, discrete
event.

**The underlying principle.** These three questions are independent — a cost can be direct and fixed and
non-recurring all at once (a work package's one-off mobilisation charge), or direct and variable and
recurring (labour paid per shift for as long as trenching continues), and the classification is always
relative to a stated cost object: the same site-supervision cost is *direct* to the project as a whole (it
would not exist without this specific contract) yet behaves as an *indirect*, non-output-tied cost within the
work package it sits in, because it does not scale with any single measurable unit of physical output. Cost
allocation of genuinely shared, company-level indirect cost (head-office overhead) to individual projects, via
a chosen cost driver and absorption rate, is a further step this KA only flags — its mechanics belong to the
operational cost-control cycle (KA 5.2, once written).

**Formula.**

`Total cost = Fixed cost + (Variable cost per unit × Quantity)`

`Average cost per unit = Total cost / Quantity` — this falls toward the variable rate as Quantity grows,
because the fixed component is spread over more units.

**Example 5.1.4 — classifying Pipeline Segment 4B's cost elements.**

*Scenario/Required.* Classify five cost elements already used in Pipeline Segment 4B's costed examples
(KA 3.2.4, KA 6.1.4) against all three questions.

*Working/Answer.*

| Cost element | Direct or indirect (to Pipeline Segment 4B) | Cost behaviour | Recurring or non-recurring |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design & engineering labour (WP1) | Direct | Largely fixed (a resourced team, not volume-driven) | Non-recurring (a single design phase) |
| Pipe & valve materials (WP2) | Direct | Variable (scales with quantity/specification ordered) | Non-recurring (a single procurement event) |
| Trenching labour & equipment (WP3) | Direct | Variable (scales with metres trenched) | Recurring (charged repeatedly as trenching proceeds) |

| Head-office overhead (not charged to this control account) | Indirect (shared across Meridian's project portfolio; allocated via a cost driver) | Fixed in total; allocated per project via an absorption rate | Recurring |

*Interpretation.* Four of the five rows are direct to Pipeline Segment 4B, underscoring that "direct" does
not mean "variable" or "recurring" — WP1's engineering labour is direct and fixed and non-recurring, all at
once. Only the fifth row is genuinely indirect at project level, and its allocation mechanics (which cost
driver, what absorption rate) are deliberately deferred to KA 5.2 rather than developed here, since this
control account carries none of that shared overhead within its own $600,000 BAC.

**Mini-case — hire vs owned trenching equipment: a fixed/variable sourcing decision.** Before selecting its
trenching approach, Meridian's team evaluates two illustrative sourcing options for the excavator needed on
WP3 (simplified rates, for teaching the cost-behaviour principle only — distinct from the definitive costed
rate built work package by work package in KA 3.2.4):

- **Hire option** — a day-rate contractor, paid per metre trenched, no fixed cost: `Total cost = $120 ×
  Quantity (m)`.
- **Owned/mobilised option** — Meridian mobilises its own excavator: a fixed mobilisation and standing cost
  of $60,000, plus a lower variable cost of $60/m: `Total cost = 60,000 + (60 × Quantity)`.

*Breakeven quantity* — the point at which both options cost the same:
`120Q = 60,000 + 60Q`
`60Q = 60,000`
`Q = 1,000 m`

*At the planned scope, 2,000 m:*
`Hire = 120 × 2,000 = 240,000`
`Owned = 60,000 + (60 × 2,000) = 60,000 + 120,000 = 180,000`

*At a hypothetical scope growth to 3,000 m:*
`Hire = 120 × 3,000 = 360,000`
`Owned = 60,000 + (60 × 3,000) = 60,000 + 180,000 = 240,000`

*Interpretation.* Below 1,000 m, hire is cheaper (its fixed cost is zero); above 1,000 m, the owned option's
fixed cost has been spread over enough metres that its lower variable rate wins, and the gap only widens as
quantity grows further ($60,000 saved at 2,000 m; $120,000 saved at 3,000 m). This is the general lesson of
cost behaviour for a sourcing decision: a fixed-cost-heavy option needs enough volume to be worthwhile, and
knowing the breakeven point — not just the cost at the currently planned quantity — is what lets the decision
survive a scope change without being re-analysed from scratch.

cannot independently verify that a specific cost genuinely behaves as its historical peers did on *this*
project — the professional confirms the tag, particularly for a cost near a fixed/variable or direct/indirect
boundary.

### 5.1.5 The cost breakdown structure (CBS): linking cost elements, the WBS and the chart of accounts

**Definition & purpose.** The **cost breakdown structure (CBS)** — AACE's TCM Framework term, already
introduced at coding level in KA 1.5.2 — is the structure that lets one dollar of project cost be viewed
simultaneously three ways: by **cost element** (what kind of resource — labour, materials, equipment,
subcontract; §5.1.4), by **work breakdown structure (WBS)** position (which work package it belongs to; KA
1.5.3), and by **natural account** (which chart-of-accounts line it posts to for statutory reporting; KA
1.5.1). This topic does not repeat KA 1.5's coding mechanics; it shows what those three views are *for*
within the cost management framework — converging, without a single dollar going missing, on the cost
baseline that §5.1.6 builds into BAC.

**The underlying principle.** Because every coded transaction carries all three tags at once (KA 1.5.2), the
same underlying cost data can be aggregated by cost element, by work package, or both simultaneously, and
every aggregation must reconcile to the same grand total — the CBS is the structural guarantee that a cost
element view and a WBS view of the same project are two windows onto one number, not two competing numbers.

**Formula.**

`Base estimate = Σ (cost elements) = Σ (work-package costs, i.e. CBS totals)`

**Example 5.1.5 — building Pipeline Segment 4B's base estimate from cost elements up to the CBS.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's five work packages (KA 6.1.4) each break down into the cost elements
classified in §5.1.4, at their base (pre-contingency) values:

| Work package | Labour ($) | Equipment ($) | Materials ($) | Subcontract ($) | WP base total ($) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| WP1 Design & engineering | 80,000 | — | — | — | **80,000** |
| WP2 Pipe & valve procurement | — | — | 140,000 | — | **140,000** |
| WP3 Trenching & earthworks | 100,000 | 30,000 | 40,000 | — | **170,000** |
| WP4 Pipe-laying & welding | 84,000 | — | — | 16,000 | **100,000** |
| WP5 Site supervision | 50,000 | — | — | — | **50,000** |
| **Cost-element total** | **314,000** | **30,000** | **180,000** | **16,000** | **540,000** |

*Required.* Confirm the matrix reconciles in both directions, to a single base estimate.

*Working.*
*Row totals (by work package):* `80,000 + 140,000 + 170,000 + 100,000 + 50,000 = 540,000`
*Column totals (by cost element):* `314,000 + 30,000 + 180,000 + 16,000 = 540,000`

*Answer.* **Base estimate = $540,000, reconciling exactly whether summed by work package (the WBS/CBS view)
or by cost element (the natural-cost-type view).**

*Interpretation.* This is the CBS doing precisely what §5.1.5's principle describes: a design manager reads
across WP1's row and sees $80,000, entirely labour; a finance reviewer reads down the Labour column and sees
$314,000 spread across four work packages; both are reading the same underlying data, and both totals agree
with each other and with the $540,000 grand total in the bottom-right corner — the same built-in cross-check
KA 1.5.3's WBS-by-cost-type matrix demonstrated for Meridian's Rail Signalling Contract, now applied to
Pipeline Segment 4B's base estimate.

**Mini-case — adding contingency without breaking the reconciliation.** Meridian's quantified risk register
for Pipeline Segment 4B (KA 12.2, KA 12.3.2) distributes $60,000 of contingency to three of the five work
packages: WP2 +$10,000 (subcontractor mobilisation delay), WP3 +$30,000 (adverse ground conditions), WP4
+$20,000 (weld defect rework); WP1 and WP5 carry none, because neither faces a quantified risk in the
register.

| Work package | Base ($) | Distributed contingency ($) | Total (= KA 6.1.4, $) |
|---|---|---|---|
| WP1 | 80,000 | 0 | 80,000 |
| WP2 | 140,000 | 10,000 | 150,000 |
| WP3 | 170,000 | 30,000 | 200,000 |
| WP4 | 100,000 | 20,000 | 120,000 |
| WP5 | 50,000 | 0 | 50,000 |
| **Total** | **540,000** | **60,000** | **600,000** |

`Cost baseline (BAC) = 540,000 + 60,000 = 600,000` (SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) —
exactly the BAC already used throughout KA 6.1–6.4 and KA 12.3, and matching each of that table's five
work-package totals individually, not merely in aggregate.

*Interpretation.* Adding contingency does not disturb the cost-element reconciliation already proved above —
it is layered on top of the CBS, work package by work package, exactly where the risk that justifies it
actually sits (WP1 and WP5 are correctly left untouched, since neither carries a quantified risk). §5.1.6
takes this same $600,000 forward and adds management reserve to complete the budget hierarchy.

### 5.1.6 The cost baseline vs the budget: contingency and management reserve placement

**Definition & purpose.** KA 3.1 built the full budgeting hierarchy — base estimate, contingency reserve,
cost baseline (BAC), management reserve, total project budget — from first principles, and KA 12.3 developed
how contingency is sized and drawn down in depth; neither is repeated here. This topic's job is narrower: to
show precisely where the cost baseline and the total project budget sit *within the cost management
framework* this KA has been building, using the CBS-derived figures from §5.1.5 as the foundation, and to
restate — once, as the framework's own governing convention — the single rule every EVM calculation in
Domain 6 depends on.

**The underlying principle.** The **cost baseline (BAC)** — base estimate plus contingency reserve — is the
figure the cost management framework treats as fixed for performance measurement; the **total project
budget** — cost baseline plus management reserve — is a wider, funding-level figure that sits *outside* that
performance measurement baseline until a formal, approved change transfers part of it in. Confusing the two
inside a cost management framework is not a minor labelling slip: every CPI, SPI and EAC formula in Domain 6
uses BAC, never the total project budget, as its denominator or reference point.

**Formula.**

`Cost baseline (BAC) = Base estimate + Contingency reserve` (built in §5.1.5)
`Total project budget = Cost baseline (BAC) + Management reserve`

**Example 5.1.6 — completing Pipeline Segment 4B's budget hierarchy.**

*Scenario.* Following §5.1.5's build, Pipeline Segment 4B's cost baseline (BAC) is $600,000. Meridian's
sponsor has set management reserve at 5.0% of BAC.

*Required.* Management reserve and the total project budget, in USD and, illustratively, in SAR.

*Working.*

| Component | USD ($) | SAR (illustrative, × 3.75) |
|---|---|---|
| Base estimate (§5.1.5) | 540,000 | 2,025,000 |
| Contingency reserve (§5.1.5) | 60,000 | 225,000 |
| **Cost baseline (BAC)** | **600,000** | **2,250,000** |
| Management reserve (5.0% of BAC) | 30,000 | 112,500 |
| **Total project budget** | **630,000** | **2,362,500** |

`Management reserve = 0.05 × 600,000 = 30,000`
`Total project budget = 600,000 + 30,000 = 630,000`

*Answer.* **Cost baseline (BAC) = $600,000 (SAR 2,250,000); Total project budget = $630,000 (SAR
2,362,500)** — matching exactly the figures already reported in KA 12.3.1 and KA 12.3.4.

*Interpretation.* The cost management framework this KA has built — cost types (§5.1.4) rolled up through
the CBS (§5.1.5) into a base estimate, contingency added to reach BAC, management reserve added on top —
arrives at the identical $600,000/$630,000 figures Domain 6 and Domain 12 already use. That agreement is not
a coincidence to be admired; it is the whole point of having one framework: whichever chapter of this BoK a
reader approaches Pipeline Segment 4B from, the numbers must reconcile.

**Mini-case — why BAC, not the total project budget, drives Domain 6.** Suppose a report mistakenly used the
$630,000 total project budget as the denominator for a CPI calculation instead of the $600,000 BAC. At
Month 3 (EV = $330,000, KA 6.1.4), the correctly-denominated read is `EV / BAC = 330,000 / 600,000 = 55.0%`
of budget earned; using the total project budget instead gives `330,000 / 630,000 = 52.4%` — a smaller,
subtly wrong figure that understates progress against the *authorised, performance-measured* baseline by
including $30,000 of reserve that was never part of that baseline in the first place. The error is easy to
make (both figures are "the budget" in casual conversation) and easy to avoid once the framework's convention
— BAC only, for every EVM calculation — is stated explicitly, as it is here and in KA 3.1.5.

### 5.1.7 Cost management roles and governance

**Definition & purpose.** Every figure this KA has built — the cost management plan, the CBS, the base
estimate, the cost baseline, the total project budget — has a named owner and a named approver; without that
accountability structure, the framework is a set of correct calculations with no one answerable for them.
This topic sets out the standard roles across a project-based contracting organisation like Meridian, and the
**RACI** convention (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) commonly used to make that accountability
explicit and auditable.

**The underlying principle.** Accountability in a cost management framework is deliberately layered, not
concentrated in one role: the people who build the numbers (control account owners, cost engineers) are
rarely the people with authority to approve the reserves that protect those numbers against risk (the
sponsor or steering committee) — a separation of duties that mirrors, at project-controls level, the
segregation principles Domain 11 develops for transactional cycles (cross-reference KA 11.3, once written).
A second, distinct governance dimension — a **delegation of authority (DoA)**, an amount-based limit on what
a given role may approve without escalation — sits alongside the reserve-type distinction KA 3.1.6 and KA
12.3 already established, and the two are easily conflated.

**Structure — a RACI matrix for Pipeline Segment 4B's cost management activities.**

| Activity | Sponsor / Steering Committee | Project Controls Manager | Control Account Owner | Finance & Accounting | PMO |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Approve cost management plan (§5.1.2) | A | R | I | C | C |
| Design & maintain CBS/cost coding (§5.1.5, KA 1.5) | I | A/R | C | C | C |
| Prepare base cost estimate, bottom-up (KA 3.2.4) | I | A | R | I | I |
| Approve cost baseline / BAC (§5.1.6) | A | R | C | I | I |
| Record actual cost, AC (KA 6.1.3) | I | A | R | R | I |
| Approve contingency drawdown, named in-scope risk (KA 12.3.3) | I | A* | R | I | I |
| Approve management-reserve transfer (§5.1.6, KA 3.1.6) | A | R | I | C | C |
| Statutory financial reporting (Domain 2) | I | I | I | A/R | I |

`*` — subject to the delegation-of-authority limit below.

**Formula.** No arithmetic defines a governance structure; the one genuinely quantitative governance rule is
the **delegation of authority (DoA)** limit — an amount above which an approval must escalate:

`Escalate if Requested amount > DoA limit for the approving role`

**Example 5.1.7 — a contingency drawdown that exceeds delegated authority.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's DoA table sets the Project Controls Manager's authority to approve a contingency
drawdown at **up to $25,000 per single event**, with any management-reserve transfer reserved to the sponsor
regardless of amount (matching the RACI table above). Later in the project, a fourth, newly identified,
in-scope risk — distinct from the three already fully worked in KA 12.2–12.3 — materialises with an actual
remediation cost of $32,000.

*Required.* State whether the Project Controls Manager may approve the drawdown alone, and why.

*Working/Answer.* `Requested amount ($32,000) > DoA limit ($25,000)` → **the Project Controls Manager cannot
approve this drawdown alone; it must escalate to the sponsor**, even though it is funded from contingency
(an in-scope, register-linked risk, not management reserve) and would ordinarily sit entirely within
delegated project-controls authority under the fund-type distinction alone (KA 12.3.1).

*Interpretation.* This is a genuinely separate governance dimension from the contingency-vs-management-
reserve distinction KA 3.1.6 and KA 12.3 already established: *which fund* a cost draws from decides whether
formal baseline change control applies at all; the **DoA limit** independently decides whether *this specific
role*, at *this specific amount*, may sign it off without a further escalation — a large-enough contingency
draw can still require sponsor sign-off even though the fund itself never needed sponsor-level authority in
principle.

**Mini-case — an ambiguous "A."** Meridian's early RACI drafts listed both the Project Controls Manager and
the Control Account Owner as jointly "Accountable" for approving the base cost estimate, reasoning that both
had a stake in the figure. When a subsequent audit asked "who, specifically, signed off this number," neither
role could produce a single named approval — each had assumed the other had formally signed it. RACI
convention holds that **exactly one role is Accountable** for a given activity (others may be Responsible,
Consulted or Informed); two "A"s is not extra rigour, it is the same failure mode as having none. Meridian's
revised table (above) assigns the Project Controls Manager sole accountability for the estimate, with the
Control Account Owner as Responsible for producing it — a correction, not a demotion.

new type of activity not yet in the table, nor resolve a genuine disagreement about where accountability
belongs — those remain organisational-design judgements for the PMO and sponsor, and every AI-enforced
routing rule is only as sound as the RACI and DoA table a human approved before it was configured.

### Key terms

- **Total Cost Management (TCM)** — AACE International's integrated cost-and-schedule discipline spanning
  the asset life cycle; overlaps with, and is used alongside, PMI's PMBOK Cost Management knowledge area.
- **Cost management plan** — the documented rules (units, precision, thresholds, methods, roles) governing
  how project cost will be estimated, budgeted and controlled; a subsidiary of the project management plan.
- **Control threshold** — a stated variance % above which a formal review is triggered.
- **Capital expenditure (capex)** — cost meeting an asset's recognition criteria (IAS 16), capitalised rather
  than expensed.
- **Operating expenditure (opex)** — cost not meeting those criteria, expensed as incurred.
- **Whole-life cost / total cost of ownership (TCO)** — capex plus the present value of opex over an asset's
  operating life.
- **Cost object** — the project, work package or control account a cost is (or is not) directly traceable to.
- **Direct cost / indirect cost** — traceable to a single cost object versus shared and allocated via a cost
  driver.
- **Fixed cost / variable cost / mixed (semi-variable) cost** — constant in total regardless of volume;
  proportional to volume; a blend of both.
- **Recurring cost / non-recurring cost** — incurred repeatedly, period after period, versus as a single,
  discrete event.
- **Cost breakdown structure (CBS)** — the structure linking cost elements, the WBS and the chart of accounts
  so the same cost can be viewed, and reconciled, three ways.
- **Cost element** — a category of resource cost (labour, materials, equipment, subcontract).
- **Cost baseline / Budget at Completion (BAC)** — base estimate plus contingency reserve; the fixed
  performance-measurement reference used identically throughout Domain 6.
- **Total project budget** — cost baseline plus management reserve; the full amount authorised and funded.
- **RACI** — Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed; a convention for assigning exactly one
  Accountable role per activity.
- **Delegation of authority (DoA)** — an amount-based limit on what a given role may approve before an
  approval must escalate; independent of the contingency/management-reserve fund distinction.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 5.1.1 [topic 5.1.1, level: recall]**
Which sequence correctly reflects the order of PMI's PMBOK Guide Cost Management processes?
A. Estimate Costs → Plan Cost Management → Control Costs → Determine Budget
B. Plan Cost Management → Estimate Costs → Determine Budget → Control Costs ✔
C. Determine Budget → Estimate Costs → Plan Cost Management → Control Costs
D. Plan Cost Management → Determine Budget → Estimate Costs → Control Costs
*Rationale:* B is the governing sequence (§5.1.1): the plan is set first, then costs are estimated, then a
budget is determined from the estimate, then costs are controlled against the budget. The other options
place a step before its logical prerequisite — D, for instance, would require determining a budget before
any cost has been estimated.

**MCQ 5.1.2 [topic 5.1.3, level: application — numerical]**
An option has capex of $50,000 and opex of $5,000 per year for 5 years. Using discount factors of 0.909,
0.826, 0.751, 0.683 and 0.621 (years 1–5, summing to 3.790), what is its discounted whole-life cost, to the
nearest $10?
A. $50,000
B. $68,950 ✔
C. $75,000
D. $18,950
*Rationale:* `PV(opex) = 5,000 × 3.790 = 18,950`; `Whole-life cost = 50,000 + 18,950 = 68,950`. A omits opex
entirely; C is the undiscounted total (`50,000 + 5,000×5`); D is the PV of opex alone, omitting capex.

**MCQ 5.1.3 [topic 5.1.4, level: application — numerical]**
A hire option costs $150 per unit with no fixed cost. An owned option costs a fixed $45,000 plus $100 per
unit. At what quantity do the two options cost exactly the same?
A. 300
B. 450
C. 900 ✔
D. 1,500
*Rationale:* `150Q = 45,000 + 100Q` → `50Q = 45,000` → `Q = 900`. A and B understate the breakeven; D
overstates it.

**MCQ 5.1.4 [topic 5.1.5, level: application — numerical]**
A control account's base cost elements are: Labour $220,000, Materials $95,000, Equipment $40,000,
Subcontract $15,000. A quantified risk register supports a contingency of $50,000. What is the resulting
cost baseline (BAC)?
A. $370,000
B. $420,000 ✔
C. $50,000
D. $470,000
*Rationale:* Base estimate `= 220,000 + 95,000 + 40,000 + 15,000 = 370,000`; `BAC = 370,000 + 50,000 =
420,000`. A omits contingency entirely; C states contingency alone; D double-counts contingency.

**MCQ 5.1.5 [topic 5.1.6, level: analysis]**
In the cost management framework, which statement correctly describes where contingency reserve and
management reserve sit relative to the cost baseline (BAC)?
A. Both sit inside the cost baseline
B. Contingency reserve sits inside the cost baseline; management reserve sits outside it, added to form the
   total project budget ✔
C. Both sit outside the cost baseline
D. Management reserve sits inside the cost baseline; contingency reserve sits outside it
*Rationale:* B restates the KA 3.1/KA 12.3 convention this framework depends on (§5.1.6): `BAC = Base
estimate + Contingency`; `Total project budget = BAC + Management reserve`. A and D misplace management
reserve; C ignores that contingency is, by definition, part of BAC.

**MCQ 5.1.6 [topic 5.1.7, level: application]**
A Project Controls Manager's delegation of authority permits approving a contingency drawdown up to $25,000
per event. A named, register-linked risk materialises with an actual cost of $32,000. What governance action
is required?
A. The Project Controls Manager approves it alone, because it is funded from contingency, not management
   reserve
B. The request must escalate beyond the Project Controls Manager, because it exceeds the delegated amount
   limit, even though it draws from contingency ✔
C. No approval is required, because the risk was already identified in the register
D. It must be reclassified as a management-reserve transfer before it can be approved
*Rationale:* B is the point of Example 5.1.7: the fund-type distinction (contingency vs management reserve)
and the amount-based DoA limit are separate governance dimensions, and either can independently trigger
escalation. A ignores the DoA limit; C ignores that being register-linked affects which fund applies, not
whether an amount limit still applies; D incorrectly conflates the two governance dimensions.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using Pipeline Segment 4B, explain how a base cost estimate built up by cost element (labour, materials,
   equipment, subcontract) reconciles to the same figure built up by work package, and how contingency is
   then added to reach BAC.
2. A capex option costs $35,000 with opex of $4,000 per year for 4 years; discount factors for years 1–4 are
   0.909, 0.826, 0.751 and 0.683. Calculate its discounted whole-life cost.
3. Using the hire-vs-owned trenching mini-case in §5.1.4, explain what the breakeven quantity means and why
   the owned option becomes progressively more attractive as scope grows beyond it.
4. Explain why "approve the cost baseline" and "approve a management-reserve transfer" are typically
   accountable to different levels of the organisation in the RACI matrix in §5.1.7, even though both actions
   change BAC.

**Answers**

1. Every coded cost in the CBS carries both a cost-element tag and a work-package tag simultaneously, so
   summing by either tag reproduces the same $540,000 base estimate (Example 5.1.5: labour $314,000 +
   equipment $30,000 + materials $180,000 + subcontract $16,000 = WP1 $80,000 + WP2 $140,000 + WP3 $170,000
   + WP4 $100,000 + WP5 $50,000 = $540,000 either way). Contingency of $60,000, sized from the quantified
   risk register (KA 12.2–12.3), is then distributed only to the three work packages whose risks it responds
   to (WP2 +$10,000, WP3 +$30,000, WP4 +$20,000), giving `BAC = 540,000 + 60,000 = $600,000`.
2. `PV(opex) = 4,000 × (0.909 + 0.826 + 0.751 + 0.683) = 4,000 × 3.169 = 12,676`. `Whole-life cost = 35,000 +
   12,676 = $47,676`.
3. The breakeven quantity (1,000 m) is the point at which the owned option's fixed mobilisation cost has
   been spread over enough metres that its lower variable rate exactly offsets the hire option's zero fixed
   cost; below it, hire is cheaper because the fixed cost has not yet been "earned back." Above 1,000 m, every
   additional metre costs $60 less under the owned option than under hire, so the total saving grows linearly
   with quantity beyond breakeven — $60,000 saved at 2,000 m, $120,000 at 3,000 m.
4. Cost-baseline approval (adding contingency to a base estimate for already-identified, in-scope risks) and
   management-reserve transfer (bringing genuinely unforeseen or newly approved scope inside the baseline)
   answer different governance questions, matching the known-unknown/unknown-unknown distinction KA 3.1.6 and
   KA 12.3.1 establish: the Project Controls Manager, operating within delegated authority (subject to the DoA
   limit, §5.1.7), can be Responsible for both, but only the sponsor is Accountable for authorising new,
   previously unbudgeted scope into the baseline — a decision that changes what the organisation has
   committed to fund, not merely how an already-committed amount is being spent.
