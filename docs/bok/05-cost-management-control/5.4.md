# Domain 5 — Cost Management & Cost Control

## KA 5.4 — Change control & cost impact

Every cost baseline built in Domain 3, every earned-value calculation in Domain 6, and every contingency
drawn down in KA 12.3 rests on one silent assumption: that the scope being measured is the scope that was
actually approved. That assumption holds only as long as **change** — to scope, cost or schedule — is
captured, assessed and formally incorporated through a disciplined process, rather than absorbed informally
on site and discovered later as an unexplained variance. This Knowledge Area is where that discipline is
taught: the purpose of integrated change control; the identify → assess → approve/reject → implement →
update-baseline process and the change register that records it; the four types of change a controls
professional meets (scope change, variation/VO, trend, claim); how to estimate a change's cost **and**
schedule impact together, not separately; trend management as the early-warning layer that precedes a
formal change; the governance choice between drawing contingency and formally re-baselining (built on, not
repeating, KA 12.3's reserve mechanics); the change control board (CCB) and its delegated authority levels;
and configuration management — keeping the cost baseline, schedule baseline and technical/drawing baseline
in agreement once a change is approved. Two related bodies of knowledge sit either side of this KA and are
deliberately not repeated here: KA 12.2–12.3 covers how *risk* is identified, quantified and funded through
contingency — a **known-unknown**, already priced into the baseline before it occurs; this KA covers how a
**change** — scope growth, a client instruction, a trend, a claim — is identified, priced and, if approved,
added to the baseline. Domain 7 (once written) covers the *contractual* mechanics of a variation or a claim
in full — entitlement, notice periods, FIDIC-style variation clauses, pricing and dispute resolution; this
KA covers the *project-controls* mechanics of what happens to the cost and schedule baseline once a change
is raised, regardless of which contractual door it came through.

The worked examples continue **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC**'s **Pipeline Segment 4B** (BAC $600,000 /
SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**; work packages WP1–WP5 from KA 6.1.4), picking up
exactly where KA 12.3.3 left the project's reserve position at the Month-4 data date: **BAC $605,000**
(the original $600,000 plus a $5,000 contingency top-up already traced in Example 12.3.3), **contingency
reserve remaining $0**, **management reserve remaining $25,000**, **total project budget $630,000**
(unchanged throughout KA 12.3). This KA introduces a new event on top of that position — a client-directed
scope addition — and shows, arithmetically, what integrated change control does with it.

### 5.4.1 The purpose of integrated change control, and why timing matters

**Definition & purpose.** PMI's PMBOK Guide names **Perform Integrated Change Control** as a core process
within Project Integration Management: reviewing every change request, approving or rejecting it, and
managing resulting changes to deliverables, project documents and baselines so that only *approved* changes
are ever incorporated. AACE International's Total Cost Management (TCM) Framework places change management
as a continuing process within cost and schedule control, tightly coupled to forecasting (KA 3.4, KA 6.3)
and to the contingency/management-reserve mechanics of KA 3.1 and KA 12.3. The word "integrated" is the
operative one: a single approved change simultaneously touches the cost baseline (BAC), the schedule
baseline, the scope/technical baseline (drawings, specifications — §5.4.7) and, under a contract, the
contract price itself (Domain 7, once written). Change control exists to make all of these move together,
deliberately and traceably — never silently, never one at a time, never after the fact.

**The underlying principle.** A change costs the least to absorb the moment it is first identified, and
costs progressively more the later it is caught — because a late-identified change usually requires undoing
completed work, not just doing new work. This is not a schedule-driven curiosity; it is the central
commercial argument for running a disciplined change process at all, rather than treating it as
paperwork that slows delivery down.

**Formula.** No single numerical law governs how much a late change costs, but the components are
predictable and additive:

`Cost of a change identified late = Cost of the same change identified early + Rework/demolition of
completed work + Remobilisation of plant/labour already stood down + Disruption or retesting of downstream
work already executed`

**Example 5.4.1 — the same change, caught early versus caught late (Pipeline Segment 4B).**

*Scenario.* §5.4.4 below prices a client-directed addition — a new isolation valve chamber on Pipeline
Segment 4B — at a **total cost impact of $38,000**, captured as a trend at Month 3 and formally approved as
Variation Order **VO-01** at Month 5, before the affected trench section is backfilled. Suppose instead the
same requirement had gone unrecorded until after WP4's pipe-laying and backfill were already complete.

| Cost component | Early (VO-01, as priced in §5.4.4, $) | Late (illustrative, after backfill, $) |
|---|---|---|
| Direct scope cost (civil, valve & materials, labour) | 32,000 | 32,000 |
| Re-excavation of completed, backfilled trench | — | 15,000 |
| Remobilisation of specialist trenching plant | — | 9,000 |
| Retesting of the already-completed weld-test sequence | — | 11,000 |
| Extended time-related overhead | 6,000 (5 working days × $1,200/day) | 12,000 (10 working days × $1,200/day) |
| **Total** | **38,000** | **79,000** |

*Required.* The cost penalty of late identification, in dollars and as a multiple.

*Working.* `Penalty = 79,000 − 38,000 = 41,000`; `Multiple = 79,000 / 38,000 = 2.08`

*Answer.* **Catching the change late more than doubles its cost (2.08×), a $41,000 penalty for identical
scope.**

*Interpretation.* Nothing about the underlying valve chamber changed between the two columns — only *when*
it was captured. The $41,000 difference is entirely re-excavation, remobilisation, retesting and extended
disruption: costs that exist only because completed work had to be undone. This is why §5.4.5's trend
register — capturing the requirement as an unconfirmed signal at Month 3, well before backfill — is not an
administrative nicety but the mechanism that made the $38,000 outcome possible at all.

**Mini-case — an unlogged verbal instruction.** A site supervisor on a different Meridian package accepts a
verbal instruction from the client's resident engineer to "just add the extra valve while you're there,"
without raising a change request. Three weeks later, the cost report shows an unexplained $34,000 variance
in WP4 with no register entry to explain it, no baseline update, and no record of who authorised it or
whether the client will pay for it. The work is technically correct, but the project controls team cannot
answer three questions a client audit will ask: was this approved, by whom, and against what budget? This is
**scope creep** in its purest form — not a large single failure, but the accumulation of small, individually
reasonable instructions executed outside the process this KA describes.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating change control as a bureaucratic delay rather than the mechanism that
keeps late-change penalties like Example 5.4.1's out of the project. (2) Allowing verbal or informal
instructions to be executed before a change request exists (the mini-case above) — the work may be entirely
legitimate, but its absence from the register makes it unauditable. (3) Confusing a **change to the
baseline** (this KA) with a routine **estimate revision** (KA 3.2) — an estimate can be refined freely as
information improves; a baseline changes only through this KA's formal process.

**AI in this topic.** Correspondence-mining tools can scan site diaries, emails, RFIs and meeting minutes for
language patterns resembling an informal instruction or an unconfirmed scope signal — such as the mini-case's
verbal instruction — and flag it for a change request to be raised, narrowing the gap between "it was said"
and "it was logged" (cross-reference KA 13.5). The tool can flag a *candidate*; it cannot determine whether
the instruction actually constitutes an approved change, was already covered by existing scope, or is
significant enough to warrant one — that classification is the professional's, and an unreviewed flood of
false positives can itself desensitise a team to genuine early warnings.

### 5.4.2 The change control process and the change register

**Definition & purpose.** The change control process converts an identified change into a governed decision
through five steps: **identify** (a change is first raised, from any source); **assess** (its cost and
schedule impact is estimated — §5.4.4); **approve/reject** (a decision is made at the correct authority
level — §5.4.6); **implement** (the approved work is executed); **update baseline** (cost, schedule and
technical baselines are revised to reflect it — §5.4.7). The **change register** (or change log) is the
single, continuously maintained record of every change moving through these five steps — the audit trail
that lets a controls professional, or an auditor, answer "what changed, who raised it, who approved it, and
what did it do to the baseline?" for any item, at any time.

**The underlying principle.** Skipping a step, not just skipping the register, is the more common failure:
approving a change without assessing its schedule impact (jumping identify → approve), or implementing a
change before it is formally approved (jumping assess → implement), each break the chain of accountability
the process exists to create. The register does not replace the process — it is the process's visible trace,
and a register with gaps (a change with no assessment recorded, or no linked baseline revision) is evidence
the process itself was not followed, not merely a paperwork omission.

**Formula.** No calculation; the process is a fixed sequence, with the register as its running state:

`Identify → Assess (cost impact + schedule impact) → Approve/Reject (at correct authority, §5.4.6) →
Implement → Update baseline (cost, schedule, technical — §5.4.7) → Close`

unremarkable, and the register's job is to make that visible too, not just to record the large items.

**Mini-case — a register catches a duplicate.** A second site engineer, unaware TR-04 already exists,
independently raises a near-identical trend for "possible additional isolation valve, WP3/4 boundary" in
Month 4. The register's search-before-raise discipline catches the duplicate before it is separately priced
and submitted to the client twice — a real, recurring failure mode on larger programmes with more than one
person authorised to log changes, and the single strongest practical argument for one register, not
several parallel spreadsheets.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Skipping the assess step and moving straight from identify to implement, common
under schedule pressure — this is exactly how the Example 5.4.1 mini-case's unlogged instruction happens.
(2) Approving a change but never executing the "update baseline" step, leaving EVM performance measurement
(KA 6.1–6.3) comparing actual progress against a baseline that no longer reflects the approved scope — the
precise failure KA 6.1.2's mini-case warns against. (3) A register with no unique, cross-referenced IDs
linking a trend to the change it becomes (TR-04 → VO-01), losing the traceability that makes Example 5.4.1's
early-versus-late comparison possible to demonstrate at all.

**AI in this topic.** Workflow-automation tools can route a raised change through each stage, notify the
correct approver at each authority level (§5.4.6), timestamp every transition, and flag an item that has
sat in "assess" beyond a target cycle time (cross-reference KA 13.4) — a genuine gain in consistency and
audit-readiness, particularly across a multi-package programme. Such tools manage the *workflow*, not the
*judgement*: they cannot perform the cost/schedule assessment (§5.4.4) or decide whether an item is approved
— an automated, fast-moving register built on an unreviewed cost estimate is simply a fast, wrong record.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.1.2 (the stale-baseline pitfall this process prevents); KA 5.4.3 (the types of
item that populate the register); KA 5.4.5 (the trend register as an upstream feeder, TR-04 → VO-01); KA
5.4.6 (approval authority); KA 5.4.7 (the "update baseline" step in full).

### 5.4.3 Types of change: scope change, variation/VO, trend and claim

**Definition & purpose.** Four distinct items commonly enter a change register, and confusing them changes
both the governance route and, on a contract, the commercial entitlement:

- **Scope change** — any change to the defined scope of work, from any source (client, design development,
  or the contractor's own error correction), used here as the general category.
- **Variation (or Variation Order, VO)** — the specific **contractual instrument** through which an
  owner/client formally instructs a scope change under the contract, typically priced and, once instructed,
  not usually disputed as to *whether* it happened (only as to its price) — the mechanics of variation
  clauses (commonly FIDIC-style, at concept level) sit in Domain 7 (once written), cross-referenced here, not
  repeated.
- **Trend** — an early, often unconfirmed or only roughly priced (ROM) indicator of a *probable future*
  change, tracked before it is formally instructed or submitted — AACE TCM's trending/forecasting practice,
  developed in full in §5.4.5.
- **Claim** — a request for additional time and/or money for an event the contractor believes is **not its
  own risk or responsibility** under the contract (e.g. client-caused delay, differing site conditions);
  unlike a variation, a claim's entitlement is typically **contested** until substantiated, and may require
  negotiation or formal dispute resolution — again, Domain 7 (once written) territory for the contractual
  mechanics; this KA's job is only to route it correctly into the register.

**The underlying principle.** The same underlying event can look like more than one of these depending on
who caused it and how confirmed it is — which is exactly why classification, not just logging, matters: it
determines whether an item needs a client instruction before proceeding (variation), can proceed on internal
authority alone (scope change), needs substantiation before any entitlement exists (claim), or should not
yet consume baseline budget at all because nothing is confirmed (trend).

**Example 5.4.3 — classifying five Pipeline Segment 4B items.**

*Scenario.*

| # | Item |
|---|---|
| A | A written instruction from the client's Engineer to add an isolation valve chamber not in the original scope |
| B | A site note, following an informal client design-review comment, that "the client may want an extra valve — not yet confirmed" |
| C | Meridian's formal submission for 10 additional days and $18,000 of prolongation cost, because the client was three weeks late releasing the tie-in permit |
| D | Meridian's own QA engineer identifies a drawing discrepancy requiring rework, corrected internally at Meridian's own cost, no client instruction, no schedule slip |
| E | A confirmed increase in measured trenching quantity under the remeasurement contract, because as-built ground levels differ from the tender drawings |

*Required.* Classify each item.

*Working/Answer.*

| # | Classification | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A | **Variation/VO** | A formal client instruction to change scope — the defining feature of a variation |
| B | **Trend** | Unconfirmed, no instruction yet — logged early as a probable future item (§5.4.5) |
| C | **Claim** | Contractor-initiated, alleging client-caused delay; entitlement contested pending substantiation |
| D | **Scope change (internal)** | No client instruction, no disputed entitlement — an internal correction within Meridian's own authority and cost |
| E | **Variation (remeasurement)** | A confirmed, priced quantity change under the contract's measurement mechanism (cross-reference KA 7.3, once written) |

*Interpretation.* Items A and E are both variations but arrive by different contractual routes (an
instruction versus a remeasurement); B is A's own earlier, unconfirmed self (§5.4.5 traces exactly this
conversion as TR-04 → VO-01); C is the only item whose entitlement is not yet settled; D never leaves
Meridian's own authority at all. All five, however they resolve, belong in the same register (§5.4.2).

**Mini-case — a claim that turns out to be a variation.** Meridian initially logs item C's underlying
event as a possible **claim** ("the client caused this delay"). During substantiation, correspondence shows
the client's Engineer had in fact issued a written instruction to delay the tie-in pending a separate
utility survey — which reclassifies the event as a **variation** (an instructed change to the sequence/
programme) rather than a contested claim. The reclassification matters commercially (an instructed variation
is normally not disputed as to cause, only priced; a claim must first establish that the client, not
Meridian, was responsible) — a live illustration of why classification is a substantive judgement, not
paperwork, and why it is reviewed as evidence develops, not fixed at first logging.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Logging an item as a variation before a formal instruction actually exists —

instead). (2) Treating every scope addition as automatically claimable, without first checking who directed
or caused it — much unentitled "claim" activity is simply an unlogged variation in disguise. (3) Leaving a
trend at "unconfirmed" indefinitely instead of closing it out (converted, or simply lapsed) once its status
is known, inflating the apparent open exposure on a project-controls dashboard.

**AI in this topic.** Natural-language classification tools can scan correspondence and suggest whether a
new item's language most resembles a variation instruction, a trend signal or a claim notice, based on
patterns such as instructive versus contested phrasing (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — a genuinely
useful first-pass triage on high-volume programmes. Genuinely contested classification — as the mini-case
shows, turning on documentary evidence of who instructed what — is a commercial and often legal judgement a
language-pattern model cannot resolve; it can propose a starting classification, never confirm one.

### 5.4.4 Estimating the cost and schedule impact of a change

**Definition & purpose.** Assessing a change means pricing **two** things, not one: its **cost impact** (the
direct cost of the new or altered scope, plus any indirect cost the change causes elsewhere) and its
**schedule impact** (the net effect on project completion, after accounting for any float the change can
absorb before it reaches the critical path). A change assessed on cost alone routinely understates its true
effect, because schedule slippage itself very often carries a cost — extended time-related overhead — that
a scope-only estimate never counts.

**The underlying principle.** The direct cost of new scope is a bottom-up estimate exactly like any other
(KA 3.2 methodology, applied here to a small increment rather than a whole project). The schedule impact is
not simply the new work's own duration — it is that duration **net of any float already available** at the
point the change is inserted (the logic behind AACE International's Recommended Practice 52R-06, *Time
Impact Analysis*, which inserts a change or delay event into the schedule network and reads off its net
effect on the finish date; full network mechanics — forward/backward pass, total float — are KA 10.2's
territory and are not repeated here). Only once the net schedule impact is known can any schedule-driven
indirect cost — extended overhead, standing plant, extended supervision — be quantified and added to the
direct cost to give the change's **total cost impact**.

**Formula(e).**

`Total cost impact = Direct cost of new/changed scope + Schedule-driven indirect cost`
`Schedule-driven indirect cost = Net schedule impact (working days) × Time-related overhead rate ($/working day)`
`Net schedule impact = New/changed activity duration − Available float at the point of insertion` (floored
at zero; a change that fits entirely within available float has a net schedule impact of zero)

**Example 5.4.4 — pricing and time-impacting Meridian's VO-01 (isolation valve chamber addition).**

*Scenario.* The trend first logged as TR-04 at Month 3 (§5.4.5) is confirmed by written client instruction
at Month 5: an additional isolation valve chamber, tying into WP4 (pipe-laying & welding), to allow future
maintenance isolation of Segment 4B without shutting down the full line. Meridian's estimating team prices
the direct scope bottom-up:

| Cost element | Amount ($) |
|---|---|
| Civil works (excavation, base slab, chamber structure) | 12,000 |
| Valve & specialist materials | 14,000 |
| Installation labour | 6,000 |
| **Subtotal — direct scope cost** | **32,000** |

A Level-3 (detailed field) schedule review of the WP4 tie-in sequence — one level of granularity finer than
the eight-activity CPM network in KA 10.2, which does not model individual tie-in points — finds the new
chamber work needs **8 working days** and identifies **3 working days** of float available at that specific
point in the sequence before it affects the downstream testing and commissioning work. The project's stated
time-related overhead rate (site supervision, standing plant, temporary facilities) is **$1,200 per working
day**.

*Required.* (a) The net schedule impact; (b) the schedule-driven indirect cost; (c) the total cost impact of
VO-01.

*Working.*

(a) `Net schedule impact = 8 − 3 = 5 working days`

(b) `Schedule-driven indirect cost = 5 × 1,200 = 6,000`

(c) `Total cost impact = 32,000 + 6,000 = 38,000`

*Answer.* **Net schedule impact = 5 working days; total cost impact = $38,000** (SAR 142,500 at the
illustrative rate).

*Interpretation.* $38,000, not $32,000, is the figure that must go forward into §5.4.6's baseline and
reserve decision — omitting the $6,000 schedule-driven component (the single most common estimating error
in this topic) would understate the true cost impact by 15.8% and, separately, would leave the schedule
baseline silently out of date even if the cost baseline were correctly updated. The $38,000 outcome also
falls squarely inside TR-04's earlier $25,000–$45,000 rough-order-of-magnitude range (§5.4.5) — a useful
sense-check that the trend process gave the team a materially correct early signal, not just an early one.

**Mini-case — pricing only the direct cost.** A different estimator on the same programme, asked to price a
comparable tie-in change on an adjacent segment, submits a figure of $29,500 covering civil works, materials
and labour only, with no schedule review performed. Three months later, the change is found to have added
4 working days to that segment's critical path, with no extended-overhead allowance ever recognised in the
baseline or reserve calculation — a real cost that was incurred but never assessed, and therefore never
funded from the correct reserve, surfacing instead as an unexplained adverse cost variance in a later EVM
report (KA 6.2) with no register entry to explain it.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Pricing only the direct scope cost and omitting the schedule-driven indirect cost

new activity's full duration as the schedule impact without first checking available float, overstating the
impact (the mirror error to pitfall 1). (3) Failing to sense-check the final figure against an earlier
trend's ROM range (§5.4.5) — a large, unexplained gap between the two is itself a signal worth investigating
before either figure is relied upon (echoing KA 6.3.1's bottom-up/formula-derived reconciliation).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted estimating tools can draft a direct-cost build-up from historical unit
rates and can run schedule-network float calculations automatically once a change activity is inserted into
a digital schedule model (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5), materially speeding up the assess step. Such
tools do not know the *specific* site float at a granular tie-in point unless the schedule model has been
built and kept current to that level of detail, and they cannot identify a knock-on disruption cost (the
mini-case's missed extended-overhead allowance) that has not been explicitly modelled as a schedule
consequence — the professional remains responsible for confirming both the direct estimate's rates and the
completeness of the schedule impact before either is reported.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2 (bottom-up cost-estimating method, applied here to a small increment); KA 10.2
(CPM network mechanics, total float, in full — not repeated here); KA 5.4.5 (TR-04's earlier ROM range,
sense-checked against this result); KA 5.4.6 (what this $38,000 figure triggers for the reserve and
baseline); KA 6.2, KA 6.3 (why an unassessed schedule impact surfaces later as an unexplained CV/SV or EAC
movement).

### 5.4.5 Trend management and early warning

**Definition & purpose.** A **trend** is an early, often unconfirmed or only roughly priced signal of a
*probable future* change — logged before a formal instruction, submission or approval exists. AACE
International's Total Cost Management (TCM) Framework describes trending as a structured, continuing
practice within forecasting: capturing emerging cost and schedule pressure as early as it can be reasonably
identified, so that forecasts (KA 3.4, KA 6.3) and reserve positions (KA 3.1, KA 12.3) can respond before,
not after, a change is confirmed. A trend does **not** change the baseline — it is deliberately kept
separate from approved scope until confirmed — but it *does* inform risk-based judgement about how much of
the remaining contingency or management reserve is genuinely still "free."

**The underlying principle.** Example 5.4.1 already showed the practical payoff: the $41,000 late-change
penalty exists only because nobody captured the requirement early. A trend register formalises "capture
early" into a repeatable discipline: log the signal, however soft, with a rough-order-of-magnitude (ROM)
range rather than a false-precision point estimate, track its status, and close it out — converted into a
formal change, or lapsed — rather than letting it sit indefinitely.

**Formula.** No calculation of its own; the discipline is a tracked, time-stamped range and a status field:

`Trend entry = {ID, date raised, description, ROM cost range, ROM schedule range, status}`, converted to a
formal change request (with a point-estimated cost and schedule impact, §5.4.4) once confirmed, or closed
if it lapses.

**Example 5.4.5 — Pipeline Segment 4B's trend register, Month 3–4.**

*Scenario.* Three items are logged as trends before any of them is confirmed:

| Trend ID | Date raised | Description | ROM cost range ($) | Status (Month 5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TR-04 | Month 3 | Possible additional isolation valve chamber, client design review | 25,000–45,000 | Converted → VO-01, actual $38,000 |
| TR-05 | Month 4 | Possible extended site access, adjacent utility works | 5,000–15,000 | Closed — did not proceed |
| TR-06 | Month 4 | Potential re-sequencing of WP4, weld-inspection backlog | 3,000–8,000 | Still open, monitoring |

*Required.* Assess the value of the register at Month 5.

*Working/Answer.* TR-04 converts into VO-01 at an actual cost ($38,000) that lands inside its original ROM
range — validating both that the signal was real and that the range was usable for early reserve planning.
TR-05 closes without cost — a reminder that not every trend becomes a change, and that closing a trend
cleanly is as much a register discipline as opening one. TR-06 remains genuinely open at Month 5: it
represents up to $8,000 of *unconfirmed* exposure that a controls professional should disclose alongside
the confirmed reserve position (§5.4.6), not ignore because it is not yet a formal change.

*Interpretation.* A trend register's value is not that every entry becomes a change — TR-05 shows the
opposite is equally normal — but that when one *does* convert, as TR-04 did, the organisation is not caught
by surprise: an approximate cost was already visible two months before an instruction existed, and
Example 5.4.1 showed exactly what that two-month head start was worth in dollars.

**Mini-case — a register that never closes anything.** A different project's trend register accumulates
eleven open trends over six months, none ever marked closed or converted, several clearly superseded by
later events. A funding review reads the register literally and treats the sum of all eleven ROM ranges as
"at-risk exposure," materially overstating the project's true remaining risk and triggering an unnecessary
escalation. The failure is not that trends were logged — that is correct practice — but that the register was
never *maintained*: closing a lapsed trend is exactly as important as opening a live one, and an unmaintained
register actively misleads rather than merely under-informing.

review rather than informing it. (3) Treating an unconfirmed trend's ROM range as if it were an approved
budget change — the baseline must stay untouched until formal approval (§5.4.6), however confident the
early signal feels.

**AI in this topic.** Anomaly-detection and text-mining tools scanning site diaries, RFIs and meeting minutes
at scale can surface candidate trend signals earlier and more consistently than manual review, and can flag
a trend that has been open unusually long for review or closure (cross-reference KA 13.5) — genuinely
valuable at the volume a large programme generates. Such tools cannot confirm whether a soft signal is real
exposure or noise, nor set the ROM range itself with any reliability absent a comparable historical data
set; a poorly tuned model risks flooding the register with low-value entries that themselves need triage,
which is a cost, not a saving, if left unmanaged by the professional.

**Cross-references.** KA 5.4.1 (why early capture matters, quantified); KA 5.4.2 (the register a trend feeds
into once confirmed); KA 5.4.4 (VO-01's confirmed pricing, sense-checked against TR-04's range); KA 3.4, KA
6.3 (forecasting and EAC — an open trend is exposure a forecast should be aware of, even unconfirmed).

### 5.4.6 Re-baselining vs contingency draw-down: the change control board and authority levels

**Definition & purpose.** Once a change is priced (§5.4.4), a project controls professional must route it
through the correct funding and approval path. KA 12.3 already established the underlying distinction in
full: a **contingency drawdown** funds a **named, pre-identified, in-scope risk** already sitting in the
risk register, and — critically — needs no *new* approval up to the available balance, because the risk and
its response were pre-approved when the register itself was baselined. A **baseline change** (re-baselining)
is required for anything that is **not** already in the register — scope growth, a client instruction, a
confirmed trend — and needs fresh approval at an authority level set by its size, because it is, by
definition, new commitment the organisation has not already pre-approved. This topic does not re-derive
KA 12.3's sizing or drawdown-curve mechanics; it adds the governance layer that decides, for a **change**
specifically, who may approve it and at what threshold.

**The underlying principle.** The two funding routes differ in *what kind of approval already exists*, not
in dollar size alone — KA 12.3.3's $42,000 contingency draw against the pre-identified ground-conditions
risk needed no fresh sign-off precisely because the register already carried that approval; a $38,000
**change**, by contrast, is new commitment with no prior approval at all, and must clear a delegated
authority ladder before a dollar of it is spent.

**Formula.** No numerical formula; a delegated-authority table, cumulative by change value:

| Authority level | Approves new commitment up to | Funding source |
|---|---|---|
| Cost controller (register only) | Zero-cost / administrative items | — |
| Project Controls Manager | $10,000 | Contingency reserve balance only, for genuinely in-scope items |
| Project Manager | $25,000 | Contingency reserve, or a pre-agreed management-reserve allocation |
| Change Control Board (CCB), chaired by the sponsor's delegate | Full available management reserve | Management reserve → transferred into BAC |
| Client/Sponsor (formal contract or budget amendment) | Any amount exceeding available management reserve | Supplemental funding → total project budget |

*(Pre-identified, in-scope risk realisations against the KA 12.2 register are pre-authorised regardless of
size, up to the available contingency balance, per KA 12.3.1 — the table above governs new commitment
only.)*

**Example 5.4.6 — routing VO-01 through the authority ladder, and its effect on the reserve position.**

*Scenario.* At Month 5, before VO-01, Pipeline Segment 4B's reserve position (carried forward from KA 12.3.3)
is: BAC $605,000; contingency remaining $0; management reserve remaining $25,000; total project budget
$630,000. VO-01's assessed total cost impact (§5.4.4) is $38,000 — new commitment, not a risk-register item.

*Required.* (a) The correct approval route; (b) the resulting reserve and baseline position.

*Working.*

(a) $38,000 exceeds the Project Manager's $25,000 threshold, so it is escalated to the **CCB**. The CCB can
approve drawing the full remaining management reserve ($25,000), but that leaves a shortfall:

`Shortfall = 38,000 − 25,000 = 13,000`

$13,000 exceeds even the CCB's available management reserve, so it escalates one further level, to the
**Client/Sponsor**, for supplemental funding — consistent with VO-01 being a client-directed variation, for
which additional funding is the expected, not exceptional, outcome (Domain 7, once written, covers the
contractual mechanics of the client paying for an instructed variation).

(b)

| | Before VO-01 | After VO-01 approval |
|---|---|---|
| BAC | 605,000 | 605,000 + 38,000 = **643,000** |
| Contingency reserve remaining | 0 | 0 |
| Management reserve remaining | 25,000 | 25,000 − 25,000 = **0** |
| Supplemental sponsor funding | — | **13,000** |
| Total project budget | 630,000 | 630,000 + 13,000 = **643,000** |
| Planned completion | End Month 6 | Month 6 + 5 working days (§5.4.4) |

*Check:* `New BAC (643,000) + Management reserve remaining (0) = 643,000 = New total project budget` ✓

*Answer.* **BAC rises to $643,000; management reserve is fully exhausted; $13,000 of supplemental funding
is required beyond the original $630,000 total project budget; planned completion moves back by 5 working
days.**

*Interpretation.* This is the mechanical consequence of KA 3.1's rule that management reserve, once used,
raises BAC — restated here for a change rather than a risk-driven top-up. Unlike KA 12.3.3's $5,000 top-up
(itself a management-reserve draw to replenish an exhausted *contingency* for an already-approved risk),
VO-01's $38,000 is entirely new, client-directed scope: the reserve mechanics are identical, but the
governance route (CCB, then sponsor) and the underlying cause (a change, not a risk realisation) are not.

**Mini-case — a risk and a change, side by side.** In the same status period, KA 12.3.3's R1 (ground
conditions, $42,000 actual cost) is drawn from contingency with no fresh approval, because it is a
pre-identified, already-budgeted risk. VO-01 ($38,000) requires CCB and sponsor approval, because it is new,
client-directed scope. The two dollar amounts are similar; the governance paths are entirely different — the
single clearest illustration in this KA of why "how much" is the wrong first question, and "is this already
in the register, or is it new commitment?" is the right one.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Routing a legitimate contingency drawdown for a named risk through the full CCB

"contingency" to avoid CCB scrutiny, a misclassification that both breaches governance and, per KA 12.3's
own pitfalls, erodes the contingency available for the risks it actually exists to cover. (3) A Project
Manager approving a change above their delegated threshold under time pressure, bypassing the CCB entirely —
an authority breach regardless of whether the change itself was reasonable.

**AI in this topic.** Dashboard tools can track cumulative approved changes in real time against remaining
contingency and management-reserve balances and automatically flag when a pending item would exceed a given
authority level's threshold (cross-reference KA 4.3, KA 4.4, once written; KA 13.5) — a genuine, low-risk
automation given the arithmetic is fully deterministic. They cannot decide whether an item is correctly
classified as a risk-register drawdown versus new commitment in the first place (pitfall 2 above), nor make
the CCB's or sponsor's approval decision itself — those remain accountable, human decisions at every rung of
the ladder.

### 5.4.7 Configuration management basics

**Definition & purpose.** **Configuration management** is the discipline of identifying, documenting and
controlling every version of the documents that define a project's current, approved state — its
**configuration items**: technical drawings and specifications, the cost baseline, the schedule baseline,
and the cost-coding/BoQ structure that ties cost to scope (KA 1.5). A change is not complete once it is
approved and funded (§5.4.6) — it is complete only once **every** affected configuration item has been
revised, cross-referenced to the change that caused it, and the superseded version is clearly retired.

**The underlying principle.** Cost, schedule and technical baselines are updated by different people, in
different systems, usually on different timetables — a finance/controls update to BAC does not
automatically trigger a drawing revision, and vice versa. Configuration management is the discipline that
keeps these updates **synchronised**: every configuration item touched by a given change carries that
change's ID, so that at any moment, every document a field team, a cost controller or an auditor might
consult describes the *same* current, approved scope — never a mix of old and new.

**Formula.** No calculation; the control is a cross-referenced revision register:

`Configuration item revision = {Item, prior revision ID, new revision ID, change ID that caused it, date
superseded}` — every configuration item touched by a change must carry that change's ID, and no change is
"closed" (§5.4.2) until every one of its configuration items shows a matching revision.

**Example 5.4.7 — VO-01's configuration record.**

*Scenario.* VO-01 is approved and funded (§5.4.6). Four configuration items are affected:

| Configuration item | Prior revision | Revised (post VO-01) | Change reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| P&ID drawing, WP4 tie-in | Rev B | Rev C | VO-01 |
| Cost baseline (BAC) | $605,000 — Baseline Rev 2 | $643,000 — Baseline Rev 3 | VO-01 |
| Schedule baseline (planned completion) | End Month 6 — Baseline Rev 2 | +5 working days into Month 7 — Baseline Rev 3 | VO-01 |
| Cost-code register (WP4) | Cost code 4B-WP4-000 ($120,000) | New cost code 4B-WP4-010 added, valve chamber ($38,000); WP4 total $158,000 | VO-01 |

*Required.* Confirm the change is complete under configuration management, and reconcile WP4's revised total.

*Working.* `WP4 revised total = 120,000 (original) + 38,000 (VO-01, new cost code 4B-WP4-010) = 158,000`

*Answer.* All four items carry the same change reference (VO-01), each superseded revision is explicitly
retired (Rev B, Baseline Rev 2), and WP4's revised total of **$158,000** reconciles exactly to its original
$120,000 plus VO-01's $38,000 — the change is complete under configuration management only once this table
has no blank cells.

*Interpretation.* The cost-code addition (4B-WP4-010) is what lets KA 6.1's EV measurement methods
(percentage-complete, weighted milestones) be applied to the new chamber work specifically, without
distorting WP4's original method or progress readings for the unrelated pipe-laying and welding scope
already under way.

**Mini-case — a lagging drawing revision.** VO-01's cost and schedule baselines are correctly revised
(Baseline Rev 3 issued) two days after CCB approval, but the P&ID drawing revision (Rev C) is not issued by
the design office for a further two weeks. In the interim, a field crew, working from the still-current
Rev B drawing on site, nearly proceeds with backfill on the original alignment, which would have required
re-excavation to accommodate the new chamber — precisely the late-change penalty priced in Example 5.4.1,
narrowly avoided only because a site supervisor cross-checked the change register before backfilling. The
cost and schedule baselines being correct was not, by itself, sufficient; the technical baseline lagging
behind them created exactly the exposure configuration management exists to prevent.

which revision is actually current. (3) Treating configuration management as a document-control-only
concern, disconnected from cost and schedule control — in fact all three must move together, on the same
change ID, for a change to be genuinely complete.

that lacks a corresponding revision in one or more configuration items — for example, VO-01 approved with a
revised cost baseline but no matching drawing revision logged — functioning as exactly the traceability
check that would have caught the mini-case's lagging drawing sooner (cross-reference KA 13.4). Such tools
confirm that *a* revision exists against a change ID; they cannot confirm the revision's technical *content*
is correct — that the new Rev C drawing actually reflects the approved chamber design — which remains an
engineering sign-off, not a system check.

### Key terms

- **Integrated change control** — the disciplined process of identifying, assessing, approving/rejecting,
  implementing and baselining every change to scope, cost or schedule, so only approved changes are
  incorporated.
- **Change register/log** — the continuously maintained, cross-referenced record of every change through
  the process, from raising to close.
- **Scope change** — a general change to defined scope, from any source.
- **Variation / Variation Order (VO)** — the contractual instrument through which an owner/client formally
  instructs a scope change under the contract.
- **Trend** — an early, often unconfirmed or ROM-priced signal of a probable future change, tracked before
  formal instruction or approval.
- **Claim** — a request for additional time and/or money for an event the contractor asserts is not its own
  risk or responsibility; entitlement is contested until substantiated.
- **Total cost impact** — direct cost of new/changed scope plus schedule-driven indirect (time-related
  overhead) cost.
- **Net schedule impact** — a change's added activity duration, net of available float at its point of
  insertion.
- **Time Impact Analysis (TIA)** — inserting a change into the schedule network and reading its net effect on
  the finish date (AACE RP 52R-06).
- **Change control board (CCB)** — the formally chartered group with delegated authority to approve changes
  above individual thresholds.
- **Delegated authority** — the pre-agreed approval limit at each role/level in the change hierarchy.
- **Re-baselining** — the formal act of revising the cost, schedule or scope baseline once a change is
  approved.
- **Contingency drawdown vs baseline change** — funding a pre-identified, in-register risk (no new approval,
  KA 12.3) versus funding new commitment (approval required at the correct authority level, this KA).
- **Configuration management** — controlling and cross-referencing every version of the documents (cost,
  schedule, technical) that define the project's current approved state.
- **Configuration item** — a specific controlled document or baseline (a drawing, the cost baseline, the
  schedule baseline, the cost-code register).
- **Scope creep** — the informal, unlogged accumulation of small changes outside the formal process.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 5.4.1 [topic 5.4.4, level: application — numerical]**
A change's direct scope cost is $32,000. A schedule review finds the change needs 8 working days, with 3
working days of float available before it affects the critical path, and the project's time-related overhead
rate is $1,200 per working day. What is the total cost impact of the change?
A. $32,000
B. $35,600
C. **$38,000** ✔
D. $41,600
*Rationale:* Net schedule impact = 8 − 3 = 5 working days; schedule-driven cost = 5 × 1,200 = 6,000; total =
32,000 + 6,000 = 38,000. A omits the schedule impact entirely; B mistakenly uses the float (3 days) rather
than the net impact; D mistakenly uses the full 8-day duration instead of netting off available float.

**MCQ 5.4.2 [topic 5.4.6, level: application — numerical]**
A change is priced at $38,000. Management reserve remaining is $25,000, and no contingency remains. What
supplemental funding, beyond the current total project budget, is required?
A. $0
B. $25,000
C. **$13,000** ✔
D. $38,000
*Rationale:* Shortfall = 38,000 − 25,000 = 13,000 (Example 5.4.6). B mistakes the drawn management reserve
for the shortfall; D fails to net off the management reserve already applied; A wrongly assumes the change
is fully funded.

**MCQ 5.4.3 [topic 5.4.3, level: analysis]**
A written instruction from the client's Engineer directs a contractor to add scope not in the original
contract. How should this be classified?
A. Trend
B. Claim
C. **Variation/VO** ✔
D. Scope change (internal)
*Rationale:* A formal client instruction to change scope is the defining feature of a variation (§5.4.3). A
trend is unconfirmed; a claim asserts client-caused entitlement for an event, not an instructed addition; an
internal scope change carries no client instruction.

**MCQ 5.4.4 [topic 5.4.6, level: analysis]**
A pre-identified, in-scope risk from the risk register materialises, and its cost is drawn from contingency
within the available balance. Why does this draw typically need no new (CCB-level) approval, even if it
exceeds a manager's normal delegated threshold for new commitment?
A. Contingency draws are always below any approval threshold
B. **The register already carries the necessary approval when the risk was originally baselined** ✔
C. Contingency, unlike management reserve, is never subject to any governance
D. The Project Manager's authority automatically extends to any amount for a named risk
*Rationale:* B states KA 12.3.1's governing distinction, reused here (§5.4.6): pre-approval already exists in
the register. A is not generally true — draw size can exceed thresholds, as KA 12.3.3's $42,000 example
shows. C is false — contingency drawdown is still monitored (KA 12.3.3). D overstates the mechanism; it is
the register's pre-approval, not unlimited personal authority, that applies.

**MCQ 5.4.5 [topic 5.4.1, level: application — numerical]**
A change's direct scope cost is $32,000 whether caught early or late. Caught late, it also requires $15,000
of re-excavation, $9,000 of remobilisation and $11,000 of retesting, plus $12,000 of extended overhead
(versus $6,000 if caught early). By how much more does the late change cost, in total?
A. $35,000
B. $38,000
C. **$41,000** ✔
D. $47,000
*Rationale:* Late total = 32,000+15,000+9,000+11,000+12,000 = 79,000; early total = 32,000+6,000 = 38,000;
difference = 79,000 − 38,000 = 41,000 (Example 5.4.1). A and D are plausible arithmetic slips; B restates the
early total rather than the difference.

**MCQ 5.4.6 [topic 5.4.2, level: recall]**
In the standard change control process, which step must occur immediately before a change may be approved
or rejected?
A. Implement
B. Update baseline
C. **Assess** ✔
D. Close
*Rationale:* The sequence is identify → assess → approve/reject → implement → update baseline (§5.4.2); a
change must be assessed for cost and schedule impact before a decision-maker can approve or reject it. A and
B both occur only after approval; D occurs last.

3. TR-04 was logged as a trend at Month 3 with a ROM range of $25,000–$45,000 and converted to VO-01 at
   Month 5 at an actual cost of $38,000. Referencing Example 5.4.1, explain what would likely have happened
   to the eventual cost if the requirement had not been captured as a trend at all.
4. **Numerical.** A Change Control Board has $18,000 of management reserve remaining. An approved change is
   priced at $30,000, and the total project budget before the change is $500,000. Calculate (a) the amount
   drawn from management reserve, (b) the supplemental funding required, and (c) the resulting total project
   budget.

**Answers**

1. VO-01's total cost impact includes its $32,000 direct scope cost (civil works, valve and materials,
   labour) plus $6,000 of schedule-driven indirect cost — 5 net working days of critical-path impact (8
   working days of new work, less 3 working days of available float) priced at the project's $1,200/working-
   day time-related overhead rate. Pricing only the $32,000 direct figure omits a real cost the change
   causes and understates its true effect by $6,000 (15.8%).
2. Contingency reserve funds risks already identified, quantified and included in the cost baseline when the
   risk register was itself approved (KA 12.3.1); that approval already covers any realisation of the named
   risk up to the available contingency balance, so no fresh, size-based authorisation is needed. The
   delegated-authority table in §5.4.6 governs *new* commitment — items with no prior approval, such as a
   change — not the drawdown of an already-approved reserve.
3. Example 5.4.1 shows that catching the same requirement only after WP4's trenching was backfilled would
   have added re-excavation ($15,000), plant remobilisation ($9,000), retesting of completed weld-test work
   ($11,000) and a longer extended-overhead period ($12,000 instead of $6,000) — a total of $79,000 against
   the $38,000 actually incurred, more than doubling the cost of identical scope purely because of when it
   was identified.
4. (a) Amount drawn from management reserve = min(18,000, 30,000) = **$18,000**. (b) Supplemental funding
   required = 30,000 − 18,000 = **$12,000**. (c) Resulting total project budget = 500,000 + 12,000 =
   **$512,000**.
