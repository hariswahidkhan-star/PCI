# Domain 5 — Cost Management & Cost Control

## KA 5.2 — Cost control cycle

Every dollar reported on a project cost report passes through four distinct states before it is finally
known with certainty, and a controls professional who cannot name which state a given dollar is in cannot
reliably answer the two questions every sponsor eventually asks: "what have we spent?" and "what will this
project finally cost?" This Knowledge Area is about that progression — the **cost control cycle**:
**commitment** (an order or subcontract has been placed, creating a forward financial exposure), **accrual**
(work has been done or goods received, but no invoice has yet arrived), **actual** (a cost has been
recognised, on an accrual-consistent basis, in the cost ledger), and **forecast** (what the remaining,
not-yet-committed or not-yet-received scope is expected to add). It is the operational discipline that AACE
International's **Total Cost Management (TCM) Framework** treats as core cost-engineering practice, and it
is the discipline that produces the very Actual Cost (AC/ACWP) figure Domain 6's earned value calculations
depend on — this KA does not repeat that EVM machinery (KA 6.1–6.4 cover it in full) but shows, cross-
referenced by number rather than reproduced, exactly *where* a clean, defensible AC figure actually comes
from before it ever reaches an EVM report. It builds directly on the transactional Procure-to-Pay mechanics
already worked in full in **KA 11.2** — the purchase order, the goods/services receipt note (GRN), the
three-way match and the GRNI accrual — and takes the cost-controller's, rather than the transaction-
processor's, view of the same data: aggregating many open commitments and accruals into a single, reconciled
cost position for a control account, and turning that position into a forecast. This KA continues **Meridian
Rail Contracting LLC's Pipeline Segment 4B** project (Budget at Completion, BAC, $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at
an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1), introduced in KA 6.1 and developed operationally in KA 11.2, and traces
one specific Meridian cost element — the Al-Noor Site Services pipe-welding subcontract on Work Package 4 —
through the full cycle across two reporting periods, with a full reconciliation. It cross-refers forward to
KA 5.1 (cost types and drivers), KA 5.3 (the cost breakdown structure and control accounts this cycle
reports against) and KA 5.4 (change control, the formal mechanism behind the commitment change worked in
5.2.9), all authored alongside or after this KA.

### 5.2.1 The cost control cycle: commitment → accrual → actual → forecast

**Definition & purpose.** The cost control cycle is the disciplined, repeating sequence by which a project
controls function converts a forward financial exposure into a known, reported cost, and then into a
forecast of the total cost still to come. Four states describe any dollar of project cost at a point in
time:

- **Commitment** — the value of a purchase order, subcontract or other binding order placed against the
  budget. Nothing has yet been received; the organisation has created an *exposure*, not yet a *cost*.
- **Accrual** — the value of goods received or work verified as performed, for which no supplier invoice has
  yet been booked. The cost has genuinely been incurred, even though no invoice exists yet to prove it.
- **Actual cost** — the accrual-adjusted cost recognised in the cost ledger to date: everything invoiced and
  booked, plus everything still accrued. This is the same AC/ACWP defined in KA 6.1.1 and KA 6.1.3.
- **Forecast** — the predicted cost of the scope not yet committed, received or invoiced, added to actual
  cost to produce an Estimate at Completion (EAC). KA 6.3 develops the full EAC formula family; this KA's
  job is to show where the *inputs* to a bottom-up forecast — the open commitment, in particular — actually
  come from (5.2.6).

**The underlying principle — narrowing uncertainty, not narrowing cost.** Each stage of the cycle does not
change how much a piece of work will ultimately cost; it changes how *certain* the organisation can be about
that cost. A commitment fixes a price and quantity with a supplier but nothing has yet been checked against
reality; an accrual confirms physical receipt or verified performance, removing supplier-side uncertainty but
not yet supplier-invoice uncertainty; an actual cost removes that too. A forecast, by contrast, moves in the
other direction — it reintroduces uncertainty deliberately, for the part of the scope that has not yet
reached any of the first three, more certain states. The cycle is therefore best understood as a funnel:
budget (least certain, a plan) narrows through commitment, accrual and actual (each more certain than the
last) while forecast reopens the funnel again for whatever remains. Skipping a stage — recognising a
commitment as if it were an actual cost, or ignoring a genuine accrual because no invoice has arrived — does
not remove the underlying uncertainty; it simply reports a false level of certainty to the reader of the
cost report.

**Formula.** The stock relationship that ties the four states together for a single cost element or PO line:

`Actual cost (accrual-adjusted) = Invoiced/posted cost to date + Accrued cost (received, not yet invoiced)`

`Open commitment = Committed value to date − Cumulative value received (GRN-verified) to date`

`Forecast (EAC, cost element) = Actual cost to date + Estimate to Complete (ETC)`,
where, on a bottom-up basis, `ETC = Open commitment (at committed rates) + Uncommitted future cost estimate`
— developed fully in 5.2.6 and, at project level, in KA 6.3.1.

**Illustrative mini-case — two projects, same cash spent, very different cost positions.** Two control
accounts each report $100,000 of actual cost this month. Control account A has no open commitments — every
dollar of its remaining $50,000 budget is genuinely uncommitted, available headroom. Control account B has
already issued purchase orders totalling a further $80,000 against its remaining $50,000 budget — it is
already $30,000 over-committed, even though its *reported actual cost* looks identical to A's. A cost report
built from actual cost alone cannot distinguish the two; only a cost control cycle that also tracks
commitment can show that B, not A, is the one heading for a budget breach next month. This is the core
justification for tracking all four states, not actual cost alone, and is worked in full at cost-element
level in 5.2.9.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Missing accruals** — treating "no invoice yet" as "no cost yet," so a genuinely incurred cost is simply
  absent from the actual cost figure until the invoice happens to arrive (developed fully in 5.2.3).
- **Double counting commitments** — recognising the full committed value of a PO as actual cost the moment it
  is issued, before anything has been received, then recognising the invoice again in full when it arrives,
  overstating cost by the committed amount for as long as both are (wrongly) carried.
- Treating the cycle as a one-way, one-time process rather than a **recurring monthly discipline** — every
  stage must be refreshed at every reporting period, not established once at project start (5.2.8).

**AI in this topic.** Workflow and ERP-embedded automation can track a cost element's progress through all
four states in real time and flag where a stage transition looks overdue (a commitment open far longer than
its typical delivery lead time; an accrual open far longer than a typical invoice lag) — a genuine,
low-risk efficiency and completeness aid (cross-reference KA 13.5). It cannot judge *why* a stage is stuck
(a genuine dispute, a slow but legitimate supply chain, a data-entry gap) — that diagnosis remains the
professional's, and an automated "ageing" flag is a prompt to investigate, never a basis for silently
reclassifying a cost's state.

transactional mechanics behind commitment, receipt and accrual); KA 5.3 (control accounts, the level at

### 5.2.2 Committed cost and commitment management: purchase orders and subcontracts

**Definition & purpose.** A **commitment** is a binding financial exposure created the moment an
organisation places a purchase order or executes a subcontract: a defined quantity, at a defined price, from
a named supplier, with defined delivery terms. **Commitment management** is the discipline of maintaining an
accurate, current register of every open commitment against a control account, so that the account's true
remaining exposure — not merely its cash spent to date — is always known.

**The underlying principle.** A control account's *reported* remaining budget headroom is meaningless unless
open commitments are netted off it, because a commitment is a real, near-certain future cash outflow even
though no cost has yet been recognised against it (KA 11.2.2 develops the full "available budget" formula
this principle rests on). Commitment tracking exists to prevent the same budget being spent twice, in good
faith, by two different requisitioners who each see only the account's cash-spent-to-date and wrongly assume
the rest is free.

**Formula.**

`Open commitment (this PO/subcontract) = Committed value to date − Cumulative value received (GRN-verified) to date`

`Total control-account commitment exposure = Σ (open commitment, all open POs/subcontracts against that account)`

**Example 5.2.2 — the commitment register for Pipeline Segment 4B at the Month-3 data date.**

*Scenario.* Continuing KA 11.2's Procure-to-Pay examples, three purchase orders are open against Pipeline
Segment 4B at the Month-3 data date:

| PO reference | Supplier | Committed value (USD) | Cumulative GRN-verified value (USD) | Open commitment (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SC-PO-4B-014 (WP4, welding) | Al-Noor Site Services | 50,000 | 25,000 | 25,000 |
| MAT-PO-4B-009 (WP2, valves) | Al Rashid Valve & Fittings Trading | 50,000 | 50,000 | 0 |
| MAT-PO-4B-011 (WP2, valves) | Al Rashid Valve & Fittings Trading | 25,000 | 22,500 (90 of 100 units received, KA 11.2.5) | 2,500 |
| **Total** | | **125,000** | **97,500** | **27,500** |

*Required.* Total open commitment exposure across the three POs.

*Working.* `Total open commitment = 25,000 + 0 + 2,500 = 27,500`

*Answer.* **$27,500** of open commitment (SAR 103,125) remains against these three purchase orders — real
future cash exposure that neither the "cash spent to date" figure nor an unadjusted actual-cost report would
show.

*Interpretation.* MAT-PO-4B-009 is fully received and closed to commitment (zero open balance); MAT-PO-4B-011
still has a live, if small, open balance pending the short-shipped units (KA 11.2.5); SC-PO-4B-014 carries the
largest open exposure of the three, at exactly the halfway point of its 1,000 m scope. A commitment register
built this way is the single source that answers "what could still land on this control account?" — a
question actual cost alone cannot answer.

**Mini-case — a variation changes a commitment before it changes an actual cost.** In Month 4, the client's
site engineer instructs an additional 20 m of tie-in welding on the Al-Noor package, at the same $50/m unit
rate, formalised as an approved amendment to SC-PO-4B-014. The moment the amendment is approved — *before*
a single additional metre is welded — the commitment register must be updated: `Revised commitment =
50,000 + (20 × 50) = 51,000`. No accrual and no actual cost yet exist for the additional 20 m; only the
*committed* exposure has grown. A commitment register that is only updated when the corresponding invoice
finally arrives would under-report Meridian's true exposure for weeks or months after the client's
instruction was given — exactly the gap commitment management exists to close. (The full worked
consequences of this variation are carried through to completion in 5.2.9.)

**Common pitfalls.**
- Approving a new requisition against a control account's *gross* remaining budget without first netting off
  open commitments already issued — silently double-spending budget already earmarked (KA 11.2.2's central
  pitfall, restated here at the register-aggregation level).
- Leaving a commitment open on the register long after the supplier has confirmed cancellation or after the
  scope has been re-let elsewhere — a "zombie" commitment overstates exposure just as surely as a missing
  accrual understates cost.
- Updating the commitment register only when an invoice arrives, rather than when the PO or its amendment is
  actually approved — this collapses the commitment stage into the actual stage and defeats the purpose of
  tracking commitment separately at all.

**AI in this topic.** A commitment-tracking dashboard can aggregate hundreds of open PO lines across many
control accounts in real time and flag accounts approaching or exceeding their available budget headroom —
valuable at portfolio scale where manual aggregation would lag by days or weeks (cross-reference KA 13.5).
It cannot judge whether an old, apparently "zombie" commitment is truly dead or merely delayed for a
legitimate reason (a paused delivery, a supplier dispute); that determination needs the procurement or site
team's direct knowledge, not just the register's ageing.

### 5.2.3 Accruals for work done but not yet invoiced

**Definition & purpose.** An **accrual**, in the cost control cycle, is the recognition of a cost for goods
received or work verified as performed, where no supplier invoice has yet been booked. In the
Procure-to-Pay cycle this is specifically the **goods-received-not-invoiced (GRNI)** accrual, fully
mechanised in KA 11.2.6; this topic treats it from the cost controller's side — as the input a monthly cost
report cannot afford to be without.

**The underlying principle.** The matching concept (KA 1.3) requires cost to be recognised in the period the
related work was actually performed, not the later period an invoice happens to arrive in. A cost report
built purely on booked invoices is a **cash/invoice-date basis** report, not an accrual basis one, and will
systematically understate the true cost position for any work performed close to the reporting cut-off —
precisely the point made from the EVM side in KA 6.1.3's accrual-lag pitfall, and shown in full P2P detail
in KA 11.2.6.

**Formula.**

`Accrual required (this cost element, period-end) = Cumulative value received/verified to date − Cumulative value invoiced and booked to date`

which, restated for a single open PO line, is exactly KA 11.2.6's GRNI formula: `GRNI accrual = Cumulative
GRN value − Cumulative invoice value booked`.

**Example 5.2.3 — the Al-Noor accrual at the Month-3 close.**

*Scenario.* Reusing KA 11.2.3's and KA 11.2.6's figures for SC-PO-4B-014: at the Month-3 close, 500 m of
welding has been GRN-verified ($25,000) and no invoice has yet been booked (Al-Noor's typical invoice lag is
3–4 weeks).

*Required.* The accrual required at the Month-3 close, and the accrual-adjusted actual cost for this
element.

*Working.*
`Accrual = 25,000 (GRN-verified) − 0 (invoiced) = 25,000`
`Actual cost (accrual-adjusted) = 0 (invoiced) + 25,000 (accrued) = 25,000`

*Answer.* **Accrual = $25,000**; **accrual-adjusted actual cost = $25,000** — entirely accrual at this data
date, with nothing yet invoiced.

*Interpretation.* Without this accrual, this cost element would report **zero** actual cost at Month 3,
despite 500 m of genuinely completed, inspected and verified welding sitting on the ground — a materially
misleading position for any cost report or EVM comparison (KA 6.2.1) built on top of it.

**Mini-case — the cost cut-off for plant hire (a distinct, self-contained example).** Pipeline Segment 4B's
trenching work (WP3) uses excavation plant hired from Desert Plant Hire Co. at a day rate of $1,200/machine-
day, invoiced monthly in arrears. Meridian's cost cut-off is close of business on the last calendar day of
each month. In April, the final three working days (28–30 April) of plant usage — 3 machine-days — are
logged on site, but Desert Plant Hire's invoice covering that period is not received until 5 May.

*Required.* State the correct period for recognising the 3-day cost, and the effect of getting it wrong.

*Working.*
`Accrual required at the April cut-off = 3 machine-days × $1,200/day = 3,600`

Under the **correct (accrual) treatment**: April's actual cost includes the $3,600 accrual; when the actual
invoice is booked in May, the April accrual is reversed and the genuine invoice line takes its place (KA
1.3.5's reversing-entry mechanism), so May's actual cost is *not* charged again for the same 3 days.

Under the **incorrect (invoice-date) treatment**: April's actual cost excludes the $3,600 (understated by
$3,600); May's actual cost includes the full $3,600 when the invoice finally lands (overstated by $3,600 for
work that was not performed in May at all).

*Answer.* The $3,600 belongs in **April**, accrued at the April cut-off, regardless of when Desert Plant Hire
happens to submit its invoice.

*Interpretation.* Note what does **not** change between the two treatments: total cost across April and May
combined is identical either way ($3,600, once). What changes is *which month's* reported cost, and any
cost-performance comparison (CV, KA 6.2.1) built on that month's data, is correct. A cut-off error is a
timing error, not a total-cost error — but a timing error is still a real reporting error, because monthly
performance reporting and forecasting decisions are made on a period-by-period basis, not only on
project-to-date totals.

**Common pitfalls.**
- **Missing accruals** — the single most common and most consequential cost control cycle error: treating
  "no invoice received" as "no cost incurred," which silently understates every period's actual cost for
  work performed near the cut-off.
- Accruing the *full remaining committed value* of a PO instead of only the *GRN-verified/measured* portion
  actually received — KA 11.2.6's pitfall, restated: only what has genuinely been received is accrued.
- Failing to reverse an accrual once the real invoice is booked, causing the same cost to be counted twice
  across two periods (the mirror-image error to a missing accrual).

**AI in this topic.** Document-intelligence and workflow tools can compare site usage logs, GRNs and
supplier billing cycles against the cost cut-off calendar and auto-flag cost elements likely to need an
accrual at each period-end — a genuine completeness aid, particularly valuable across a large number of open
commitments where manual review would miss the smaller, easily forgotten tail items (cross-reference KA
13.5). It cannot verify that a flagged usage log is itself accurate, nor substitute for the site-level
confirmation that work was genuinely performed as logged — the professional's sign-off on the accrual amount
remains essential before it is posted.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.3.2, KA 1.3.3, KA 1.3.5 (the matching concept and reversing-entry mechanism this
topic applies); KA 1.4.1 (the accrual-versus-provision boundary — a GRNI-style accrual from a measured
receipt is normally a straightforward accrual, not an IAS 37 provision); KA 6.1.3 (the accrual-lag pitfall
from the EVM side); KA 11.2.6 (the full GRNI mechanics and journal entries this topic reuses); 5.2.9 (the
Al-Noor accrual carried through to a full two-period reconciliation).

### 5.2.4 Actual cost capture and the cost ledger

**Definition & purpose.** **Actual cost capture** is the process of consolidating every recognised cost —
invoiced and booked, plus accrued — into the project's **cost ledger**: the running, cumulative record of
cost by cost element, control account and period, which is both the source of the AC/ACWP figure used in
Domain 6 and the primary output a cost controller reports each month.

**The underlying principle.** Actual cost is drawn from several distinct source systems, each capturing a
different type of cost element, and each with its own natural lag and verification method: a **timesheet or
payroll system** for labour, the **Procure-to-Pay system** (KA 11.2) for materials and subcontract cost, an
**equipment/plant log** for owned or hired equipment, and, where relevant, a **provisions register** for
estimated liabilities (KA 1.4). The cost ledger's job is to bring all of these together onto one common,
accrual-consistent, correctly coded basis — none of these source systems, taken alone, is "the" actual cost.

**Formula.**

`Cost ledger actual cost (period, cost element) = Σ (invoiced/posted amounts from all source systems) + Σ (accruals raised, this cost element) − Σ (accruals reversed, this cost element)`

**Example 5.2.4 — reconstructing Pipeline Segment 4B's Month-3 cost ledger by source system.**

*Scenario.* Reusing KA 6.1.3's Month-3 cost-element table, now attributed to its source system:

| Cost element | Amount (USD) | Primary source system |
|---|---|---|
| Labour | 140,000 | Timesheet/payroll |
| Materials | 95,000 | Procure-to-Pay (PO/GRN/invoice) |
| Equipment | 30,000 | Equipment/plant log |
| Subcontract | 15,000 | Procure-to-Pay (PO/GRN/invoice) |
| **Ledger total (unadjusted)** | **280,000** | |
| Al-Noor accrual (5.2.3) | 25,000 | Procure-to-Pay (GRNI) |
| **Ledger total (accrual-adjusted)** | **305,000** | |

*Required.* The accrual-adjusted actual cost, and which source systems it draws from.

*Working.* `280,000 (unadjusted ledger total) + 25,000 (Al-Noor GRNI accrual) = 305,000`

*Answer.* **$305,000** accrual-adjusted actual cost, drawn from three distinct source systems (timesheet/
payroll, Procure-to-Pay, equipment log) plus one accrual posting.

*Interpretation.* This is the identical figure already established in KA 6.1.3's mini-case and resolved
operationally in KA 11.2.6; this topic's purpose is only to make explicit that "the actual cost" is not one
number pulled from one system, but a consolidation across several, each with its own timing and verification
characteristics — which is exactly why a period-end reconciliation between the consolidated cost ledger and
each source system (5.2.5) is a necessary control, not an optional extra.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Sourcing "actual cost" from a single system (most often Procure-to-Pay alone) and omitting labour or
  equipment cost captured elsewhere, understating true actual cost.
- Applying different cut-off dates across source systems (timesheets closed on the 28th, invoices captured
  to the 31st) without reconciling the mismatch — the cost ledger's data date is only as reliable as its
  least disciplined source system.
- Treating the cost ledger figure as self-evidently correct because it is "what the ERP says" — an ERP
  consolidates exactly what has been posted to it; a source-system gap upstream produces a clean-looking but
  incomplete ledger total downstream.

**AI in this topic.** Robotic process automation and AI-assisted reconciliation tools can extract, timestamp
and consolidate postings from multiple source systems into the cost ledger automatically, and flag where one
source system's cut-off appears to lag another's (cross-reference KA 13.5) — a genuine efficiency and
consistency gain over manual consolidation. It does not verify that any individual source system's
underlying data (a timesheet, a GRN, an equipment log entry) is itself correct or complete; a fast, automated
consolidation of an incomplete source simply produces a fast, incomplete cost ledger.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.5 (chart of accounts and cost coding — the structure the ledger is organised
by, developed fully there and not repeated here); KA 6.1.3 (AC/ACWP, the EVM figure this ledger produces);
KA 11.2 (the Procure-to-Pay source system in full); 5.2.5 (cost coding and reconciliation across source
systems, immediately following).

### 5.2.5 Cost coding and reconciliation from source systems: data integrity

**Definition & purpose.** **Cost coding** is the practice, developed fully in KA 1.5, of tagging every
transaction with a segmented cost code — project, WBS phase, discipline and natural account — so that cost
can be reported at any level of the cost breakdown structure. This topic applies that structure specifically
to Pipeline Segment 4B and closes the cost control cycle's data-integrity loop: confirming that what the
cost ledger reports **reconciles** to what the source systems actually captured, at the level of an
individual, coded cost element.

**The underlying principle.** Coding happens once, at the point a transaction is captured (a PO line, a
timesheet entry, a GRN); every downstream report — the cost ledger, a control-account roll-up, an EVM
comparison — inherits whatever coding decision was made at that single point. A reconciliation between the
coded cost ledger and the underlying source-system transaction detail (exactly the check formalised in KA
1.5.5) is what confirms no transaction has been coded to the wrong element, omitted entirely, or
double-posted, before that data is relied on for forecasting or reporting.

**Formula.** Restating KA 1.5.5's reconciliation identity, applied at cost-element level:

`Σ (Coded cost ledger detail, this element) = Σ (Source-system transaction detail, same element, same period)`

— necessary but not sufficient (KA 1.5.5): a clean reconciliation confirms completeness of the total, not
that every individual transaction was coded to the *correct* element.

**Example 5.2.5 — coding and reconciling the Al-Noor cost element.**

*Scenario.* The Al-Noor welding package is coded `4B.30.02.530` — Pipeline Segment 4B, WBS phase 30
(Installation & Testing), discipline 02 (pipeline/mechanical), natural account 530 (Subcontract cost) —
following the segmented cost-code structure of KA 1.5.2. At the Month-3 close, the cost ledger reports
$25,000 against this code (Example 5.2.3); the underlying Procure-to-Pay source detail for the same code
shows one GRN (GRN-4B-142, $25,000, KA 11.2.3) and no booked invoice.

*Required.* Reconcile the coded cost ledger figure to the source-system detail.

*Working.* `Coded ledger detail (4B.30.02.530) = 25,000` vs `Source-system detail (GRN 25,000 + invoice 0 +
accrual 25,000 already reflected once, not twice) = 25,000` — the two agree exactly.

*Answer.* **No reconciling break** — the $25,000 coded to `4B.30.02.530` is fully explained by one GRN and
one accrual posting, with no invoice yet booked.

*Interpretation.* A clean reconciliation like this confirms *completeness* (nothing is missing, nothing is
duplicated) but, per KA 1.5.5's own qualification, does not on its own prove the $25,000 was coded to the
*correct* WBS phase or discipline — that judgement still rests on someone confirming the work itself was
genuinely Installation & Testing, pipeline/mechanical scope, which it is.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a clean total-to-total reconciliation as proof of correct classification — KA 1.5.4b's
  miscoding mini-case shows precisely how a reconciliation can balance while individual amounts sit against
  the wrong WBS phase or discipline.
- Reconciling only at project close rather than every reporting period, by which point any coding error has
  already propagated through months of cost and performance reports.
- Allowing ad-hoc, unapproved cost codes "just this once" for an unusual transaction — these are
  disproportionately the codes that later prove hardest to reconcile.

**AI in this topic.** Automated classification tools can propose a cost code for a new transaction from its
description with a stated confidence score (KA 1.5.6 develops this in full), and anomaly-detection models
can flag a coded transaction that is statistically unusual for its assigned code combination — both are
genuinely useful accelerants for coding at volume. Neither replaces the reconciliation control itself, which
is a deterministic, rules-based check, nor the professional judgement of whether a clean reconciliation
actually reflects correct classification, not merely equal totals.

**Cross-references.** KA 1.5.1–1.5.3 (the chart of accounts, cost breakdown structure and cost-code
anatomy this element's code follows — not repeated here); KA 1.5.5 (the reconciliation logic and its "necessary
but not sufficient" limitation, applied here); KA 1.5.6 (AI-assisted classification and anomaly detection in
full); 5.2.4 (the cost ledger this reconciliation validates).

### 5.2.6 Cost-to-complete and the Estimate at Completion linkage

**Definition & purpose.** This topic makes explicit the point at which the cost control cycle hands off to
forecasting: the **open commitment** developed in 5.2.2, together with an estimate of any genuinely
**uncommitted** future scope, *is* the raw material of a **bottom-up Estimate to Complete (ETC)** — the
independent, re-estimate route to an EAC introduced conceptually in KA 6.3.1. This topic does not repeat KA
6.3's EAC formula family (Methods 1–3, their assumptions, or the choice between them); it shows, concretely,
where a defensible bottom-up figure is actually assembled from.

**The underlying principle.** A bottom-up ETC is only as good as the commitment and accrual data behind it.
For scope that is already committed (an open PO or subcontract), the most defensible near-term forecast is
usually the *committed rate itself* — there is no more reliable predictor of what a specific 500 m of
welding will cost than the price already agreed with the supplier for exactly that scope, provided no
variation or performance issue is expected. For scope not yet committed at all, forecasting falls back on
estimating methods (KA 3.2) rather than commitment data, because there is, by definition, no commitment yet
to draw on.

**Formula.**

`Bottom-up ETC (cost element) = Open commitment (at committed/PO rates) + Uncommitted future cost estimate (for scope not yet ordered)`

`Bottom-up EAC (cost element) = Actual cost to date + Bottom-up ETC`

**Example 5.2.6 — the bottom-up EAC for the Al-Noor cost element at Month 3.**

*Scenario.* At the Month-3 data date (Example 5.2.2–5.2.3): actual cost (accrual-adjusted) = $25,000; open
commitment on SC-PO-4B-014 = $25,000; the remaining 500 m of welding is fully covered by the existing PO, so
there is no uncommitted future scope for this element.

*Required.* The bottom-up ETC and EAC for this cost element.

*Working.*
`Bottom-up ETC = 25,000 (open commitment) + 0 (uncommitted scope) = 25,000`
`Bottom-up EAC = 25,000 (actual) + 25,000 (ETC) = 50,000`

*Answer.* **ETC = $25,000**; **EAC = $50,000** — exactly the original committed value of SC-PO-4B-014.

*Interpretation.* This is precisely the "bottom-up (independent re-estimate)" route KA 6.3.1 describes in
general terms, now shown built directly from operational commitment data rather than from an abstract
re-estimating exercise: because the remaining scope is fully covered by a firm-price PO with no known
variation, the bottom-up EAC is, in this instance, simply the committed value — a useful, low-effort check
against any statistical (top-down) EAC method computed at project level in KA 6.3.2–6.3.4. Where a cost
element's remaining scope is *not* fully committed, or where a known issue (a price escalation clause, a
disputed variation) makes the committed rate unreliable, the bottom-up ETC must instead draw on a genuine
re-estimate (KA 3.2), not merely on the open commitment figure.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating the committed value as automatically the correct ETC even where a known, credible risk to that
  price exists (a pending claim, a confirmed but unpriced variation) — the committed rate is a strong
  starting point, not an unconditional guarantee.
- Confusing a **bottom-up** EAC (built element by element from commitment and estimate data, as here) with a
  **top-down**, formula-derived EAC (KA 6.3.2–6.3.4, built from project-level CPI/SPI) — both are legitimate,
  and KA 6.3.1 recommends using one as a check on the other, not choosing only one.
- Forgetting to include genuinely **uncommitted** scope in the ETC at all, because commitment data alone is
  visible and easy to query, while scope not yet ordered requires a deliberate estimating step that is easy
  to skip.

**AI in this topic.** A reporting-automation tool can roll up open commitment data across an entire control
account in seconds and produce a first-pass bottom-up ETC/EAC automatically, refreshed at every data date
(cross-reference KA 13.5) — a genuine, low-risk efficiency gain, since the underlying arithmetic is
deterministic once the commitment register is complete and correctly coded. It cannot know whether a
committed rate is actually still reliable (a supplier in financial difficulty, an unresolved dispute) or
whether uncommitted future scope has been estimated at all — both remain matters for the professional's
review before a bottom-up figure is reported alongside, or in place of, a top-down EAC.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.3.1 (the bottom-up ETC/EAC concept in full, including reconciling it against a
formula-derived figure); KA 3.2 (cost estimation methods, used here for genuinely uncommitted scope); KA 5.2.2
(the open commitment data this ETC is built from); KA 6.3.2–6.3.4 (the top-down EAC formula family this
bottom-up figure checks against, not repeated here).

### 5.2.7 Value of work done versus cost incurred

**Definition & purpose.** **Value of work done (VOWD)** is the value, at contract or budget rates, of the
physical work completed to date — a term used throughout commercial and contract administration, and
particularly in interim payment applications (cross-reference KA 7.3, KA 7.4), for what is conceptually the
same measurement as Earned Value (EV/BCWP, KA 6.1.1). This topic sets VOWD against **cost incurred** — the
accrual-adjusted actual cost this KA has built up through 5.2.1–5.2.5 — from the cost controller's,
commercial reporting perspective, without repeating KA 6.2.1's formal treatment of Cost Variance (CV).

**The underlying principle.** VOWD and EV answer the same underlying question — "how much of the scope,
valued at agreed rates, has genuinely been completed?" — but are commonly measured through different
mechanisms: EV through an internal, pre-agreed EVM measurement method (KA 6.1.4), VOWD often through
certified quantities under a Bill of Quantities or a subcontract's own certification process (KA 7.3). Where
both are properly measured against the *same* scope and the *same* data date, they should closely align;
where they diverge materially, the divergence itself is a signal that one of the two measurement processes
needs investigating, not that the two concepts are actually measuring different things.

**Formula.** No new formula distinct from KA 6.2.1's CV; restated here in VOWD/cost-incurred language for
commercial reporting:

`Cost-incurred gap = Value of work done (VOWD) − Cost incurred (accrual-adjusted actual cost)`

A positive gap is favourable (more value delivered than it has cost to deliver); a negative gap is adverse.

**Example 5.2.7 — VOWD versus cost incurred, Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 3.**

*Scenario.* Reusing figures already established elsewhere in this book: VOWD (≈ EV, KA 6.1.4) = $330,000;
cost incurred (accrual-adjusted actual cost, KA 6.1.3) = $305,000.

*Required.* The cost-incurred gap, and its interpretation for a commercial cost report.

*Working.* `Gap = 330,000 − 305,000 = 25,000`

*Answer.* **+$25,000**, a favourable gap.

*Interpretation.* This is numerically identical to the accrual-adjusted CV already noted in KA 6.1.3's
mini-case; the figure is repeated here deliberately to make one point: this favourable position is *only*
visible once cost incurred is accrual-complete. Using the unadjusted ledger AC of $280,000 instead would
report a gap of $50,000 — materially overstating how favourable the true position is, purely because a
genuine $25,000 accrual (5.2.3) was omitted. A cost controller reporting VOWD against cost incurred therefore
has a direct, practical stake in this KA's accrual discipline: a materially wrong gap is not a measurement
problem with VOWD or EV, it is a data-completeness problem in cost incurred.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Comparing VOWD (certified/contract-rate value) against cost incurred without confirming both are measured
  to the *same* data date and the *same* scope — a common source of an apparently large, spurious gap.
- Reporting the cost-incurred gap without disclosing whether cost incurred is accrual-adjusted, materially
  changing how favourable or adverse the figure appears to a reader.
- Treating VOWD and EV as interchangeable without checking which one a particular report or contract clause
  actually requires — they are closely related, not always identical, particularly where a BoQ certification
  process and an internal EVM measurement method diverge in their timing or method (KA 7.3, KA 6.1.4).

**AI in this topic.** Dashboard tools can compute and refresh the VOWD-versus-cost-incurred gap automatically
across many control accounts and flag where it moves materially period-on-period (cross-reference KA 4.4,
KA 13.5). They cannot judge whether an observed gap reflects genuine cost performance or an accrual-
completeness problem in the underlying cost incurred figure — exactly the distinction this topic exists to
draw, and one that still requires the professional to trace the gap back to its source before reporting it.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.1.1, KA 6.1.4 (EV/BCWP, the internal-measurement counterpart to VOWD); KA 6.2.1
(Cost Variance — the formal EVM treatment of this same comparison, not repeated here); KA 7.3, KA 7.4 (Bills
of Quantities and interim payment applications, where VOWD terminology is used directly); 5.2.3 (the accrual
discipline this topic's example depends on).

### 5.2.8 The monthly cost control routine

**Definition & purpose.** The **monthly cost control routine** is the repeating cycle of activities a project
controls function performs every reporting period to keep the commitment register, accruals, actual cost and
forecast all current, reconciled and ready for management reporting (KA 4.3). It is the operational
"heartbeat" that makes everything covered in 5.2.1–5.2.7 happen reliably, period after period, rather than as
a one-off exercise.

**The underlying principle.** Because every stage of the cost control cycle depends on the one before it —
an accrual cannot be assessed without knowing what has been received; actual cost cannot be finalised
without accruals; a forecast cannot be trusted without a current commitment register — the routine must run
in a fixed, disciplined **sequence**, anchored to a single, non-negotiable **cost cut-off date** each period
(5.2.3), rather than as a set of activities performed independently and reconciled only when convenient.

**The routine, stage by stage:**

| Step | Activity | Primary input |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Confirm the period's cost cut-off date and communicate it to all cost-data sources | Reporting calendar |
| 2 | Capture actual cost from all source systems (labour, P2P, equipment) to the cut-off | 5.2.4 |
| 3 | Update the commitment register: new POs/subcontracts, amendments, closures | 5.2.2 |
| 4 | Raise (or reverse) accruals for work done/received but not yet invoiced | 5.2.3 |
| 5 | Reconcile the coded cost ledger to source-system detail | 5.2.5 |
| 6 | Update cost-to-complete and the forecast (bottom-up and/or formula-derived) | 5.2.6, KA 6.3 |
| 7 | Compare VOWD/EV to cost incurred; investigate any material or unexplained gap | 5.2.7, KA 6.2 |
| 8 | Report: management cost report, variance commentary, forecast disclosure | KA 4.3 |

**Example 5.2.8 — sequencing failure and its consequence.**

*Scenario.* A control account's cost team runs Step 8 (reporting) before completing Step 4 (accruals) for
the period, because the report is due before all supplier invoices for the period have been chased.

*Required.* State the consequence for the reported cost position.

*Working/Answer.* Any cost genuinely incurred but not yet invoiced or accrued is simply absent from the
report — an understated actual cost, an overstated (too favourable) VOWD-versus-cost-incurred gap (5.2.7),
and a forecast built on an artificially low actual-cost base. The report is not "approximately right, pending
minor updates"; it is built on an incomplete cost ledger and should not be issued as if it were complete
without at least a stated caveat on what remains to be accrued.

*Interpretation.* The routine's sequence is not bureaucratic ordering for its own sake — each step is a
genuine input dependency for the ones that follow, and skipping or reordering a step produces a specific,
predictable class of reporting error, not a random one.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Running steps out of sequence under reporting-deadline pressure (Example 5.2.8), producing a

- Allowing the cost cut-off date to drift or differ silently between source systems (5.2.4's pitfall,

  project controls, site/procurement and finance — several steps (commitment updates, accrual verification)
  depend on information only the site or procurement team holds.

**AI in this topic.** Workflow-automation tools can enforce the routine's sequence directly — blocking Step
8's report generation until Steps 2–7 are marked complete for the period — turning a procedural discipline
into a system-enforced control (cross-reference KA 13.5). This is a genuinely reliable, low-risk use of
automation because it enforces a known, deterministic sequence; it does not, and should not, silently relax
the sequence "to hit a deadline," which would simply move the Example 5.2.8 failure mode from a human
decision to an automated one.

### 5.2.9 Worked example — the Al-Noor welding subcontract through the cost control cycle, Month 3 to Month 5

**Definition & purpose.** This topic draws 5.2.1–5.2.6 together into the single exhibit a cost controller
would actually prepare: one Meridian cost element, tracked through commitment, accrual, actual cost and
forecast, across two full reporting periods, with a complete reconciliation of the movement between them.

**Example 5.2.9 — the Al-Noor Site Services pipe-welding package (WP4, PO SC-PO-4B-014), Month 3 to Month 5.**

*Scenario.* Continuing Examples 5.2.2–5.2.3 and KA 11.2.3–11.2.8: PO SC-PO-4B-014, originally 1,000 m of
pipe welding at $50/m ($50,000), coded `4B.30.02.530`. At Month 3: 500 m GRN-verified ($25,000), no invoice
booked, full $25,000 accrued. In Month 4 (per KA 11.2.8): Al-Noor's invoice for the 500 m is booked
($25,000), the Month-3 accrual is reversed, 10% retention ($2,500) is withheld, and $22,500 net cash is
paid; $25,000 of the PO remains open (the remaining 500 m). In Month 4, the client's site engineer
additionally instructs 20 m of extra tie-in welding at the same $50/m rate, formalised as an approved PO
amendment. By the Month-5 data date, the remaining 500 m plus the 20 m variation (520 m in total) has been
completed, inspected and GRN-verified, and Al-Noor's final invoice for that quantity has been received and
matched.

*Required.* (a) The revised commitment after the variation; (b) the Month-5 GRN, accrual and actual cost
position; (c) the cost element's bottom-up ETC and EAC at Month 3 and at Month 5; (d) a full reconciliation
of the EAC movement between the two periods; (e) the final retention position.

*Working.*

(a) *Revised commitment:* `50,000 (original) + (20 m × 50/m) = 50,000 + 1,000 = 51,000`

(b) *Month-5 receipt, invoice and accrual:*
`GRN value, Month 5 = 520 m × 50/m = 26,000`
`Cumulative GRN to date = 25,000 (Months 3–4) + 26,000 (Month 5) = 51,000` (matches the revised commitment
exactly — the PO is now fully received)
Al-Noor's final invoice for 520 m = `520 × 50 = 26,000`, matched with zero variance (price $50/m unchanged;
quantity 520 m ≤ cumulative GRN increment 520 m; value $26,000 ≤ remaining PO value of 51,000 − 25,000 =
26,000 — three-way match passes per KA 11.2.4's logic).
`Cumulative invoice booked to date = 25,000 (Month 4) + 26,000 (Month 5) = 51,000`
`GRNI accrual at Month-5 close = 51,000 (cumulative GRN) − 51,000 (cumulative invoice booked) = 0` — fully
invoiced, nothing left to accrue.
`Actual cost (accrual-adjusted), Month 5 = 25,000 (already recognised, Months 3–4) + 26,000 (Month-5 invoice) = 51,000`

(c) *Bottom-up ETC and EAC:*

| Measure | Month 3 | Month 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Commitment (cumulative, revised) | 50,000 | 51,000 |
| Cumulative GRN (received/verified) | 25,000 | 51,000 |
| Cumulative invoice booked | 0 | 51,000 |
| GRNI accrual | 25,000 | 0 |
| Actual cost (accrual-adjusted) | 25,000 | 51,000 |
| Open commitment | 25,000 | 0 |
| Bottom-up ETC | 25,000 | 0 |
| Bottom-up EAC (cost element) | 50,000 | 51,000 |

Check, Month 3: `Actual 25,000 + ETC 25,000 = EAC 50,000` ✓, and `EAC 50,000 = Commitment 50,000` (no
variance, remaining scope priced at the committed rate). Check, Month 5: `Actual 51,000 + ETC 0 = EAC 51,000`
✓, and `EAC 51,000 = Commitment 51,000` (fully closed out, no variance against the current, revised
commitment).

(d) *Reconciliation of the EAC movement, Month 3 to Month 5:*

`EAC movement = EAC(Month 5) − EAC(Month 3) = 51,000 − 50,000 = 1,000`

`Movement explained by: approved scope variation (20 m × $50/m) = 1,000`
`Movement explained by: price variance, productivity variance or other unexplained cause = 0`
`Check: 1,000 (variation) + 0 (unexplained) = 1,000` ✓ — the entire movement is accounted for.

Comparing final actual cost against the **original**, pre-variation commitment: `51,000 − 50,000 = 1,000`
adverse — but, per the reconciliation above, wholly attributable to an approved, client-instructed scope
change (cross-reference KA 5.4), not to a cost overrun on the originally committed scope, which closed at
exactly its committed rate with zero variance.

(e) *Retention:*
`Retention, Month-5 certificate = 10% × 26,000 = 2,600`
`Net cash paid, Month-5 certificate = 26,000 − 2,600 = 23,400`
`Cumulative retention held = 2,500 (Month 4) + 2,600 (Month 5) = 5,100`
Check: `Cumulative retention 5,100 = 10% × cumulative invoiced 51,000 = 5,100` ✓

*Answer.* The Al-Noor cost element closes at a final **EAC of $51,000** against a **revised commitment of
$51,000** — a zero variance against the current, authorised commitment, with the entire $1,000 movement from
the Month-3 EAC fully and cleanly explained by an approved 20 m scope variation. Cumulative retention held is
**$5,100**, with **$45,900** paid in net cash across the two certificates ($22,500 + $23,400).

*Interpretation.* This is what a defensible cost-control-cycle reconciliation looks like: every dollar of
movement between two reporting periods is traced to a named, specific cause (here, a single approved
variation), with nothing left over as an unexplained residual. A cost report that instead simply stated "EAC
increased by $1,000" without this trace would leave a reader unable to distinguish legitimate, authorised
scope growth from a genuine cost-control failure — precisely the distinction KA 6.3.6's EAC diagnostic
sequence asks a professional to make at project level, now demonstrated at the level of a single, fully
traceable cost element.

### Key terms

- **Cost control cycle** — the repeating sequence commitment → accrual → actual → forecast by which a
  forward financial exposure becomes a known cost and, ultimately, a forecast.
- **Commitment** — the value of a purchase order or subcontract placed, a forward exposure not yet an
  actual cost.
- **Open commitment** — committed value to date less cumulative GRN-verified value received to date.
- **Accrual (GRNI accrual)** — the value of goods received or work verified as performed but not yet
  invoiced, recognised as cost under the matching concept.
- **Actual cost (accrual-adjusted)** — invoiced/posted cost to date plus accrued cost; the same measure as
  AC/ACWP (KA 6.1.1, KA 6.1.3).
- **Cost cut-off (date)** — the fixed period-end date to which all source systems' data must be captured and
  accrued for a period's cost report to be complete.
- **Cost ledger** — the consolidated, accrual-adjusted, coded record of actual cost by cost element,
  control account and period, drawn from multiple source systems.
- **Bottom-up ETC/EAC** — an Estimate to Complete/Estimate at Completion built from open commitment data
  plus a genuine estimate of uncommitted scope, rather than from a top-down statistical formula (KA 6.3.1–
  6.3.4).
- **Value of work done (VOWD)** — the value, at contract/budget rates, of physical work completed to date;
  conceptually aligned with Earned Value (EV/BCWP) but often measured through certified quantities in a
  commercial/BoQ context (KA 7.3, KA 7.4).
- **Cost-incurred gap** — VOWD minus accrual-adjusted actual cost; numerically the same relationship as Cost
  Variance (CV, KA 6.2.1), viewed from a commercial cost-control perspective.
- **Monthly cost control routine** — the fixed, repeating sequence of steps (cut-off, capture, commitment
  update, accrual, reconciliation, forecast update, gap review, reporting) performed every period.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 5.2.1 [topic 5.2.1, level: recall]**
Which sequence correctly describes the cost control cycle for a purchased item?
A. Actual cost → Commitment → Accrual → Forecast
B. **Commitment → Accrual → Actual cost → Forecast** ✔
C. Forecast → Commitment → Actual cost → Accrual
D. Accrual → Commitment → Forecast → Actual cost
*Rationale:* An order is committed before anything is received (accrued) or invoiced (actual); the forecast
then predicts what remains beyond the actual cost recognised so far. A, C and D each place a later-stage
event before an earlier one it logically depends on.

**MCQ 5.2.2 [topic 5.2.2, level: application — numerical]**
A purchase order is issued for $80,000. To date, goods/services receipt notes (GRNs) totalling $52,000 have
been recorded against it. What is the open commitment?
A. $132,000
B. $80,000
C. $52,000
D. **$28,000** ✔
*Rationale:* `Open commitment = Committed value − Cumulative GRN-verified value = 80,000 − 52,000 = 28,000`
(5.2.2). A wrongly adds the two figures; B ignores what has already been received; C mistakes the received
value for the open balance.

**MCQ 5.2.3 [topic 5.2.3, level: application — numerical]**
At a period-end, cumulative GRN-verified value on a PO is $40,000 and $28,000 has been invoiced and booked.
What accrual (GRNI) should be raised?
A. $68,000
B. $40,000
C. $28,000
D. **$12,000** ✔
*Rationale:* `Accrual = Cumulative GRN value − Cumulative invoice value booked = 40,000 − 28,000 = 12,000`
(5.2.3, KA 11.2.6). A wrongly adds the two figures; B ignores what has already been invoiced; C mistakes the
invoiced value for the accrual.

**MCQ 5.2.4 [topic 5.2.3, level: analysis]**
Hired plant is used on site for the final three days of April, but the supplier's invoice, covering that
usage, is not received until 5 May. Under the matching concept, in which period should the cost be
recognised?
A. May, when the invoice is received
B. **April, accrued at the April cost cut-off** ✔
C. Split evenly between April and May
D. Neither month — recognise it only when payment is actually made
*Rationale:* The matching concept recognises cost in the period the work/usage occurred, not the period the
invoice happens to arrive in (KA 1.3; 5.2.3's plant-hire mini-case). A and D both defer recognition to a
later, irrelevant event; C invents a split with no basis in when the usage actually occurred.

**MCQ 5.2.5 [topic 5.2.6, level: application — numerical]**
A cost element has actual cost to date of $34,000 and an open commitment of $9,000, with no uncommitted
future scope expected. What is the bottom-up EAC for this element?
A. $9,000
B. $25,000
C. $34,000
D. **$43,000** ✔
*Rationale:* `Bottom-up ETC = Open commitment + uncommitted scope = 9,000 + 0 = 9,000`; `Bottom-up EAC =
Actual cost + ETC = 34,000 + 9,000 = 43,000` (5.2.6). A states only the ETC; B subtracts instead of adding;
C ignores the open commitment entirely.

**MCQ 5.2.6 [topic 5.2.7, level: analysis]**
A control account reports a Value of Work Done (VOWD) of $410,000 and an accrual-adjusted cost incurred of
$395,000. What does this most directly indicate?
A. The project is behind schedule
B. **The value of work performed exceeds what it has cost to perform it — a favourable cost position,
analogous to a positive Cost Variance** ✔
C. $15,000 of committed cost remains unspent
D. The forecast at completion has automatically improved by $15,000
*Rationale:* A positive VOWD-minus-cost-incurred gap parallels EV minus AC (a favourable CV, KA 6.2.1) — more
value delivered than it has cost to deliver (5.2.7). A confuses a cost comparison with a schedule signal; C
describes commitment, not VOWD or cost incurred; D wrongly assumes a single period's favourable gap
automatically continues into the forecast, which requires its own separate analysis (KA 6.3).

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Explain why a signed purchase order, on its own, is not yet an "actual cost," and state what would be
   double-counted if it were treated as one the moment it was issued.
2. A different purchase order has a committed value of $36,000. Cumulative GRN-verified receipts to date are
   $22,000, and $14,000 has been invoiced and booked. Compute the accrual (GRNI) required, the accrual-
   adjusted actual cost, and the open commitment.
3. Using the plant-hire cut-off mini-case in 5.2.3, explain why a cut-off error does not change the total
   cost of the project across two periods combined, but does distort the reported performance of a single
   period.
4. In the Al-Noor worked example (5.2.9), the cost element's EAC moved from $50,000 at Month 3 to $51,000 at
   Month 5. Explain why this movement should not, on its own, be read as a sign of poor cost control.

**Answers**

1. A purchase order creates a forward financial exposure — a commitment — but nothing has yet been received
   or consumed, so no cost has genuinely been incurred yet (5.2.1). Treating it as an actual cost immediately
   would recognise an expense before any goods or services exist to justify it, and would then double-count
   the same cost a second time once the genuine GRN-based accrual and, later, the invoice were recognised in
   the normal way.
2. `Accrual (GRNI) = Cumulative GRN value − Cumulative invoice booked = 22,000 − 14,000 = 8,000`.
   `Accrual-adjusted actual cost = Invoiced 14,000 + Accrued 8,000 = 22,000` (equal to cumulative GRN value,
   as expected, since nothing has been received beyond what is GRN-verified and no invoice-price variance is
   indicated). `Open commitment = Committed value − Cumulative GRN value = 36,000 − 22,000 = 14,000`.
3. The cost cut-off only decides *which period* a genuinely incurred cost is reported in, not whether the
   cost exists at all; the matching concept requires it in the period the work or usage actually occurred.
   Recognising it a period late does not remove the cost from existence — it simply moves it into the
   following period, understating the true period's actual cost (and its cost-performance comparison, KA
   6.2.1) and overstating the next period's, even though the combined two-period total is identical either
   way (5.2.3).
4. The entire $1,000 movement is fully explained by a single, approved scope variation (20 m of additional
   tie-in welding at the same $50/m unit rate) instructed by the client's engineer — not by a price increase
   or a productivity shortfall on the originally committed scope. The cost element in fact closed at exactly
   its current, revised commitment ($51,000 actual against $51,000 revised commitment), a zero variance
   against the current authorised scope. The movement reflects legitimate, traceable change control (cross-
   reference KA 5.4), not a cost-control failure (5.2.9).
