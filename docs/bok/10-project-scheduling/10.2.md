# Domain 10 — Project Scheduling

## KA 10.2 — Network analysis & the Critical Path Method

KA 10.1 covers how a schedule's logic is built — defining activities, sequencing them with dependency types
(Finish-to-Start, Start-to-Start, Finish-to-Finish, Start-to-Finish), applying leads and lags, and estimating
durations. This KA assumes that logic network already exists and answers the question that follows
immediately from it: *given the network, how long will the project take, which activities cannot slip
without delaying it, and by how much can the rest slip safely?* The answer is the **Critical Path Method
(CPM)** — a network-analysis technique named and described in both PMI's *PMBOK Guide* (as the primary
schedule network analysis technique) and AACE International's Total Cost Management (TCM) Framework (as a
core planning and scheduling practice). CPM is arithmetic, not judgement: given activity durations and
logic, it mechanically produces early dates, late dates, float and the critical path. What it does *not* do
is validate that the logic or the duration estimates are realistic — that remains a professional
responsibility, covered throughout this KA's pitfalls and its "AI in this topic" notes.

This KA continues the **Pipeline Segment 4B** project used throughout Domains 3 and 6, now viewed at the
schedule (activity-network) level rather than the cost (work-package) level. Domain 6 aggregated this same
scope of work into five monthly-measured cost work packages (WP1–WP5, BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at an
illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**); a schedule network is built and analysed at a finer activity grain and
typically in weeks or days, not months. The two views therefore do not share a single common time axis or
activity list — a normal, expected feature of real projects, where the cost baseline and the detailed
schedule are usually maintained at different levels of the WBS (cross-reference KA 3.3, KA 5.3). The reader
will nonetheless recognise several activities below from Domain 6's control account: design & engineering
(≈ WP1), pipe & valve procurement (≈ WP2, split here into fabrication and delivery), trenching (≈ WP3) and
pipe-laying & welding (= WP4). Two activities are added that Domain 6's cost view folded into adjacent work
packages (regulatory approval; testing & commissioning), and WP5 (site supervision) does not appear as a
network activity at all, because it is measured by Level of Effort (KA 6.1.4) and spans the whole project
duration — a **hammock activity** with no logic of its own, not a discrete, sequenced piece of work, and so
it plays no part in a CPM calculation (KA 10.1 treats activity types in full).

### 10.2.1 The activity network: nodes, logic and the precedence diagramming method

**Definition & purpose.** A **network diagram** represents a project's activities and their logical
dependencies graphically, so that the sequence and interdependency of work — not just a list of durations —
can be seen and analysed. The near-universal modern convention, used by virtually all scheduling software
(Primavera P6, Microsoft Project) and described in the PMBOK Guide, is **Activity-on-Node (AON)**, also
called the **Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM)**: each activity is drawn as a box (**node**) carrying its
data (ID, description, duration and, once calculated, its dates and float), and arrows between boxes
represent the logical dependency, not a unit of work.

**The underlying principle.** A network diagram only has value if it is a genuine, complete statement of
*how the work must be sequenced* — every activity's node should have every predecessor it truly depends on,
no more and no fewer. Missing logic understates the schedule's real duration and float; unnecessary,
overly-conservative logic (a "logic-tied" schedule with dependencies that do not reflect a genuine physical
or resource constraint) overstates it and produces a false critical path. CPM's arithmetic (10.2.2–10.2.5)
is only as trustworthy as the logic feeding it.

An older convention, **Activity-on-Arrow (AOA)**, also called the **Arrow Diagramming Method (ADM)**, drew
the activity itself along the arrow and used nodes only as events (start/finish points), sometimes requiring
"dummy" zero-duration arrows purely to preserve correct logic. AOA is now rarely used in practice but is
still occasionally encountered in legacy schedules or examined at awareness level; every technique in this
KA is presented in the AON/PDM form used throughout the rest of this book.

**The running network — Pipeline Segment 4B construction-phase schedule.** Eight activities, all related by
Finish-to-Start (FS) logic (KA 10.1 covers the other three relationship types), with durations in working
weeks:

| ID | Activity | Duration (weeks) | Predecessor(s) | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Design & route engineering | 4 | — | Start activity |
| B | Regulatory approval & way-leave | 3 | A | FS |
| C1 | Pipe & valve fabrication | 3 | A | FS |
| C2 | Pipe & valve transport & delivery | 3 | C1 | FS |
| D | Trenching & earthworks | 5 | B | FS |
| E | Pipe-laying & welding | 4 | C2, D | FS (both) |
| F | Testing & commissioning | 2 | E | FS |
| G | Site reinstatement & handover | 1 | F | FS |

Read as prose: design must finish before either the regulatory approval or the pipe fabrication can start;
trenching cannot start until the way-leave is approved; pipe-laying cannot start until *both* the pipe has
been delivered *and* the trench is ready — the network's only **merge point** with two predecessors; testing
follows welding, and handover follows testing. This is a valid, complete, non-cyclical (a **directed acyclic
graph**) statement of Pipeline Segment 4B's construction logic and is used unchanged through the rest of this
KA.

**Fig 10.2.1 — Pipeline Segment 4B: activity-on-node logic diagram.**
Diagram type: AON network diagram, left-to-right flow. Eight rectangular nodes, one per activity in the table

**Common pitfalls.**
- Confusing the *arrow* with a unit of work in AON diagrams — the arrow shows dependency only; the box (node)
  carries the duration and data.
- Building a network with **dangling** logic — an activity with no predecessor that should have one, or no
  successor that should have one — which silently distorts every downstream calculation without producing an
  error message in most scheduling software.
- Treating a schedule with a single start and single finish activity as mandatory in principle; it is good
  practice (and assumed throughout this KA) but real schedules sometimes have multiple valid start or finish
  points, which complicates — but does not invalidate — the same underlying arithmetic.

**AI in this topic.** Schedule-authoring assistants embedded in modern PM/scheduling suites can propose a
first-draft logic network from an activity list and a natural-language description of sequencing rules, and
can automatically flag dangling logic, missing predecessors or circular references (cross-reference KA 13.4,
KA 13.5) — a genuine time saving over building a first draft manually. They cannot verify that the proposed
logic reflects the *actual* physical and resource constraints of the real project (e.g. that trenching truly
cannot start before way-leave approval, as opposed to merely "usually" not starting before it) — that
judgement remains with the scheduler, who must review and, where necessary, correct any AI-proposed logic
before it is baselined. **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 10.1 (activity definition, dependency types FS/SS/FF/SF, leads and lags, duration
estimating); KA 6.1.4 (Level of Effort and why WP5 does not appear here); KA 3.3, 5.3 (WBS and cost-account
level vs schedule level); KA 10.3 (what happens to this network under compression).

### 10.2.2 The forward pass: Early Start (ES) and Early Finish (EF)

**Definition & purpose.** The **forward pass** calculates, for every activity, the earliest point in the
schedule at which it could start (**Early Start, ES**) and finish (**Early Finish, EF**), working through
the network from the start activity to the finish activity, respecting all logic. It answers: "as early as
the network's logic allows, when can each activity begin and end?"

**The underlying principle.** An activity cannot start before *every one* of its predecessors has finished
(under simple FS logic, as used throughout this network). Where an activity has more than one predecessor —
Pipeline Segment 4B's merge point at E — it is governed by whichever predecessor finishes *latest*; the
earliest an activity with two predecessors can genuinely start is the later of the two early finishes, never
the earlier one.

**Formula(e).**
`EF_i = ES_i + Duration_i`
`ES_i = max(EF_j) for all predecessors j of activity i` (or `ES = 0` for a start activity with no
predecessor), where all durations, ES and EF are expressed in the same time unit (here, working weeks,
counted from a project time-origin of week 0; KA 10.2.7 converts these offsets into calendar dates).

**Example 10.2.2 — Forward pass for the Pipeline Segment 4B network.**

*Scenario.* The eight-activity network and durations from 10.2.1, project start at week 0.

*Required.* ES and EF for every activity.

*Working.* Proceeding in logic order (an activity cannot be calculated until all its predecessors are):

`ES_A = 0` (start activity) → `EF_A = 0 + 4 = 4`
`ES_B = EF_A = 4` → `EF_B = 4 + 3 = 7`
`ES_C1 = EF_A = 4` → `EF_C1 = 4 + 3 = 7`
`ES_C2 = EF_C1 = 7` → `EF_C2 = 7 + 3 = 10`
`ES_D = EF_B = 7` → `EF_D = 7 + 5 = 12`
`ES_E = max(EF_C2, EF_D) = max(10, 12) = 12` → `EF_E = 12 + 4 = 16`
`ES_F = EF_E = 16` → `EF_F = 16 + 2 = 18`
`ES_G = EF_F = 18` → `EF_G = 18 + 1 = 19`

*Answer.*

| ID | Duration (wk) | ES | EF |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4 | 0 | 4 |
| B | 3 | 4 | 7 |
| C1 | 3 | 4 | 7 |
| C2 | 3 | 7 | 10 |
| D | 5 | 7 | 12 |
| E | 4 | 12 | 16 |
| F | 2 | 16 | 18 |
| G | 1 | 18 | 19 |

*Interpretation.* The earliest the whole project can finish is **week 19** (`EF_G`) — this is the network's
**calculated early finish**, the shortest possible duration given the logic and durations as estimated. Note
how E's ES (12) is governed by D (EF 12), not by C2 (EF 10): C2's branch reaches the merge point two weeks
"early," a fact that becomes central to float in 10.2.4 and 10.2.5.

**Fig 10.2.2 — Forward-pass network: ES/EF annotated.**
Diagram type: the same AON layout as Fig 10.2.1, each node now showing a standard four-cell data box —
top-left ES, top-right EF, activity ID and duration in the centre, bottom row reserved (populated in Fig
10.2.3). Values as in the table above. Brand blue `#1D4ED8` node borders; the merge arrow into E from D is
drawn as a solid, heavier line than the arrow from C2, with a small annotation "governs ES_E = 12" to
visually flag which predecessor drives the merge.

*Underlying data:* the forward-pass table above.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Taking the *first-calculated* predecessor's EF at a merge point rather than the *maximum* of all
  predecessors' EF — a frequent manual-calculation error that understates ES at every downstream activity.
- Forgetting that ES/EF are offsets from the network's time-origin (week 0), not calendar dates — converting
  them to real dates requires the calendar treatment of 10.2.7, not a direct read of the numbers.
- Calculating an activity before all of its predecessors have been calculated, especially in a large network
  processed out of strict logic order — this is why scheduling software processes the network algorithmically
  rather than by eye once it grows beyond a handful of activities.

**AI in this topic.** For a network of Pipeline Segment 4B's size, the forward pass is simple enough to
verify by hand, but production schedules routinely contain hundreds or thousands of activities; scheduling
software performs the forward pass instantaneously and exactly, and AI-assisted logic-checking tools can
additionally scan for the dangling-logic and merge-point errors noted above before the pass is even run
(cross-reference KA 13.5). The tool's arithmetic is not in question; what still requires the professional is
confirming that the *durations themselves* are realistic estimates (KA 10.1) — a perfectly executed forward
pass over an unrealistic duration estimate simply produces a precise, wrong date.

**Cross-references.** KA 10.1 (duration estimating); KA 10.2.3 (the mirror-image backward pass); KA 10.2.7
(converting ES/EF offsets to calendar dates); KA 10.3 (compressing durations on this same network).

### 10.2.3 The backward pass: Late Start (LS) and Late Finish (LF)

**Definition & purpose.** The **backward pass** calculates, for every activity, the *latest* point at which
it could start (**Late Start, LS**) and finish (**Late Finish, LF**) without delaying the project's overall
finish date, working through the network from the finish activity back to the start activity. It answers:
"how late can each activity run before it starts eating into the project's finish date?"

**The underlying principle.** The backward pass is the forward pass performed in reverse, using the *minimum*
rather than the *maximum* at any point where an activity has more than one successor: an activity cannot
finish any later than the earliest of its successors' late starts, because delaying it past that point would
force at least one successor to start later than its own late-start limit allows.

**Formula(e).**
`LS_i = LF_i − Duration_i`
`LF_i = min(LS_k) for all successors k of activity i` (or `LF` = the project's target finish date for a
finish activity with no successor — in the unconstrained base case below, this target is simply the
calculated early finish of the whole network, `EF_G = 19`, from 10.2.2).

**Example 10.2.3 — Backward pass for the Pipeline Segment 4B network (base case, unconstrained).**

*Scenario.* Continuing 10.2.2's network. No external contractual date is imposed, so the project's target
finish is set equal to the calculated early finish: `LF_G = EF_G = 19`.

*Required.* LS and LF for every activity.

*Working.* Proceeding in reverse logic order:

`LF_G = 19` → `LS_G = 19 − 1 = 18`
`LF_F = LS_G = 18` → `LS_F = 18 − 2 = 16`
`LF_E = LS_F = 16` → `LS_E = 16 − 4 = 12`
`LF_D = LS_E = 12` → `LS_D = 12 − 5 = 7`
`LF_C2 = LS_E = 12` → `LS_C2 = 12 − 3 = 9`
`LF_C1 = LS_C2 = 9` → `LS_C1 = 9 − 3 = 6`
`LF_B = LS_D = 7` → `LS_B = 7 − 3 = 4`
`LF_A = min(LS_B, LS_C1) = min(4, 6) = 4` → `LS_A = 4 − 4 = 0`

*Answer.*

| ID | Duration (wk) | LS | LF |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4 | 0 | 4 |
| B | 3 | 4 | 7 |
| C1 | 3 | 6 | 9 |
| C2 | 3 | 9 | 12 |
| D | 5 | 7 | 12 |
| E | 4 | 12 | 16 |
| F | 2 | 16 | 18 |
| G | 1 | 18 | 19 |

*Interpretation.* At the merge point going backward — activity A, feeding both B and C1 — the **minimum** of
the two successors' late starts governs (`LF_A = min(4, 6) = 4`), the mirror image of the forward pass's
maximum rule. Activities A, B and D show identical ES/LS and EF/LF (no gap at all yet — formally confirmed as
zero float in 10.2.4); C1 and C2 show a gap between their early and late dates that 10.2.4 and 10.2.5
quantify precisely.

**Fig 10.2.3 — Backward-pass network: LS/LF annotated.**
Diagram type: the same AON layout, each node's data box now also showing bottom-left LS and bottom-right LF,
completing the standard four-corner box (ES top-left, EF top-right, LS bottom-left, LF bottom-right) used
throughout the rest of this KA. Values as in the table above. The A→C1 arrow (the non-governing branch at the
backward merge into A) is rendered as a lighter, dashed blue line to visually distinguish it from the
governing A→B arrow, mirroring the merge-point convention introduced in Fig 10.2.2.

*Underlying data:* the backward-pass table above.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using the *maximum* of successors' late starts at a backward merge point instead of the minimum — this is
  the single most common manual backward-pass error and produces an LF that is too late, understating float
  problems everywhere upstream of the error.
- Starting the backward pass from an arbitrary or wrong target finish date rather than either the network's
  own calculated early finish (the unconstrained base case above) or a genuine imposed contractual date (the
  constrained case examined in 10.2.4) — the choice materially changes every LS/LF in the network, as shown
  next.
- Assuming LS/LF are fixed facts about the work in the way durations are — they are entirely dependent on the
  *target* finish date chosen, and must be recalculated whenever that target changes.

**AI in this topic.** As with the forward pass, the backward-pass arithmetic itself is a solved, automatable
problem in any scheduling tool, and AI-assisted schedule review can flag backward-pass anomalies (e.g. an LF
earlier than the corresponding EF, which is logically impossible and indicates a data error) faster than a
manual review of a large schedule (cross-reference KA 13.5). It cannot determine what the *correct* target
finish date should be for the backward pass — that is a contractual and commercial decision (KA 7.2), which
the professional must set correctly before the calculation is run.

**Cross-references.** KA 10.2.2 (the forward pass this mirrors); KA 10.2.4 (total float, and the constrained,
negative-float variant of this same backward pass); KA 7.2 (contractual milestones and sectional completion
dates, a common source of an imposed target finish).

### 10.2.4 Total float and identifying the critical path

**Definition & purpose.** **Total Float (TF)**, also called **total slack**, is the amount of time an
activity can slip from its early dates without delaying the project's target finish date. The **critical
path** is the sequence of activities, from the network's start to its finish, with the *least* total float —
in the normal, unconstrained case, exactly zero. It is the longest path through the network, and it directly
determines the project's overall duration: any slippage on a critical-path activity delays the finish date
day-for-day (or week-for-week), while slippage on any other activity, up to its float, does not.

**The underlying principle.** Total float exists wherever an activity's early dates (from the forward pass)
and late dates (from the backward pass) are not identical — that gap is time the activity can "spend" without
consequence to the finish date. Where early and late dates coincide exactly, there is no such cushion: the
activity is critical, in the precise technical sense that any delay to it is a delay to the whole project.

**Formula.**
`TF_i = LS_i − ES_i = LF_i − EF_i`
(both expressions give the same result for any correctly calculated activity — a useful cross-check).

**Example 10.2.4 — Total float and the critical path, Pipeline Segment 4B (base case).**

*Scenario.* Combining the forward-pass table (10.2.2) and backward-pass table (10.2.3).

*Required.* TF for every activity, and the critical path.

*Working.*

| ID | ES | EF | LS | LF | TF = LS−ES | TF = LF−EF |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 4 | 0 | 4 | 0 | 0 |
| B | 4 | 7 | 4 | 7 | 0 | 0 |
| C1 | 4 | 7 | 6 | 9 | 2 | 2 |
| C2 | 7 | 10 | 9 | 12 | 2 | 2 |
| D | 7 | 12 | 7 | 12 | 0 | 0 |
| E | 12 | 16 | 12 | 16 | 0 | 0 |
| F | 16 | 18 | 16 | 18 | 0 | 0 |
| G | 18 | 19 | 18 | 19 | 0 | 0 |

Both formulae agree for every activity (a completed, internally consistent CPM pass). Six activities show
TF = 0; only C1 and C2 show float (2 weeks each).

*Answer.* **The critical path is A → B → D → E → F → G**, duration `4 + 3 + 5 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 19` weeks,
matching the calculated project finish (`EF_G = 19`) exactly. **C1 → C2 is not critical**, carrying 2 weeks
of total float.

*Interpretation.* Any slip on A, B, D, E, F or G — a delayed design deliverable, a slow regulatory approval,
extra time in the trench, a welding delay, and so on — pushes the whole project finish date back by the same
amount, unless recovered elsewhere. A slip of up to 2 weeks on the pipe fabrication/delivery chain (C1–C2)
has, by itself, no effect on the finish date at all. Note, too, that C1–C2 corresponds to Domain 6's
highest-value work package by original budget (WP2, pipe & valve procurement, $150,000 / SAR 562,500 at the
illustrative rate, KA 6.1.4) — a reminder that a work package's *cost* size has no bearing on whether it is
*schedule*-critical; the two are independent dimensions entirely (cross-reference KA 6.2.5's parallel point
about CPI and SPI being independent).

**Mini-case — an imposed finish date and negative float.** Suppose the Pipeline Segment 4B contract requires
mechanical completion (activity G) by the **end of week 17**, two weeks earlier than the network's calculated
19-week duration (i.e. `LF_G = 17` replaces the unconstrained `LF_G = 19` used above). The backward pass is
recalculated with this new target; the forward pass (ES/EF) is unaffected, because it depends only on logic
and durations, never on the target finish.

`LF_G = 17` → `LS_G = 16`; `LF_F = 16` → `LS_F = 14`; `LF_E = 14` → `LS_E = 10`; `LF_D = 10` → `LS_D = 5`;
`LF_C2 = 10` → `LS_C2 = 7`; `LF_C1 = 7` → `LS_C1 = 4`; `LF_B = 5` → `LS_B = 2`;
`LF_A = min(LS_B, LS_C1) = min(2, 4) = 2` → `LS_A = 2 − 4 = −2`.

`TF_A = LS_A − ES_A = −2 − 0 = −2` (and likewise TF = −2 throughout A, B, D, E, F, G); `TF_C1 = 4 − 4 = 0`;
`TF_C2 = 7 − 7 = 0`.

Every activity on the path A–B–D–E–F–G now shows **−2 weeks of total float**: this is **negative float**, and
it means the project, calculated on its current logic and durations, is already forecast to *miss* the
imposed week-17 date by two weeks unless the schedule is compressed (KA 10.3: crashing or fast-tracking) or
the logic is genuinely improved. The critical path under an imposed constraint is formally redefined as the
path with the **least** (most negative, if any is negative) total float — here still A–B–D–E–F–G, now at −2
rather than 0 — while C1–C2 shows 0, meaning that chain, although not on the critical path, is closer to
zero float than the constrained critical path and carries no *additional* risk relative to the imposed date.
Negative float is one of the clearest, most decision-relevant signals in schedule analysis and is a routine
finding in delay-analysis and forensic-schedule work (cross-reference KA 10.4).

Diagram type: the same AON layout as Figs 10.2.1–10.2.3, each node's four-corner data box complete (ES, EF,
LS, LF) plus a fifth value, TF, shown centred beneath the ID. The six critical-path nodes (A, B, D, E, F, G)

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting "the critical path" as a fixed, permanent feature of a project — it is a calculated output of the
  *current* logic, durations and target finish date, and can and does change as the project progresses,
  activities complete, or logic is revised (KA 10.4).
- Assuming total float is "free" to use without consequence — consuming float on a non-critical activity does
  not delay the finish date, but it does consume the buffer that protects the finish date from *future* risk
  on that same chain (formally explored as free float in 10.2.5).
- Reading a *positive* total float everywhere as proof a project is healthy without checking whether the
  amount of float is small relative to remaining duration — a near-critical path (10.2.6) carries real risk
  even without technically being critical.
- Confusing "critical" (zero, or minimum, float — a schedule concept) with "important" or "high-value" in the
  everyday sense — as the C1–C2 example above shows, the highest-budget activity is not necessarily on the
  critical path.

**AI in this topic.** Scheduling software calculates total float and identifies the critical path
automatically and instantly on every recalculation, and can be configured to raise an alert the moment any
activity's float turns negative (cross-reference KA 13.5) — a genuinely valuable, low-effort early-warning
capability, especially on large, frequently-updated schedules. It cannot judge whether an imposed target
finish date is commercially realistic, nor propose a *sound* recovery plan on its own initiative — compression
options (KA 10.3) carry cost, resource and quality trade-offs that require professional and commercial
judgement, not just an algorithm that finds the mathematically shortest path.

**Cross-references.** KA 10.2.2, 10.2.3 (forward and backward pass); KA 10.2.5 (free float); KA 10.2.6
(near-critical and multiple critical paths); KA 10.3 (schedule compression: crashing, fast-tracking); KA 10.4
(schedule control and re-baselining as the critical path evolves); KA 7.2 (contractual milestones as a source
of imposed target dates).

### 10.2.5 Free float — and why it is not the same as total float

**Definition & purpose.** **Free Float (FF)** is the amount of time an activity can slip without delaying
the *early start of any of its immediate successors* — a stricter, more local test than total float, which
only asks whether the *project finish date* is delayed. An activity can have total float but zero free
float: the difference tells a scheduler exactly where a chain's float genuinely sits.

**The underlying principle.** Total float is often shared across a *chain* of consecutive non-critical
activities rather than belonging to any one of them individually. Free float isolates how much of that shared
float can be consumed by a single activity *before it starts eating into the next activity's own early
start* — which matters operationally, because a resource or contractor working on an activity with float
still needs to know whether slipping affects the very next piece of work, not just the distant project finish
date.

**Formula.**
`FF_i = min(ES_k) for all successors k of activity i, minus EF_i` — i.e. `FF_i = min(ES_k) − EF_i`
(for the network's finish activity, with no successor, `FF` is conventionally taken as the project's target
finish date minus its EF, which equals its total float).

**Example 10.2.5 — Free float for the Pipeline Segment 4B network (base case).**

*Scenario.* Continuing the base-case forward-pass table (10.2.2).

*Required.* FF for every activity.

*Working.*
`FF_A = min(ES_B, ES_C1) − EF_A = min(4, 4) − 4 = 0`
`FF_B = ES_D − EF_B = 7 − 7 = 0`
`FF_C1 = ES_C2 − EF_C1 = 7 − 7 = 0`
`FF_C2 = ES_E − EF_C2 = 12 − 10 = 2`
`FF_D = ES_E − EF_D = 12 − 12 = 0`
`FF_E = ES_F − EF_E = 16 − 16 = 0`
`FF_F = ES_G − EF_F = 18 − 18 = 0`
`FF_G = 19 − 19 = 0` (project finish basis, no successor)

*Answer.*

| ID | TF | FF |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 0 |
| B | 0 | 0 |
| C1 | **2** | **0** |
| C2 | **2** | **2** |
| D | 0 | 0 |
| E | 0 | 0 |
| F | 0 | 0 |
| G | 0 | 0 |

*Interpretation.* C1 and C2 both carry 2 weeks of *total* float, but their *free* float diverges sharply:
C2 can slip up to 2 weeks on its own without affecting anything downstream (its only successor, E, is
governed by D, not by C2) — its float is genuinely free. C1, despite also showing 2 weeks of total float, has
**zero** free float: if C1 slips at all, C2's early start slips by the same amount immediately, because C2's
ES is defined as exactly C1's EF. The 2 weeks of float in this chain belongs to the *pair* C1–C2 together, not
to C1 individually — C1 cannot "spend" any of it without passing the consequence straight on to C2.

**Mini-case — float consumption along a chain.** Suppose C1 (pipe & valve fabrication) genuinely runs 1 week
late, finishing at week 8 instead of its planned EF of week 7. C2's ES immediately moves to week 8 (it cannot
start before fabrication is delivered), so C2 now runs week 8 to week 11 instead of week 7 to week 10 — a
1-week slip has been passed straight through, consuming exactly 1 of C2's 2 weeks of free float. C2 now has
only 1 week of float left (`EF_C2 = 11`; `ES_E` is still governed by D at week 12, so `FF_C2 = 12 − 11 = 1`).
The project finish date is still not affected — the chain still has 1 week of float in hand — but a *second*
1-week slip anywhere in the C1–C2 chain would fully exhaust it and put the chain on the critical path
alongside A–B–D–E–F–G. This is exactly why free float, not total float alone, should be tracked
activity-by-activity during execution (KA 10.4): total float tells a scheduler the chain still has room; free
float tells them precisely how a delay at one point in the chain erodes the room left for what follows it.

Chart type: paired bar chart. Horizontal axis: the eight activity IDs. For each, two bars — "Total float"
(grey) and "Free float" (brand blue `#1D4ED8`). Six activities (A, B, D, E, F, G) show both bars at zero. C1
shows a grey bar at 2 with a blue bar at 0 (visually flagged, e.g. a small "△" marker, to draw attention to

*Underlying data:* the TF/FF table above.

**Animation 10.2.5 — "Float consumption along the C1–C2 chain" [digital-only].** Frame 1: C1 and C2 shown in

trailing after each activity's EF). Frame 2: C1's bar visibly stretches by 1 week (a "1-week delay" label
appears), its EF marker sliding from week 7 to week 8. Frame 3: C2's start marker is dragged along

**Common pitfalls.**
- Assuming an activity is free to slip by its full total float — as C1 shows, an activity's *own* free float
  can be zero even while the chain it sits in has genuine float; slipping C1 by even a day immediately moves
  C2's start.
- Reporting float only at the project level (total float against the finish date) during execution, rather
  than tracking free float activity-by-activity — this misses exactly the early-warning signal the mini-case
  above illustrates, where a chain's float is being silently consumed well before it threatens the critical
  path.
- Forgetting that free float, like total float, must be recalculated after every schedule update — it is not
  a fixed property of an activity, only of its position in the network logic and durations at a point in
  time.

**AI in this topic.** A scheduling tool can recompute both total and free float automatically on every status
update and can be configured to flag a chain whose free float has fallen below a defined threshold — a
faster, more consistent version of the manual "is this chain still safe" check performed here (cross-reference
KA 13.5). It cannot decide what threshold is operationally meaningful for a given chain (a threshold of "1
week remaining" may be trivial on a 12-month chain and urgent on a 3-week one), nor diagnose *why* float is
being consumed (poor productivity, a supplier delay, a resourcing conflict) — that diagnosis remains with the
professional.

**Cross-references.** KA 10.2.4 (total float and the critical path); KA 10.4 (progress measurement and
tracking float erosion over the life of the project); KA 10.1 (dependency types — free float calculations
differ slightly under SS/FF/SF logic and lags, covered there).

### 10.2.6 Near-critical paths, multiple critical paths and float thresholds

**Definition & purpose.** A **near-critical path** is a path through the network with total float greater
than zero but small enough, relative to the project's remaining duration or to normal schedule variability,
that it carries real risk of becoming critical. **Multiple critical paths** exist when two or more paths tie
for the least (typically zero) total float simultaneously. Both concepts extend 10.2.4's single-path view of
"the critical path" into a more realistic picture of where a project's genuine schedule risk sits.

**The underlying principle.** A single calculated critical path, viewed in isolation, understates a
project's real fragility, because durations are estimates, not certainties (10.2.8 returns to this point
formally). A path sitting at 1–2 weeks of float on a project with months of remaining duration behaves, for
practical risk purposes, almost like a second critical path: ordinary variability in a handful of its
activities can easily consume that float and make it the *actual* longest path by completion. There is no
single universal numeric threshold for "near-critical" in any named standard; organisations typically define
their own (a common rule of thumb is float below some small absolute number of periods, or below roughly
5–10% of total project duration, applied consistently and stated in the schedule basis document), and it must
be applied consistently, not selectively.

**Example 10.2.6 — Identifying the near-critical path, Pipeline Segment 4B.**

*Scenario.* From 10.2.4/10.2.5's base-case float table: the critical path A–B–D–E–F–G carries 0 weeks of
float over a 19-week project; the C1–C2 chain carries 2 weeks.

*Required.* Assess whether C1–C2 should be flagged as near-critical, and why it matters.

*Working.* 2 weeks of float on a 19-week project is `2 / 19 ≈ 10.5%` of total duration — at or just above a
typical 5–10% near-critical threshold, and, in absolute terms, well within the range that a single
below-average week of fabrication productivity or one short customs delay on delivery could consume (as
10.2.5's mini-case demonstrated concretely, using exactly this chain).

*Answer.* **C1–C2 should be flagged and actively monitored as near-critical**, even though it is not, today,
on the critical path.

*Interpretation.* Reporting only the single critical path (A–B–D–E–F–G) to the project sponsor would be
technically correct but practically incomplete: it hides the fact that a second chain, carrying real
schedule value (Domain 6's highest-cost work package, WP2), sits only two weeks of float away from also
driving the finish date. Professional schedule reporting routinely presents a **near-critical band** — every
path within a stated float threshold of zero — not the single critical path alone.

**Mini-case — schedule compression can create a second critical path.** Suppose Trenching & earthworks (D) is
compressed from 5 weeks to 3 weeks by adding a second excavation crew (a crashing decision, formally costed
in KA 10.3). Recalculating: `EF_D = 7 + 3 = 10`; the merge into E now ties exactly — `ES_E = max(EF_C2, EF_D)
= max(10, 10) = 10` — shortening the whole project to 17 weeks (`EF_G = 17`). Recomputing the backward pass
against this new 17-week target shows **every one of the eight activities at zero total float**: A–B–D–E–F–G
and A–C1–C2–E–F–G are now **both** critical paths simultaneously, tied at exactly 6 weeks each between A and
E (`3 + 3` either way). This is the direct, mechanical consequence of compressing the critical path by exactly
the amount of float the near-critical path was carrying: **the two chains converge**. Any further attempt to
shorten the project now requires compressing *both* paths together — compressing D alone will no longer
shorten the finish date at all, because C1–C2 has become an equally binding constraint. This is precisely why
schedule compression analysis (KA 10.3) must re-run the full CPM after every proposed change, not simply
re-check the path that was critical before the change.

**Fig 10.2.6 — Float by path: base case vs after compressing D.**
Chart type: horizontal bar chart, two panels side by side. Left panel ("Base case"): two bars, "A–B–D–E–F–G"
at 0 weeks float and "A–C1–C2–E–F–G" at 2 weeks float, with a shaded "near-critical zone" band drawn from 0 to
roughly 2 weeks (≈10% of the 19-week duration). Right panel ("After compressing D by 2 weeks"): both bars now
at 0 weeks float, both rendered in bold brand blue `#1D4ED8` with the label "dual critical path." Both panels
share the same horizontal float-weeks scale for direct visual comparison.

*Underlying data:* base case — critical path 0 wk, C1–C2 chain 2 wk (19-week project); compressed case — both
paths 0 wk (17-week project).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting and managing only the single, named critical path while ignoring near-critical paths entirely —
  the most common and most consequential omission in routine schedule status reporting.
- Applying an inconsistent or undocumented near-critical threshold from one reporting period to the next,
  which makes trend comparison meaningless and can look like selective, favourable framing.
- Assuming that compressing the critical path always shortens the project by the full amount compressed — as
  the mini-case shows, once a near-critical path's float is fully consumed, it becomes equally binding, and
  further compression of only the original critical path achieves nothing further.

**AI in this topic.** Scheduling and analytics tools can automatically rank every path in a network by float
and flag the near-critical band on every recalculation — valuable for surfacing paths a human reviewer might
not think to check manually on a large schedule (cross-reference KA 13.5). Predictive models trained on
historical project data may also flag which *type* of near-critical chain (e.g. long-lead procurement, like
C1–C2) has historically been more likely to become critical in similar projects — a genuinely useful
prioritisation signal. Neither replaces the professional judgement needed to decide *what to do* about a
flagged near-critical path (accept the risk, add resource, resequence, or renegotiate scope) — the tool
identifies risk; it does not own the response.

**Cross-references.** KA 10.2.4 (total float, critical path); KA 10.2.5 (free float, and the float-consumption
mechanism that turns near-critical into critical); KA 10.3 (crashing, fast-tracking and their re-analysis
requirement); KA 12.2 (qualitative and quantitative risk analysis applied to schedule risk more broadly).

### 10.2.7 Calendars in network analysis

**Definition & purpose.** A **project calendar** defines which days are available for work — typically
excluding weekends and public holidays — and converts an activity's duration, expressed in working periods
(as used throughout this KA so far), into actual calendar dates. Real schedules commonly use **multiple
calendars**: office-based activities might follow a standard 5-day working week, while site-based activities
might follow a 6-day working week, and different regions observe different public holidays.

**The underlying principle.** CPM's forward and backward pass, as calculated in 10.2.2–10.2.5, work entirely
in *working-period offsets* from a time-origin — they know nothing about weekends or holidays. Converting
those offsets into real calendar dates that a sponsor, contractor or regulator can actually read requires a
second, separate step: mapping each working-period offset onto the specific calendar assigned to that
activity. Two activities with an identical *working-duration* can have very different *calendar spans* if
they sit on different calendars — a common source of confusion between "duration" and "elapsed time."

**Formula/method.** There is no single algebraic formula; the method is: starting from a stated project start
date, count forward the required number of working periods on the applicable calendar (skipping non-working
days), to arrive at each activity's calendar start and finish date. Scheduling software performs this
automatically per activity, per its assigned calendar.

**Example 10.2.7 — Converting the critical path to calendar dates.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's construction-phase schedule begins on **Monday, 5 January 2026**, on a
standard 5-day working week (Monday–Friday), with no public holidays falling within the schedule at this
stage.

*Required.* The calendar finish date for the critical path (19 working weeks, `EF_G = 19` from 10.2.2).

*Working.* Nineteen working weeks of 5 days each, with weekends off throughout and no holidays, span exactly
19 complete Monday-to-Friday work weeks. Starting on Monday, 5 January 2026, the 19th work week's Friday falls
130 calendar days after the start date.

*Answer.* **The calculated calendar finish is Friday, 15 May 2026.**

*Interpretation.* Nineteen *working* weeks correspond to roughly 18.6 *calendar* weeks once weekends are
excluded from the working-period count — the two figures are not the same measure, and reporting "19 weeks"
without stating whether it means working or calendar weeks is a common source of ambiguity in schedule
communication.

**Mini-case — a single public holiday and its calendar impact.** Suppose a national public holiday falls on
what would otherwise be a working Monday, roughly midway through the schedule (week 10). That single lost
working day pushes every subsequent working-day offset one place later on the calendar. Because the
schedule's calculated finish previously landed on a Friday, the loss of one working day pushes the finish to
the very next available working day — skipping the intervening weekend — moving it from **Friday, 15 May
2026 to Monday, 18 May 2026**: a **three-calendar-day** slip caused by a **one-working-day** loss. This is a
routine, easily-overlooked effect: losing a working day just before a weekend or holiday period costs more
calendar time than the same loss mid-week, purely because of where it falls on the calendar.

A related, awareness-level complexity: had Trenching & earthworks (D) been assigned a **6-day site working
calendar** (Monday–Saturday, common for site-based civil works) while the office-based design and procurement
activities remained on the standard 5-day calendar, D's 5-*working*-week duration would span noticeably fewer
*calendar* days than an activity of the same working-duration on the 5-day calendar — illustrating why mixed
calendars, while realistic and routinely used in Primavera P6 and Microsoft Project, must be assigned
deliberately and reviewed, since two "5-week" activities on different calendars are not, in calendar terms,
equally long.

**Fig 10.2.7 — Critical path activities mapped to calendar dates.**
Chart type: Gantt-style bar chart. Vertical axis: activities A, B, D, E, F, G (the critical path) in sequence;
horizontal axis: calendar dates from 5 January 2026 to 18 May 2026. Each activity is drawn as a horizontal bar

**Common pitfalls.**
- Quoting "duration" and "elapsed calendar time" interchangeably — as the base-case example shows, they are
  numerically different even before any holiday is considered.
- Applying a single project-wide calendar to every activity when site and office work genuinely follow
  different working patterns, understating (or overstating) real calendar durations for the affected
  activities.
- Forgetting to update the calendar (and therefore every downstream calendar date) when a new public holiday
  is gazetted or a working pattern changes mid-project — the CPM offsets do not change, but every calendar
  date derived from them does.

**AI in this topic.** Scheduling software applies calendar conversion automatically and can incorporate
region-specific public-holiday calendars from maintained reference data, removing routine manual
date-counting entirely (cross-reference KA 13.5). It cannot know about a *newly announced* or organisation-specific
non-working day until that information is entered, nor decide which calendar is the *correct* one to assign
to a given activity (a site-access or client-imposed working-hours restriction, for example) — that remains a
planning judgement for the scheduler.

**Cross-references.** KA 10.2.2 (forward-pass working-period offsets, the input to this conversion); KA 10.1
(activity and calendar assignment as part of schedule development); KA 10.4 (progress reporting against
calendar-dated baselines).

### 10.2.8 PERT and probabilistic durations (awareness)

**Definition & purpose.** Every duration used in 10.2.1–10.2.7 was a single number — a **deterministic**
estimate. The **Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT)**, described alongside CPM in the PMBOK Guide
as a schedule network analysis technique, instead estimates each activity's duration as a **three-point
range** — optimistic, most likely and pessimistic — and combines them into a single expected duration and a
measure of that duration's uncertainty. This topic treats PERT at **awareness level only**: applying it to one
or two activities to show the mechanics, not re-running the full network probabilistically (full
schedule-risk simulation, using Monte Carlo methods, is covered at awareness level in KA 10.3 and in depth,
for risk more generally, in KA 12.2).

**The underlying principle.** A single-point duration estimate, however carefully made, implies a false
precision: real activities have a range of plausible outcomes, and that range is rarely symmetrical (there is
usually more room for things to run late than to finish early). PERT's three-point estimate captures that
asymmetry directly, and a weighted average — biased toward the most-likely estimate but pulled toward whichever
tail is wider — produces an **expected duration** that is often a more realistic planning figure than the
most-likely estimate alone.

**Formula(e).**
`Te = (O + 4M + P) / 6` — the PERT expected (weighted-average) duration, where O = optimistic duration,
M = most likely duration, P = pessimistic duration (all in the same time unit).
`σ = (P − O) / 6` — the activity's standard deviation, a measure of its duration uncertainty.
`Variance = σ²`.

**Example 10.2.8 — PERT expected duration for Trenching & earthworks (D).**

*Scenario.* Ground conditions for Pipeline Segment 4B's trenching are only partly known in advance. The
planning team estimates: optimistic O = 4 weeks (favourable, easily-excavated ground throughout);
most likely M = 5 weeks (the deterministic estimate used in 10.2.1–10.2.7); pessimistic P = 9 weeks
(unexpected rock or groundwater requiring specialist excavation methods over a significant length).

*Required.* The PERT expected duration and standard deviation for activity D.

*Working.*
`Te = (O + 4M + P) / 6 = (4 + 4×5 + 9) / 6 = (4 + 20 + 9) / 6 = 33 / 6 = 5.5` weeks
`σ = (P − O) / 6 = (9 − 4) / 6 = 5 / 6 = 0.83` weeks (2 d.p.); `Variance = 0.83² ≈ 0.69` weeks²

*Answer.* **Te = 5.5 weeks; σ ≈ 0.83 weeks.**

*Interpretation.* The PERT-weighted expected duration (5.5 weeks) is materially *higher* than the
deterministic most-likely estimate used throughout this KA's main worked network (5 weeks), precisely because
the pessimistic tail (9 weeks, a 4-week spread above most-likely) is wider than the optimistic tail (1 week
below most-likely) — an asymmetric risk that a single most-likely estimate hides entirely. This does not mean
the deterministic CPM calculated earlier in this KA is "wrong"; it means it represents one specific scenario
(the most-likely case) rather than the probability-weighted expectation, and a controls professional should
understand which one any quoted schedule duration actually represents.

**Mini-case — not every critical-path activity carries equal risk.** Applying the same method to Testing &
commissioning (F): optimistic O = 1.5 weeks, most likely M = 2 weeks, pessimistic P = 3 weeks.
`Te = (1.5 + 4×2 + 3) / 6 = 12.5 / 6 ≈ 2.08` weeks; `σ = (3 − 1.5) / 6 = 0.25` weeks.

Both D and F sit on the critical path, but D's uncertainty (σ ≈ 0.83 weeks) is more than three times F's
(σ = 0.25 weeks), and D's expected duration diverges from its deterministic estimate far more (5.5 vs 5 weeks,
a 0.5-week gap) than F's does (2.08 vs 2 weeks, a 0.08-week gap). A project team with limited risk-analysis
time should prioritise D, not spread attention evenly across every critical-path activity — precisely the kind
of prioritisation that quantitative schedule-risk analysis (Monte Carlo simulation across the whole network,
awareness-level in KA 10.3, in full in KA 12.2) is designed to support at scale, activity by activity, rather
than the two-activity comparison shown here.

**Fig 10.2.8 — Three-point estimate and expected duration: Trenching (D) vs Testing & commissioning (F).**
Chart type: two overlaid distribution curves (approximating the beta distribution PERT is based on) on a
shared horizontal "duration (weeks)" axis. D's curve is wide and visibly right-skewed, with O, M, P and Te
marked (4, 5, 9, 5.5); F's curve is narrow and nearly symmetrical, with its own O, M, P and Te marked (1.5, 2,

*Underlying data:* D — O 4, M 5, P 9, Te 5.5, σ 0.83. F — O 1.5, M 2, P 3, Te 2.08, σ 0.25 (all weeks).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Substituting Te for the most-likely duration in a CPM network without recalculating the whole forward and
  backward pass — Te values do not simply "slot in" to a schedule already baselined on most-likely durations
  without re-running 10.2.2–10.2.5 in full.
- Treating PERT's Te as a guaranteed or "safe" duration — it is still a single expected value, not a
  worst-case or a guarantee; genuine confidence-level statements (e.g. "80% likely to finish by...") require
  simulation across the whole network (KA 10.3, KA 12.2), not a single activity's three-point estimate.
- Applying PERT mechanically to every activity regardless of how genuinely uncertain it is — as the D-vs-F
  comparison shows, three-point estimation adds most value where duration uncertainty is real and material,
  not as a blanket exercise.

**AI in this topic.** Historical-data models can suggest plausible optimistic/most-likely/pessimistic ranges
for an activity by pattern-matching against similar past work (e.g. trenching through comparable ground
conditions on prior projects), and simulation tools can run PERT/Monte Carlo analysis across an entire network
far faster than any manual calculation (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). Neither can verify that the
suggested ranges genuinely reflect the specific site, contractor and conditions of *this* project rather than
a generic historical average — an AI-suggested three-point estimate is a starting point for the estimator's
judgement, not a substitute for it, consistent with the estimating principles of KA 3.2.

**Cross-references.** KA 10.2.1–10.2.5 (the deterministic CPM this topic extends); KA 10.3 (schedule
compression and awareness-level Monte Carlo simulation); KA 12.2 (quantitative risk analysis, expected value
and Monte Carlo in full depth); KA 3.2 (estimate classes and ranges, the cost-estimating parallel to
three-point duration estimating).

### Key terms

- **Activity-on-Node (AON) / Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM)** — the modern network-diagramming
  convention in which each node is an activity and arrows represent logical dependency only.
- **Forward pass** — the network calculation, start to finish, that produces Early Start (ES) and Early
  Finish (EF) for every activity: `EF = ES + Duration`; `ES = max(EF of predecessors)`.
- **Backward pass** — the network calculation, finish to start, that produces Late Start (LS) and Late Finish
  (LF) for every activity: `LS = LF − Duration`; `LF = min(LS of successors)`.
- **Total Float (TF)** — the time an activity can slip without delaying the project's target finish date:
  `TF = LS − ES = LF − EF`.
- **Free Float (FF)** — the time an activity can slip without delaying the early start of its immediate
  successor(s): `FF = min(ES of successors) − EF`.
- **Critical path** — the path through the network with the least (normally zero) total float; the longest
  path from start to finish, and the direct determinant of project duration.
- **Near-critical path** — a path with float low enough, relative to project duration or normal variability,
  to carry real risk of becoming critical.
- **Negative float** — total float below zero, arising when a target finish date is imposed earlier than the
  network's calculated early finish; indicates the current plan is already forecast to miss that date.
- **Project calendar** — the definition of working vs non-working days used to convert working-period offsets
  (ES/EF/LS/LF) into calendar dates.
- **PERT three-point estimate** — optimistic (O), most likely (M) and pessimistic (P) duration estimates
  combined into an expected duration `Te = (O + 4M + P) / 6` and standard deviation `σ = (P − O) / 6`.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 10.2.1 [topic 10.2.1, level: recall]**
In the Activity-on-Node (AON) convention used by modern scheduling software, what does an arrow between two
activity boxes represent?
A. A unit of work with its own duration
B. **A logical dependency between the two activities** ✔
C. The activity's total float
D. A resource assignment
*Rationale:* In AON/PDM, the node carries the activity's data (including duration); the arrow shows only the
logical relationship between activities. A confuses AON with the legacy Activity-on-Arrow convention; C and D
are unrelated node/arrow properties.

**MCQ 10.2.2 [topic 10.2.2, level: application — numerical]**
An activity has ES = 9 weeks and a duration of 6 weeks. What is its EF?
A. 3 weeks
B. **15 weeks** ✔
C. 54 weeks
D. 6 weeks
*Rationale:* `EF = ES + Duration = 9 + 6 = 15`. A incorrectly subtracts; C multiplies instead of adding; D
restates the duration alone, ignoring ES.

**MCQ 10.2.3 [topic 10.2.4, level: application — numerical]**
An activity has ES = 4, EF = 7, LS = 6, LF = 9 (all in weeks). What is its total float?
A. 0 weeks
B. **2 weeks** ✔
C. 3 weeks
D. 9 weeks
*Rationale:* `TF = LS − ES = 6 − 4 = 2` (cross-checked: `LF − EF = 9 − 7 = 2`, the same answer by the
alternative formula). A wrongly assumes the activity is critical; C mixes LS and EF; D restates LF alone.

**MCQ 10.2.4 [topic 10.2.4, level: analysis]**
A project's contract imposes a finish date earlier than the schedule's calculated early finish. After
recalculating the backward pass against the imposed date, several activities show total float of −2 weeks.
What does this indicate?
A. A data-entry error that must be corrected before the schedule can be used
B. **The current plan, as logically sequenced, is forecast to miss the imposed date by 2 weeks unless the
   schedule is compressed or the logic is improved** ✔
C. Those activities have 2 weeks of safe buffer against the imposed date
D. The critical path has moved entirely onto a different, unrelated set of activities
*Rationale:* Negative float is a valid, meaningful CPM result (not an error) showing the plan currently misses
an imposed target by that amount (Example/Mini-case, 10.2.4). C reverses the meaning of negative float; D is
unsupported — the path showing negative float remains the (now more urgently) critical one.

**MCQ 10.2.5 [topic 10.2.5, level: analysis]**
Two consecutive, non-critical activities, X then Y (X immediately precedes Y, with no other successor of X),
both show 3 weeks of total float. X's free float is calculated as 0 weeks. What does this tell a scheduler?
A. X and Y are both, in fact, on the critical path
B. The 3 weeks of float belongs to the X–Y chain as a whole; if X slips at all, Y's early start slips by the
   **same amount immediately, even though the project finish date is not yet affected** ✔
C. There has been a calculation error, because total float and free float must always be equal
D. Y no longer has any float remaining
*Rationale:* This is the classic TF/FF divergence (10.2.5): X's total float is shared with Y down the chain,
so X's own free float can be zero even though the pair still has genuine (project-level) float. A is
incorrect — 3 weeks of float means neither is critical; C is false, TF and FF frequently differ; D is
unsupported by the data given (Y's own float has not been stated to be exhausted).

**MCQ 10.2.6 [topic 10.2.8, level: application — numerical]**
An activity's three-point duration estimate is: optimistic O = 3 weeks, most likely M = 4 weeks, pessimistic
P = 11 weeks. What is the PERT expected duration, Te?
A. 4.0 weeks
B. **5.0 weeks** ✔
C. 6.0 weeks
D. 7.0 weeks
*Rationale:* `Te = (O + 4M + P) / 6 = (3 + 4×4 + 11) / 6 = (3 + 16 + 11) / 6 = 30 / 6 = 5.0` weeks. A wrongly
reports the most-likely estimate (M) alone, ignoring the optimistic/pessimistic weighting entirely; C is the
simple (unweighted) average of O, M and P — `(3 + 4 + 11) / 3 = 6.0` — which omits PERT's 4x weighting on M;
D is the midpoint of the optimistic and pessimistic estimates alone — `(3 + 11) / 2 = 7.0` — which ignores M
completely.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. A network has activity Y with predecessors W (EF = 14 weeks) and X (EF = 17 weeks). What is Y's Early
   Start, and which predecessor governs it?
2. Using the Pipeline Segment 4B base-case float table (10.2.4), explain in one or two sentences why C1–C2 is
   described as "near-critical" even though it is not on the critical path.
3. An activity has LS = 20 weeks, LF = 26 weeks, ES = 15 weeks and EF = 21 weeks. Calculate its total float
   using both formula variants and confirm they agree.
4. Explain, in your own words, why an activity can show positive total float but zero free float, and why
   this matters for a scheduler tracking progress during execution.

**Answers**

1. `ES_Y = max(EF_W, EF_X) = max(14, 17) = 17` weeks, governed by **X** (the predecessor with the later
   finish).
2. C1–C2 carries only 2 weeks of total float on a 19-week project (≈10.5% of total duration) — small enough,
   relative to normal schedule variability (as the 10.2.5 float-consumption mini-case and the 10.2.8 PERT
   treatment of a comparable activity both illustrate), that routine delays could plausibly consume it and
   make that chain the actual longest path to completion, even though it is not critical today.
3. `TF = LS − ES = 20 − 15 = 5` weeks; `TF = LF − EF = 26 − 21 = 5` weeks. Both formulae agree at **5 weeks**
   of total float.
4. Total float measures slack against the *project finish date*; free float measures slack against the
   *immediate successor's early start*. Where an activity sits in a chain of consecutive non-critical
   activities, the chain's float is shared collectively rather than owned by any single activity in it — the
   first activity in the chain can show zero free float (any slip passes straight to the next activity)
   while the chain as a whole still shows total float against the finish date. A scheduler who tracks only
   total float can be surprised to find a "safe" activity's slip has already reduced a downstream activity's
   safety margin to nothing; tracking free float activity-by-activity catches this earlier.
