# Domain 10 — Project Scheduling

## KA 10.1 — Schedule development

A cost baseline (KA 3.3) and an earned-value measurement system (KA 6.1) both depend on something that must
exist before either can be built: an approved project schedule. Schedule development turns an approved scope
statement and Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) (KA 5.3) into a logically sequenced, duration-estimated network
of activities — the schedule baseline against which progress is later measured (KA 10.4) and the time-phased
spine onto which the cost baseline is loaded (KA 3.3). This Knowledge Area follows the sequence set out in
PMI's *PMBOK Guide* Schedule Management knowledge area and AACE International's Total Cost Management (TCM)
Framework planning-and-scheduling process: define activities from the WBS; sequence them with logic
relationships and any necessary leads/lags; estimate their durations; identify milestones; and freeze the
result as the baseline schedule. It closes with an awareness-level introduction to schedule-quality
assessment, in the spirit of the widely used DCMA 14-Point Assessment. This KA continues **Meridian Rail
Contracting LLC** (KA 1.1) and its **Pipeline Segment 4B** package (KA 6.1; BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at
an illustrative **SAR 3.75 = USD 1**) — a pipeline relocation and tie-in package within Meridian's wider
24-month, $10,000,000 rail-signalling contract that must connect into a live rail corridor (KA 1.4, KA 10.3).
The activity network built here — Design & route engineering; Regulatory approval & way-leave; Pipe & valve
fabrication and transport/delivery; Trenching & earthworks; Pipe-laying & welding; Testing & commissioning;
Site reinstatement & handover; plus Site supervision as a Level-of-Effort "hammock" activity — is the exact
network KA 10.2 analyses with the Critical Path Method and KA 10.3 compresses and resources. This KA's job
stops at building that network correctly; computing dates from it is KA 10.2's.

### 10.1.1 Activity definition and decomposition from the WBS

**Definition & purpose.** An **activity** (or **schedule activity**) is the smallest unit of work in a
schedule with a defined duration, sequencing logic and, usually, a resource assignment — distinct from a
**work package**, the lowest level of the WBS (KA 5.3), which defines a deliverable but is not yet a
scheduled task. **Activity definition**, set out in the PMBOK Guide's Schedule Management knowledge area, is
the process of decomposing each WBS work package into the activities needed to produce it.

**The underlying principle.** Decomposition follows the same logic as the WBS itself: each level of breakdown
should satisfy the **100% rule** — child activities must, together, represent all of the parent work
package's scope, no more and no less. A widely used practitioner sizing heuristic, the **8/80 rule**, holds
that a well-formed activity should represent roughly 8 to 80 hours of effort — fine enough to track
meaningfully, coarse enough to stay manageable. Decomposing too coarsely makes progress measurement
meaningless; too finely, it inflates administrative cost without improving control.

**Formula.** No numerical formula attaches to decomposition itself; the 100% rule is checked as: `Σ (child
activity scope/budget) = parent work package scope/budget` (no gap, no overlap).

**Example 10.1.1 — Decomposing WP2 Pipe & valve procurement into activities.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's WP2 (Pipe & valve procurement), first introduced in KA 6.1.4 with a budget
of $150,000 (SAR 562,500), is decomposed by the control-account owner into two activities that will carry
into the schedule network built in 10.1.2–10.1.3 and analysed in KA 10.2: **C1 Pipe & valve fabrication**
and **C2 Pipe & valve transport & delivery**, reflecting that the two are genuinely sequential, separately
trackable operations rather than one undifferentiated procurement task.

| Activity | Scope | Budget ($) |
|---|---|---|
| C1 Pipe & valve fabrication | Manufacture to specification | 90,000 |
| C2 Pipe & valve transport & delivery | Freight, customs, delivery to site | 60,000 |
| **Total** | | **150,000** |

*Required.* Confirm the decomposition satisfies the 100% rule against WP2's budget.

*Working.* `90,000 + 60,000 = 150,000`.

*Answer.* **C1 and C2 sum exactly to WP2's $150,000 budget** — the 100% rule is satisfied.

*Interpretation.* WP2 remains a single, reconcilable control account for cost reporting (KA 5.3, KA 6.1),
but is now specific enough, at the activity level, to sequence and duration-estimate individually — exactly
the split KA 10.2's network uses.

**Mini-case — Meridian Rail Contracting LLC: decomposing a fit-out work package.** Meridian's $10,000,000
rail-signalling contract (KA 1.1, KA 1.4) separately includes a Signal Control Room fit-out work package,
budgeted at an illustrative $400,000. Fit-out work is craft- rather than quantity-driven, so decomposition
follows trade sequence: **Containment & first-fix cabling** ($140,000), **Panel installation** ($160,000),
**Testing & commissioning** ($100,000) — summing to `140,000 + 160,000 + 100,000 = 400,000` ✓. The
decomposition *basis* differs from WP2's rate-driven split, but the 100% rule applies identically either way.

**Fig 10.1.1 — WBS decomposition: Pipeline Segment 4B → WP2 Pipe & valve procurement → two activities.**

**Common pitfalls.**
- Decomposing to the work-package level and stopping there — a work package is a deliverable grouping, not
  yet an activity with a duration and logic that can be sequenced.
- Violating the 100% rule by decomposing on two inconsistent bases at once, so activity budgets or durations
  do not reconcile to the parent.
- Over-decomposing low-value, low-risk work to the same fine grain as high-value, high-risk work, inflating
  schedule maintenance effort without a control benefit.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI tools can draft a candidate activity list from a scope narrative or historical
work-package library (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5), speeding up first-draft decomposition. Such a tool
cannot independently confirm the 100% rule is satisfied for *this* scope, or that proposed boundaries reflect
genuine, separately trackable deliverables — the professional must verify completeness before the list feeds
the network (10.1.2–10.1.3).

**Cross-references.** KA 5.3 (WBS, cost breakdown structure, control accounts); KA 6.1.4 (WP1–WP5
measurement methods, reused throughout this KA); KA 1.1, KA 1.4 (Meridian Rail Contracting LLC); KA 10.1.2
(sequencing the activities defined here); KA 10.2.1 (this same activity list drawn into the CPM network).

### 10.1.2 Sequencing activities: dependency types (FS/SS/FF/SF) and leads/lags

**Definition & purpose.** **Sequencing** is the process of identifying and documenting the logical
relationships (**dependencies**) between activities — which activity must, or should, occur before, with, or
after another. The PMBOK Guide's Schedule Management knowledge area defines four types of **precedence
relationship**, each describing how the start or finish of one activity (the **predecessor**) constrains the
start or finish of another (the **successor**):

- **Finish-to-Start (FS)** — successor cannot start until predecessor finishes; the most common relationship.
- **Start-to-Start (SS)** — successor cannot start until predecessor starts; used where two activities can
  run concurrently once the first is under way.
- **Finish-to-Finish (FF)** — successor cannot finish until predecessor finishes; used where two activities
  must complete together.
- **Start-to-Finish (SF)** — successor cannot finish until predecessor starts; the rarest relationship,
  typically used for shift-handover situations.

A **lag** is a directed delay inserted after a relationship is satisfied (e.g., a curing time, an inspection
hold, a delivery lead time); a **lead** is a negative lag — a deliberate *overlap*.

**The underlying principle.** Real work is rarely a single unbroken FS chain; sequencing exists to capture
the *genuine* physical, contractual or resource constraint driving each dependency, not to be filled in
mechanically. Every relationship and lag chosen here feeds KA 10.2's forward and backward pass — an invented
or unjustified dependency distorts the critical path just as surely as a wrong duration estimate.

**Formula(e).** Using ES/EF (Early Start/Early Finish) offsets from a common project start (t = 0):

`FS + lag: ES(successor) = EF(predecessor) + Lag`
`SS + lag: ES(successor) = ES(predecessor) + Lag`
`FF + lag: EF(successor) = EF(predecessor) + Lag`
`SF + lag: EF(successor) = ES(predecessor) + Lag`

where `EF(activity) = ES(activity) + Duration`. Where an activity has more than one predecessor, the
**governing constraint is the one producing the latest permissible early start** (the max-rule; the full
forward-pass method, applied to the network below, is KA 10.2's job).

**Example 10.1.2 — Computing successor dates under FS and SS relationships.**

*Scenario.* Predecessor Activity X: ES = 0, duration 4 weeks. Successor Y is Start-to-Start with X, lag 1
week; successor Z is Finish-to-Start with X, lag 1 week.

*Working.* `EF(X) = 0 + 4 = 4`; `ES(Y) = ES(X) + 1 = 1` (SS); `ES(Z) = EF(X) + 1 = 5` (FS).

*Answer.* **X finishes week 4; Y can start as early as week 1; Z can start as early as week 5.**

*Interpretation.* The same 1-week lag on the same predecessor produces very different successor timing
depending only on relationship type — Y starts while X is still running, Z only once X plus the lag has
elapsed.

**Example 10.1.2b — the real network: Pipeline Segment 4B's construction-phase activity logic.** Extending
this to the eight activities carried into KA 10.2's Critical Path Method analysis (durations estimated in
10.1.4):

| Activity | Description | Duration (wk) | Predecessor(s) & relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Design & route engineering | 4 | — (project start) |
| B | Regulatory approval & way-leave | 3 | A, FS |
| C1 | Pipe & valve fabrication | 3 | A, FS |
| C2 | Pipe & valve transport & delivery | 3 | C1, FS |
| D | Trenching & earthworks | 5 | B, FS |
| E | Pipe-laying & welding | 4 | C2, FS **and** D, FS |
| F | Testing & commissioning | 2 | E, FS |
| G | Site reinstatement & handover | 1 | F, FS |

Every relationship above is Finish-to-Start with zero lag — a deliberately simple, clean logic set. **WP5
Site supervision** ($50,000, Level of Effort — KA 6.1.4) is *not* one of these discrete activities. It is
modelled instead as a **hammock activity**: Start-to-Start with A (project start, lag 0) and Finish-to-Finish
with G (project finish, lag 0) — exactly the representation needed for support work (supervision, PM, QA/HSE)
that has no discrete logic of its own, and why it plays no part in KA 10.2's CPM calculation.

*Working.* `ES(WP5) = ES(A) + 0 = 0` (SS); `EF(WP5) = EF(G) + 0 = 19` (FF, using KA 10.2.2's dates);
`Duration(WP5) = 19 − 0 = 19 weeks`.

*Answer/Interpretation.* **WP5 spans week 0 to week 19** without ever being assigned a predecessor or
successor among A–G. A hammock activity's "duration" is not estimated the way a discrete activity's is — it
is *derived* from the span of the activities it hangs across, which is why WP5 does not appear as a node in
KA 10.2's precedence diagram.

**Animation 10.1.2 — "Same lag, four different outcomes" [digital-only].** Frame 1: a fixed predecessor bar

**Common pitfalls.**
- Defaulting every relationship to Finish-to-Start, when the true constraint is Start-to-Start or
  Finish-to-Finish — this artificially lengthens the schedule.
- Using a lag to paper over a missing activity rather than adding the activity itself.
- Forcing a genuinely Level-of-Effort activity (like WP5) into the discrete FS chain instead of modelling it
  as a hammock — this wrongly makes supervision "critical" or gives it artificial float.

**AI in this topic.** Scheduling-integrated AI features can propose candidate logic links from a
natural-language scope description or a historical schedule library (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) and
flag unusual combinations. Such tools have no direct knowledge of the real constraint driving a dependency
unless supplied as structured input — the professional must validate every suggested link and confirm which
activities are genuinely hammock/LOE work before the network is accepted.

**Cross-references.** KA 5.3 (WBS providing the activities being sequenced); KA 10.1.1 (activity

### 10.1.3 Assembling the network: the Precedence Diagramming Method, and the hammock activity in full

**Definition & purpose.** The **Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM)**, also called **Activity-on-Node
(AON)**, is the network-diagramming convention used by virtually all modern scheduling software, in which
activities are drawn as **nodes** and the four dependency types (10.1.2) are drawn as directed **arrows**
between them. KA 10.2.1 covers PDM's full diagramming conventions — including the legacy Activity-on-Arrow
method it superseded — and renders this same network as a formal precedence diagram; that treatment is not
repeated here. This topic's job is narrower and precedes it: confirming the assembled network (10.1.2b) is
*complete and well-formed* before it is diagrammed and calculated, and developing the **hammock activity**
treatment KA 10.2 relies on but does not itself build.

**The underlying principle.** A network is only ready to be calculated once every discrete activity has at
least one predecessor (other than the project start) and one successor (other than the project finish) — an
**open end** left by accident corrupts every downstream calculation without necessarily producing a software
error. A hammock activity is a deliberate, *intentional* exception: because it tracks elapsed time or a
related activity's span rather than its own discrete logic (KA 6.1.4's Level of Effort method), it is
properly excluded from the logic network and instead pinned to the network's boundary points.

**Formula.** No new formula; a hammock activity's derived span is `Duration(hammock) = EF(governing finish
point) − ES(governing start point)`, using whichever SS/FF relationships anchor it (Example 10.1.2b).

**Example 10.1.3 — Confirming the network is well-formed before it goes to KA 10.2.**

*Scenario.* The eight-activity logic table (10.1.2b) plus the WP5 hammock.

*Required.* Check every discrete activity has a predecessor and a successor, and confirm the hammock is
correctly anchored rather than left dangling.

*Working.*

| Activity | Has a predecessor? | Has a successor? |
|---|---|---|
| A | — (legitimate start activity) | Yes (B, C1) |
| B | Yes (A) | Yes (D) |
| C1 | Yes (A) | Yes (C2) |
| C2 | Yes (C1) | Yes (E) |
| D | Yes (B) | Yes (E) |
| E | Yes (C2, D) | Yes (F) |
| F | Yes (E) | Yes (G) |
| G | Yes (F) | — (legitimate finish activity) |
| WP5 (hammock) | Anchored by SS to A | Anchored by FF to G |

*Answer.* **Every activity in the network is either logically connected on both sides, or is a legitimate
boundary activity (A at the start, G at the finish), or is a deliberately anchored hammock (WP5).** No
unintended open ends exist.

*Interpretation.* This check must pass *before* KA 10.2 draws the precedence diagram and runs the
forward/backward pass — an accidental open end silently understates true project duration or float at that
point, an error easy to miss visually but caught systematically by a table-based review (developed further
in 10.1.7).

**Mini-case — what would go wrong if WP5 were forced into the discrete chain.** A scheduler unfamiliar with
hammock modelling who instead gives WP5 a Finish-to-Start relationship following G would make the *entire*
$50,000 of supervision appear to occur only after handover — the opposite of reality, since supervision runs
throughout construction — and would tack an unrelated extra duration onto the critical path. The SS/FF
hammock avoids this: WP5's span is *derived* from the network's boundaries, never added to them.

**Animation 10.1.3 — "Where the hammock hangs" [digital-only].** Frame 1: the A–G bars appear in sequence at

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating the PDM diagram as complete once it "looks" connected, without a systematic predecessor/successor
  check like the table above.
- Modelling a hammock's span with a fixed, typed-in duration rather than deriving it from its SS/FF anchors —
  it then silently stops updating when the anchoring activities' dates change.
- Assuming the "governing" predecessor at a merge point is intuitively obvious — KA 10.2.2's forward pass
  shows it is D (EF 12), not the visually "longer-looking" C2 chain (EF 10), that governs E.

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted scheduling features can scan a draft network for orphaned or dangling
activities and flag them automatically before diagramming (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). Such tools do
not reliably distinguish a genuine hammock/LOE activity from one simply missing its real predecessor or
successor by error — both look identical to an automated scan, and the professional must confirm which is
which.

### 10.1.4 Estimating activity durations: analogous, parametric and three-point (PERT) estimating

**Definition & purpose.** **Duration estimating** forecasts how many work periods each activity will take,
given its scope and resources. PMBOK's Schedule Management knowledge area and AACE's TCM Framework both
describe the same family of estimating methods already introduced for cost in KA 3.2, applied here to time:

- **Analogous (top-down) estimating** — scaling a duration from a comparable, completed activity or project,
  adjusted by a size or complexity factor. Fast, but only as reliable as the analogue's genuine comparability.
- **Parametric estimating** — deriving a duration from a measurable quantity and a productivity/output rate.
  More precise where a reliable rate exists and the work is genuinely repetitive or quantifiable.
- **Three-point (PERT) estimating** — combining an **optimistic (O)**, **most likely (M)** and **pessimistic
  (P)** duration into a single expected value and a measure of uncertainty, used where genuine duration risk
  exists.

**The underlying principle.** No estimating method removes uncertainty; each manages it differently.
Analogous estimating trades precision for speed; parametric estimating trades generality for precision once a
reliable rate exists; three-point estimating makes uncertainty explicit rather than hiding it inside a single
figure. KA 10.2.8 develops three-point/PERT estimating further, at network scale, for Trenching and Testing &
commissioning; this topic introduces the technique's mechanics using a different activity so the two
treatments complement rather than repeat one another.

**Formula(e).**
Parametric: `Duration = Quantity / Productivity rate`.
Three-point (PERT, beta-weighted average): `TE = (O + 4M + P) / 6`; standard deviation `σ = (P − O) / 6`.

**Example 10.1.4a — Analogous estimating: Activity A, Design & route engineering.**

*Scenario.* A comparable, recently completed project, "Pipeline Segment 3A," had a 1,500 m route and a
design-phase duration of 3 weeks. Pipeline Segment 4B's route is 2,000 m, with broadly similar complexity.

*Required.* An analogous duration estimate for Activity A, scaled by route length.

*Working.* `Scaling factor = 2,000 / 1,500 = 1.333`; `Estimated duration = 3 weeks × 1.333 = 4 weeks`.

*Answer/Interpretation.* **Activity A is estimated at 4 weeks** — the duration carried into the network
(10.1.2b) and used throughout KA 10.2's CPM analysis. The estimate is only as good as the analogy: it assumes
Segment 4B's complexity per metre is genuinely comparable to Segment 3A's, and a genuine complexity
difference should be adjusted with documented judgement, not left as pure scaling arithmetic.

**Example 10.1.4b — Parametric estimating: Activity D, Trenching & earthworks.**

*Scenario.* Activity D requires 2,000 m of open-cut trench (WP3, KA 6.1.4). A trenching crew, working one
shift a day, five days a week, achieves a sustained productivity rate of 80 m per day for this soil type.

*Required.* The parametric duration estimate, in working days and weeks.

*Working.* `Duration (days) = 2,000 m / 80 m per day = 25 days`; `Duration (weeks) = 25 / 5 = 5 weeks`.

*Answer/Interpretation.* **Activity D is estimated at 25 working days (5 weeks)** — the duration carried into
the network. Parametric estimating is only as reliable as the productivity rate: a rate drawn from
dissimilar ground conditions or an unrepresentative crew silently mis-estimates the duration even though the
arithmetic itself is exact.

**Mini-case — Example 10.1.4c: Three-point (PERT) estimating for Activity E, Pipe-laying & welding.**

*Scenario.* Activity E's deterministic duration, used throughout KA 10.2's CPM network, is 4 weeks. Because
welding on a live-corridor tie-in package carries genuine risk from weather exposure and third-party
inspection hold points, the estimator also gathers a three-point range: **Optimistic (O) = 3 weeks**;
**Most likely (M) = 4 weeks** (matching the deterministic figure); **Pessimistic (P) = 8 weeks** (weather
delays and inspection backlogs).

*Required.* The PERT expected duration and standard deviation for Activity E.

*Working.*
`TE = (O + 4M + P) / 6 = (3 + 4×4 + 8) / 6 = (3 + 16 + 8) / 6 = 27 / 6 = 4.5 weeks`
`σ = (P − O) / 6 = (8 − 3) / 6 = 5 / 6 ≈ 0.83 weeks`

*Answer.* **Expected duration = 4.5 weeks; standard deviation ≈ 0.83 weeks.**

*Interpretation.* The PERT-weighted expected duration (4.5 weeks) exceeds the deterministic most-likely
estimate KA 10.2 uses (4 weeks), because the pessimistic tail (8 weeks) is wider than the optimistic tail (3
weeks) — an asymmetric risk a single most-likely figure hides entirely. This does not make the deterministic
CPM network "wrong": it represents the most-likely scenario, not the probability-weighted expectation. KA
10.2.8 carries the same logic further, for Trenching (D) and Testing & commissioning (F).

**Fig 10.1.4 — PERT (beta) distribution for Activity E, Pipe-laying & welding.**
Chart type: a smoothed, right-skewed density curve over the duration axis (3–8 weeks), brand blue `#1D4ED8`
fill, with vertical reference lines at O (3), M (4), TE (4.5) and P (8), each labelled.
*Underlying data:* O = 3, M = 4, TE = 4.5, P = 8, σ ≈ 0.83 (Example 10.1.4c).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using analogous estimating without checking the analogue is genuinely comparable in scope, ground
  conditions and crew.
- Using a parametric productivity rate from a different context without adjustment.
- Collapsing a three-point estimate to just the "most likely" figure, discarding O and P entirely — this
  loses the uncertainty signal three-point estimating exists to capture.

**AI in this topic.** Historical-data and ML-based estimating tools can propose productivity rates or
analogous scaling factors from a database of past activities (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5), and GenAI
tools can draft plausible O/M/P ranges from a narrative description. None has direct knowledge of *this*
activity's actual site conditions, crew or weather exposure unless supplied as structured input — the
professional must validate any AI-proposed duration or range against local, current conditions before it
enters the schedule — **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 3.2 (the equivalent cost-estimating methods); KA 10.1.2b (durations feeding the
network); KA 10.2.8 (three-point/PERT developed further, for Trenching and Testing & commissioning); KA 10.3
(schedule risk and the same duration ranges reused there); KA 12.2 (quantitative risk analysis, the same O/M/P
logic applied to risk events).

### 10.1.5 Milestones

**Definition & purpose.** A **milestone** is a significant point or event in the schedule — typically the
achievement of a key deliverable, a decision gate, or a contractual date — represented in the network as a
zero-duration activity with no resource or cost of its own. Milestones summarise progress for stakeholders
who do not need full activity-level detail, and, in construction and engineering contracts, are frequently
tied directly to contractual payment or notice obligations (cross-reference KA 7.2, KA 7.4).

**The underlying principle.** Because a milestone has zero duration, it cannot itself carry float except as
inherited from the activities feeding it (KA 10.2.4), and it is not "estimated" — it simply marks the
computed finish (or start) of whatever relationship defines it. Milestones are most useful when few,
unambiguous and verifiable — each should have an objective, observable completion criterion, not a
description different reviewers could interpret differently.

**Formula.** None; a milestone's date is the ES or EF of the network node it represents.

**Example 10.1.5 — The Pipeline Segment 4B project milestone schedule.**

*Scenario.* Using the network of 10.1.2b, with dates as computed by KA 10.2.2's forward pass (cited here, not
re-derived):

| MS-1 Design approved | EF(A) | 4 |
| MS-2 Regulatory approval / way-leave granted | EF(B) | 7 |
| MS-3 Pipe & valves delivered to site | EF(C2) | 10 |
| MS-4 Trenching complete | EF(D) | 12 |
| MS-5 Pipe-laying & welding complete | EF(E) | 16 |
| MS-6 Testing & commissioning complete | EF(F) | 18 |
| MS-7 Mechanical completion / handover | EF(G) | 19 |

*Working.* Each milestone marks exactly one activity's finish, in the same sequence as the network logic
(10.1.2b); MS-5 implicitly depends on E having incorporated both its predecessors (C2 and D) at the merge
point.

*Answer/Interpretation.* **Seven project milestones, from MS-1 (week 4) to MS-7 (week 19)**, each objectively
verifiable (a deliverable issued, an approval granted, a delivery received, an inspection passed). WP5 Site

**Common pitfalls.**
- Defining a milestone with a vague, non-verifiable criterion rather than an objective one — this
  reintroduces exactly the subjectivity milestones are meant to remove.
- Assigning a milestone a duration or a directly attached cost — a milestone is zero-duration by definition;
  associated cost belongs to the activities leading into it, or the weighted-milestone EV method (KA 6.1.4).
- Expecting a hammock/LOE activity to align with a specific milestone — its progress is elapsed-time-based,
  not event-based.

**AI in this topic.** NLP-based contract-analytics tools can extract candidate milestone dates and
descriptions directly from contract text (cross-reference KA 7.2, KA 13.4), speeding up the first draft
considerably. Such extraction cannot confirm the wording matches the *true* completion criterion the parties
intend, or that the milestone is technically achievable given the network it sits within — the professional
must verify each AI-extracted milestone against the contract and the schedule logic.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.1.4 (WP4's weighted-milestone EV method, a related but distinct concept); KA 7.2,
KA 7.4 (contractual milestones, payment applications); KA 10.2.2 (the forward pass that defines these dates);
KA 10.4 (progress measurement against milestones).

### 10.1.6 The baseline schedule

**Definition & purpose.** The **baseline schedule** (or **schedule baseline**) is the approved version of the
project schedule — activities, sequencing, durations and dates — formally frozen as the reference point
against which actual progress is measured (KA 10.4) and from which the cost baseline is time-phased
(KA 3.3). It is the schedule equivalent of the Performance Measurement Baseline (PMB) covered for cost in
KA 3.3.5, and the two should always be built from the same, single, approved network.

**The underlying principle.** A schedule that is never frozen cannot support a variance conversation: "are we
behind?" is meaningless without a fixed "behind *what*?" Once approved, the baseline changes only through
formal change control (cross-reference KA 5.4, KA 8.4) — a re-baseline, fully documented and traceable —
never through quiet, undocumented adjustment. Between re-baselines, the **current schedule** is compared
against the frozen baseline to reveal genuine slippage or gain — a comparison that depends directly on
knowing which activities are critical (zero float) and which carry float (KA 10.2.4).

**Formula.** None new; a baseline record is simply the network of 10.1.2b–10.1.3 (activities, relationships,
durations, dates) captured and version-locked at approval. Where a comparison is made:
`Schedule slip (weeks) = Current forecast finish − Baseline finish`.

**Example 10.1.6 — Why the same one-week delay means two different things.**

*Scenario.* Pipeline Segment 4B's baseline schedule (10.1.5) has the project finishing at week 19. KA 10.2.4
has already established that, in this base-case network, Activity B (Regulatory approval & way-leave) sits on
the critical path with **zero total float**, while Activity C1 (Pipe & valve fabrication) carries **2 weeks
of total float**. Suppose a one-week delay occurs — first considered on B, then, separately, on C1.

*Required.* The effect of each one-week delay on the current forecast finish date, relative to the week-19
baseline.

*Working.* B is critical (TF = 0, KA 10.2.4): any delay to B passes straight through to the finish date, so a
one-week delay to B moves the current forecast finish from week 19 to week 20. C1 carries 2 weeks of total
float (KA 10.2.4, KA 10.2.5): a one-week delay to C1 is fully absorbed within that float and does not, by
itself, change the finish date.

*Answer.* **A one-week delay to B produces a one-week schedule slip against the baseline. The same one-week
delay to C1 produces no slip against the baseline at all** — though it does consume half of C1's float,
leaving only 1 week in reserve (cross-reference KA 10.2.5's free-float treatment of this same chain).

*Interpretation.* A baseline comparison must always be read alongside the schedule's float picture, not
duration alone: the *same-sized* delay can be a genuine, reportable schedule variance or a harmless,
fully-absorbed event, depending entirely on which activity it hits. A re-baseline is warranted only where a
genuinely approved scope or logic change has occurred (KA 5.4, KA 8.4) — not because the current forecast has
drifted through unmanaged slippage, which is a performance issue to report and address, not baseline away.

**Fig 10.1.6 — Baseline finish vs current forecast: the effect of a one-week delay on B versus C1.**
Chart type: two small paired-bar panels sharing a week-0–20 axis. Left panel ("Delay on B, critical"):
baseline finish marker at week 19 (grey), current forecast marker at week 20 (brand blue `#1D4ED8`), gap
bracketed "+1 week." Right panel ("Delay on C1, float-bearing"): both markers at week 19, no gap, with a
small annotation "1 of 2 weeks of float consumed."
*Underlying data:* baseline finish 19 (both panels); revised forecast 20 (B-delay panel) vs 19 (C1-delay
panel); C1's float 2 weeks before, 1 week after (KA 10.2.4, KA 10.2.5).

**Animation 10.1.6 — "Same delay, two outcomes" [digital-only].** Frame 1: the baseline Gantt (Fig 10.1.3),
finish marker at week 19. Frame 2: B's bar stretches by one week; the finish marker slides to week 20, with a
callout "critical path — no float to absorb this." Frame 3: the schedule resets; C1's bar instead stretches
by one week; nothing downstream moves ("float absorbed — 1 week remains"). Narration cue: "The baseline
doesn't move just because something slipped. Whether the finish date moves depends on where the slip
happened."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Re-baselining informally, without a documented, approved change — this destroys the audit trail and makes
  every future variance comparison meaningless (KA 5.4, KA 8.4).
- Baselining a schedule before the network has been checked for logic errors (10.1.3, 10.1.7).
- Reading a schedule slip in isolation from the float picture, as if every delay of the same size carried the
  same consequence.

**AI in this topic.** Schedule-health monitoring tools can automatically compare the current schedule against
the stored baseline on every update and flag changes outside expected tolerances (cross-reference KA 13.5).
Such a tool cannot judge whether a detected change reflects a genuinely approved re-baseline or an
unauthorised, undocumented adjustment — that governance judgement remains the accountable professional's.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.3.5 (the Performance Measurement Baseline for cost, built on the same underlying
schedule); KA 5.4, KA 8.4 (change control); KA 6.2 (formal Schedule Variance, SV, once EVM is applied); KA
10.2.4–10.2.5 (total and free float, the mechanism this baseline comparison depends on); KA 10.4 (ongoing
progress measurement against this baseline).

### 10.1.7 Schedule quality checks: a DCMA-style review (awareness)

**Definition & purpose.** Before a schedule is baselined (10.1.6) and relied upon for reporting and
forecasting, it should be tested for basic **logical soundness** — not whether the dates are "right" in some
absolute sense, but whether the network is free of structural defects that make its calculated dates
untrustworthy. The best-known framework for this kind of check, widely referenced across the project-controls
industry, originated with the U.S. Defense Contract Management Agency and is generally known as the
**DCMA 14-Point Assessment**; it is described here at awareness level, as a set of check *categories* a
professional should recognise and be able to apply in principle — not reproduced as an authoritative
checklist with exact published thresholds, which should always be confirmed against current guidance before
formal use.

**The underlying principle.** A schedule can be internally "correct" arithmetic — every date computed exactly
as its logic dictates (KA 10.2) — and still be untrustworthy if the logic itself is poorly formed: open ends,
an unrealistic proportion of leads or unusually long lags standing in for unmodelled work, hard-coded dates
overriding calculated logic, or activities too long in duration for progress to be observed within a
reporting period. Running structured checks before baselining is cheaper than discovering the defect
mid-project, once a distorted critical path has already misdirected management attention.

**Formula.** None; these are structural/logical checks, typically expressed as counts or percentages against
the total activity or relationship count.

**Example 10.1.7 — Applying a small set of quality checks to the Pipeline Segment 4B network.**

*Scenario.* Using the network assembled in 10.1.2b–10.1.3 (eight discrete activities, eight FS relationships,
plus the WP5 hammock's two SS/FF relationships — ten relationships in total), the schedule owner runs a
logic-health review before baselining.

| Check (in the spirit of DCMA-style categories) | What it looks for | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Logic / open ends | Any discrete activity with no predecessor (other than A, the start) or no successor (other than G, the finish) | None — confirmed in Example 10.1.3's table |
| Leads (negative lags) | Any relationship using a negative lag to force an early start | None used |
| Lags | Any relationship carrying a positive lag | None among A–G (all FS+0); WP5's SS/FF anchors also carry zero lag |
| Relationship mix | Proportion of the network's relationships that are Finish-to-Start | 8 of 10 (80%) — the remaining 20% is WP5's deliberate SS/FF hammock, not a defect |
| Hard constraints | Any activity date fixed by an imposed constraint rather than calculated from logic | None in this base-case network (an imposed target date is examined separately in KA 10.2.4's mini-case) |
| High duration | Any single discrete activity with an unusually long duration relative to the 19-week project | D (5 weeks) is the longest discrete activity — unremarkable; WP5's 19-week span is excluded from this check, as its progress is tracked by elapsed time (LOE), not by discrete deliverable milestones |

*Required.* An overall assessment of whether the network is fit to baseline.

*Answer/Interpretation.* No open ends, no unexplained negative lags, no lags requiring justification, no
hard-coded constraints, and a relationship mix that is predominantly FS with a single, deliberate,
well-documented SS/FF exception (WP5). No findings require correction before baselining. This is what a
schedule-quality review looks like in practice: not a pass/fail gate on arithmetic (scheduling software
already guarantees that is internally consistent), but a professional judgement on whether the *logic*
underlying it is sound enough to trust as a baseline and, later, as the yardstick for progress reporting (KA
10.4) and cost time-phasing (KA 3.3).

**Fig 10.1.7 — Schedule-quality scorecard: Pipeline Segment 4B, pre-baseline review.**
Chart type: horizontal traffic-light scorecard, one row per check category from the table above, coloured
green ("clear") throughout for this network, brand blue `#1D4ED8` used for the header band; the "Relationship
mix" and "High duration" rows carry a small footnote marker linking to the WP5 explanation.
*Underlying data:* the check/result table in Example 10.1.7 (six categories, all green, with two explained
exceptions noted rather than flagged as defects).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating a "clean" mechanical check as proof the schedule is realistic — logic checks catch structural
  defects, not whether the sequencing and durations reflect genuine constructability.
- Running quality checks only once, at initial baselining, rather than repeating them whenever the schedule
  is substantially updated — logic defects can be introduced by any subsequent edit.
- Citing specific numeric DCMA thresholds as fixed, universally mandated pass/fail limits without confirming
  them against current, organisation- or contract-specific guidance — this chapter deliberately treats them
  as illustrative categories, not a verbatim reproduction of any published metric table.

**AI in this topic.** This is a category where automated tools already add clear, practical value: many
scheduling platforms and add-ins can run structured, DCMA-style logic checks automatically on every schedule
update — counting open ends, leads, lags, constraint types and long-duration activities far faster than a
manual review (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). Such a tool cannot judge *constructability* — whether
structurally clean logic represents a realistic, achievable real-world sequence — or distinguish a
deliberate, well-justified exception (like WP5's hammock) from a genuine defect without being told which is
which. A clean automated score is a necessary check, not a sufficient one — **AI proposes; the professional
disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 10.1.2–10.1.3 (the relationships and network this reviews); KA 10.1.6 (baselining,
which this check should precede); KA 10.2 (the critical path, which a logic defect can distort); KA 8.4
(Monitoring & Controlling, where schedule-quality review recurs throughout execution).

### Key terms

- **Activity / schedule activity** — the smallest scheduled unit of work, distinct from a WBS work package.
- **100% rule / 8/80 rule** — decomposition principles: child activities must fully represent the parent's
  scope; a well-formed activity typically spans roughly 8–80 hours of effort.
- **Predecessor / successor** — the activity that constrains, and the activity that is constrained by, a
  precedence relationship.
- **Finish-to-Start (FS), Start-to-Start (SS), Finish-to-Finish (FF), Start-to-Finish (SF)** — the four
  precedence relationship types.
- **Lag / lead** — a deliberate delay after, or overlap before, a relationship is otherwise satisfied.
- **Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM) / Activity-on-Node (AON)** — the modern network-diagramming
  convention (full treatment KA 10.2.1).
- **Open end** — an unintended activity with no predecessor or successor other than the network's boundary
  points.
- **Hammock activity** — a support/Level-of-Effort activity (e.g., WP5 Site supervision) modelled with SS/FF
  anchors to the network's boundaries rather than discrete logic of its own.
- **Analogous, parametric, three-point (PERT) estimating** — the three duration-estimating methods, with
  `TE = (O + 4M + P) / 6` and `σ = (P − O) / 6` for three-point estimating.
- **Milestone** — a zero-duration point marking a significant, verifiable event.
- **Baseline schedule / schedule baseline** — the approved, frozen schedule used for progress comparison.
- **DCMA 14-Point Assessment** — a widely referenced schedule-quality check framework (awareness level).

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 10.1.1 [topic 10.1.2, level: recall]**
In the Precedence Diagramming Method, a Start-to-Start (SS) relationship means:
A. The successor cannot finish until the predecessor finishes
B. **The successor cannot start until the predecessor starts** ✔
C. The successor cannot start until the predecessor finishes
D. The successor cannot finish until the predecessor starts
*Rationale:* B is the definition of SS. A describes FF; C describes FS; D describes SF.

**MCQ 10.1.2 [topic 10.1.2, level: application — numerical]**
An activity has ES = 10 weeks and a duration of 8 weeks. Its successor is linked by a Finish-to-Start
relationship with a 2-week lag. What is the successor's earliest start?
A. 18 weeks (forgetting the lag)
B. **20 weeks** ✔
C. 26 weeks
D. 12 weeks
*Rationale:* `EF(predecessor) = ES + Duration = 10 + 8 = 18`; `ES(successor) = EF(predecessor) + Lag = 18 + 2
= 20`. A omits the lag; C wrongly adds the predecessor's duration a second time; D wrongly adds the lag to
the predecessor's start instead of its finish.

**MCQ 10.1.3 [topic 10.1.4, level: application — numerical]**
Using three-point (PERT) estimating, an activity has Optimistic = 10 days, Most Likely = 13 days, Pessimistic
= 22 days. What is the expected duration?
A. 13 days
B. **14 days** ✔
C. 15 days
D. 22 days
*Rationale:* `TE = (O + 4M + P) / 6 = (10 + 52 + 22) / 6 = 84 / 6 = 14`. A is just the most-likely figure,
ignoring O and P; C is a plausible rounding slip; D is the pessimistic figure alone.

**MCQ 10.1.4 [topic 10.1.1, level: analysis]**
A work package is decomposed into three activities whose budgets are $40,000, $35,000 and $20,000, against a
work-package budget of $100,000. What does this indicate?
A. The activities satisfy the 8/80 rule
B. **The decomposition violates the 100% rule, because the activity budgets do not sum to the work package's
   budget** ✔
C. The work package has been decomposed too finely
D. This is normal, because activity budgets never need to reconcile to the work package
*Rationale:* `40,000 + 35,000 + 20,000 = 95,000 ≠ 100,000`; $5,000 of scope is unaccounted for, violating the
100% rule (10.1.1). A is unrelated (8/80 concerns activity size, not budget reconciliation); C and D
misstate the principle.

**MCQ 10.1.5 [topic 10.1.3, level: application]**
In the Pipeline Segment 4B network, Activity E (Pipe-laying & welding) has two predecessors: C2 (Early
Finish, week 10) and D (Early Finish, week 12), both on Finish-to-Start relationships with zero lag. What is
E's earliest start?
A. Week 10
B. **Week 12** ✔
C. Week 22 (the sum of both)
D. Week 11 (the average)
*Rationale:* Where an activity has more than one predecessor, the governing constraint is the *later* of the
early finishes — `ES(E) = max(EF(C2), EF(D)) = max(10, 12) = 12`, confirmed by KA 10.2.2's forward pass. A
wrongly takes the earlier value; C and D are not meaningful operations on start-date constraints.

**MCQ 10.1.6 [topic 10.1.2/10.1.3, level: recall]**
Which of Pipeline Segment 4B's original five work packages is modelled as a hammock activity, linked to the
network by Start-to-Start and Finish-to-Finish relationships rather than by discrete Finish-to-Start logic?
A. WP2 Pipe & valve procurement
B. WP3 Trenching & earthworks
C. WP4 Pipe-laying & welding
D. **WP5 Site supervision** ✔
*Rationale:* WP5 is measured by Level of Effort (KA 6.1.4) and spans the whole project without its own
sequenced logic, correctly modelled with SS (to project start) and FF (to project finish) anchors (10.1.2b,
10.1.3). WP2, WP3 and WP4 are all represented as discrete, logically sequenced activities in the network
(C1/C2, D and E respectively).

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using KA 10.2.4's float figures (Activity B: zero total float; Activity C1: 2 weeks of total float),
   explain why a one-week delay to B changes Pipeline Segment 4B's current forecast finish date while the
   same one-week delay to C1 does not.
2. Using three-point (PERT) estimating, calculate the expected duration and standard deviation for an
   activity with Optimistic = 12 days, Most Likely = 15 days, Pessimistic = 24 days.
3. Explain, in your own words, why WP5 Site supervision is modelled as a hammock activity using Start-to-Start
   and Finish-to-Finish logic, rather than being included as a discrete activity in the Finish-to-Start chain
   analysed by the Critical Path Method in KA 10.2.

**Answers**

1. Activity B sits on the critical path with zero total float (KA 10.2.4), so any delay to it passes straight
   through to the project finish date — a one-week delay to B moves the current forecast finish from week 19
   to week 20. Activity C1 carries 2 weeks of total float, so a one-week delay is fully absorbed within that
   float (leaving 1 week in reserve) and does not, by itself, change the forecast finish date at all.
2. `TE = (12 + 4×15 + 24) / 6 = (12 + 60 + 24) / 6 = 96 / 6 = 16` days; `σ = (24 − 12) / 6 = 12 / 6 = 2` days.
3. WP5 represents support effort (site supervision, PM, QA/HSE oversight) that is measured by Level of Effort
   — its progress tracks elapsed time or the span of the activities it oversees, not a discrete, physically
   measurable deliverable of its own (KA 6.1.4). Forcing it into the Finish-to-Start chain would wrongly
   imply it has its own sequenced logic and could distort the critical path; anchoring it with Start-to-Start
   (to the project start) and Finish-to-Finish (to the project finish) correctly derives its span from the
   network around it instead.
