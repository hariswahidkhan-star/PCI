# Domain 10 — Project Scheduling

## KA 10.4 — Progress measurement & schedule control

Progress measurement is where the schedule and the cost/earned-value system meet. Every physical percent
complete, milestone achievement or units-installed count a controls professional records against the schedule
is the same underlying data that Domain 6 converts into Earned Value (EV) for cost performance — the two are
not separate data-collection exercises, but two uses of one measurement. This Knowledge Area covers that
meeting point from the scheduling side: how progress is rolled up to update a Critical Path Method (CPM)
schedule (cross-reference KA 10.1, 10.2); how Schedule Variance (SV) and the Schedule Performance Index
(SPI), fully defined in KA 6.2, must be read alongside the schedule's float and critical path before either
is used to judge real risk; and how **Earned Schedule (ES)** converts EV/PV performance into genuine time
units — SV(t) and SPI(t) — fixing the well-documented weakness of value-based SPI examined in KA 6.2.7. It
closes with forecasting a completion date and maintaining the schedule baseline. Detailed EVM formulae (PV,
EV, AC, CV, SV, CPI, SPI, TCPI, the EAC family) are defined once, in full, in KA 6.1–6.3 (PMI's *Practice
Standard for Earned Value Management*; AACE International's Total Cost Management Framework) and used here
identically without repetition; this KA's job is the scheduling-specific application and its own time-based
measures.

### 10.4.1 Progress measurement methods for schedule statusing and the physical percent complete roll-up

**Definition & purpose.** Physical percent complete is the measured, objective proportion of a schedule
activity's scope actually accomplished. It is the raw input that both updates the CPM schedule (remaining
duration, forecast finish — 10.4.2) and becomes Earned Value once multiplied by the activity's budget
(KA 6.1.4). The standard measurement methods that generate it — 0/100, 50/50, percentage complete by units,
weighted milestones, and LOE — are defined in full in KA 6.1.4 and are **not repeated here**; this topic
covers how that per-activity percentage is rolled up across a WBS into a single project-level figure, and why
the weighting basis chosen matters.

**The underlying principle.** A single activity's percent complete is unambiguous once its measurement method
is fixed. A *project-level* percent complete is not, because combining individual percentages requires
choosing a weight first — and different, equally defensible bases (budgeted cost, planned duration, planned
labour-hours) can produce materially different headline figures from the *same* physical reality, echoing the
method-sensitivity lesson of KA 6.1.6.

**Formula.**
`Weighted % complete = Σ (weight_i × % complete_i) / Σ weight_i`
for all activities *i*, where *weight* is a chosen, fixed basis. Weighting by budgeted cost reduces this
formula exactly to EV/BAC (KA 6.1.5); weighting by planned duration or labour-hours gives a genuinely
different, equally valid figure.

**Example 10.4.1 — Same physical progress, two weighting bases: Pipeline Segment 4B at Month 3.**

*Scenario.* The five Pipeline Segment 4B work packages and their Month-3 percent complete (KA 6.1.4), each
now also assigned an illustrative planned duration for schedule-weighting purposes:

| WP | % complete | Budget ($) | Planned duration (weeks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| WP1 Design | 100% | 80,000 | 4 |
| WP2 Procurement | 50% | 150,000 | 6 |
| WP3 Trenching | 45% | 200,000 | 10 |
| WP4 Pipe-laying | 50% | 120,000 | 8 |
| WP5 Supervision | 50% | 50,000 | 24 |
| **Total** | | **600,000** | **52** |

*Required.* Overall physical percent complete weighted by (a) budget and (b) planned duration.

*Working.*
(a) `(80,000×100% + 150,000×50% + 200,000×45% + 120,000×50% + 50,000×50%) / 600,000`
`= (80,000 + 75,000 + 90,000 + 60,000 + 25,000) / 600,000 = 330,000 / 600,000 = 55.0%`
(b) `(4×100% + 6×50% + 10×45% + 8×50% + 24×50%) / 52 = (400+300+450+400+1,200) / 52 = 2,750 / 52 = 52.9%`

*Answer.* **Budget-weighted = 55.0%** (identical to EV/BAC, KA 6.1.6). **Duration-weighted = 52.9%.**

*Interpretation.* The 2.1-percentage-point gap arises because WP5 (LOE) carries 46% of total planned
*duration* but only 8% of total *budget* — weighting by duration gives its 50% completion far more influence.
Neither figure is wrong; each is the correct output of its stated basis. A scheduler quoting 52.9% and a cost
engineer quoting 55.0% are not disagreeing — the weighting basis must be stated alongside the number.

*Underlying data:*

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Planned % complete (PV/BAC) | 10.0% | 25.0% | 45.0% | 70.0% | 90.0% | 100.0% |

**Animation 10.4.1 — "Two curves, one project" [digital-only].** The planned curve draws itself Month 1–6; a
blue marker appears at Month 3 (55.0%, above the line — ahead of plan) and a second at Month 5 (67.5%, below
the line — now behind), joined by a dashed segment. Narration cue: "Watch which side of the planned line the
actual line sits on — it can cross from ahead to behind between two reporting periods."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting a "percent complete" figure without stating its weighting basis, inviting a false discrepancy
  between the scheduling and cost functions' numbers.
- Letting a long-duration, low-budget LOE activity dominate a duration-weighted roll-up out of proportion to
  its real cost or effort.
- Treating a healthy roll-up as a substitute for reviewing individual activity status — it can mask a
  critical-path activity that is badly behind (10.4.3).

**AI in this topic.** A scheduling tool with an AI logic-check feature can flag activities whose reported
percent complete is inconsistent with their remaining-duration entries far faster than a manual review
(cross-reference KA 13.5). It cannot verify the percentage was itself honestly measured on site — an
automated check on bad source data simply confirms a wrong number faster.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.1.4 (measurement methods); KA 6.1.5, 6.1.6 (aggregation/method-sensitivity);
KA 10.1 (schedule development); KA 5.3 (WBS/control accounts).

### 10.4.2 Updating and statusing the schedule: remaining duration and progress override

**Definition & purpose.** Statusing is the periodic act of entering actual progress into the CPM schedule as
of a data date — actual start/finish dates and either percent complete or an independently re-estimated
**remaining duration (RD)** — so the network's forward pass recalculates realistic forecast dates for work
not yet started (cross-reference KA 10.2).

**The underlying principle.** Two methods derive RD, and they can diverge sharply. **Retained logic (linear)**
derives RD mechanically from percent complete, implicitly assuming the *original planned rate* applies to the
remainder — the time-based parallel of EAC Method 1's assumption (KA 6.3.2). **Bottom-up (progress override)**
instead re-estimates RD from the rate actually being achieved — the parallel of the bottom-up ETC re-estimate
(KA 6.3.1). Where realised productivity differs materially from plan, only the second method captures it.

**Formula.** `RD (retained logic) = OD × (1 − % complete)`, where OD = original planned duration.
`RD (bottom-up) = remaining quantity / observed rate of progress`, from an independent re-estimate.

**Example 10.4.2 — Statusing mini-case: WP3 Trenching & earthworks, two remaining-duration methods.**

*Scenario.* WP3 (2,000 m of trenching, KA 6.1.4) has an original planned duration of 10 weeks (planned rate
2,000 m ÷ 10 weeks = 200 m/week). By the data date, 6 weeks have elapsed and 900 m have been excavated —
45.0% physical complete (matching KA 6.1.4), against 60.0% of planned duration already elapsed (6/10).

*Required.* RD under (a) retained logic and (b) bottom-up, and the resulting forecast duration under each.

*Working.*
(a) `RD = 10 × (1 − 0.45) = 5.5 weeks` → forecast duration `= 6 + 5.5 = 11.5 weeks`
(b) Observed rate `= 900/6 = 150 m/week`; remaining `= 2,000 − 900 = 1,100 m`
`RD = 1,100/150 = 7.33 weeks` → forecast duration `= 6 + 7.33 = 13.33 weeks`

*Answer.* **RD (retained logic) = 5.5 weeks (11.5 weeks total). RD (bottom-up) = 7.33 weeks (13.33 weeks
total).**

*Interpretation.* WP3's local schedule ratio — percent complete ÷ percent duration elapsed, 45.0%/60.0% = 0.75
— already signals a productivity shortfall (a preview of the SPI-type ratio formalised in 10.4.3–10.4.4).
Retained logic ignores that shortfall for the remainder; bottom-up carries the observed 150 m/week rate
forward and gives a longer, better-evidenced forecast. As with the analogous EAC choice (KA 6.3.6), the
professional must judge — and document — whether the shortfall is a one-off or a persisting rate, not default
to whichever the software calculates automatically.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Accepting a software-calculated retained-logic RD by default on an activity with an established adverse
  trend, without checking it against observed rates.
- Confusing percent duration elapsed with percent complete — they answer different questions.
- Failing to flow a materially longer RD through to logic-linked successor activities (KA 10.1, 10.2).

**AI in this topic.** AI-assisted scheduling tools can compare a declared percent complete/RD against an
activity's own trend or comparable activities elsewhere in the portfolio, flagging entries inconsistent with
observed productivity (cross-reference KA 13.5). They cannot confirm which assumption — one-off cause or
persisting rate — actually applies here; that judgement remains the professional's — **AI proposes; the
professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 6.3.1, 6.3.2 (the analogous cost-side ETC/EAC choice); KA 10.1, 10.2 (the schedule
logic/forward pass this feeds); KA 10.3 (compression once a slip is confirmed).

### 10.4.3 Schedule variance and SPI in the scheduling context: reading value against the critical path

**Definition & purpose.** SV and SPI are defined, derived and interpreted in full in KA 6.2.2 and KA 6.2.4 —
**not repeated here.** This topic covers the one addition a controls professional must make before using
them to judge real schedule risk: because SV/SPI are **value-based** measures aggregated across a control
account or project, they cannot by themselves distinguish a shortfall that threatens the finish date from one
that does not. That distinction requires reading SV/SPI alongside the schedule's **critical path and total
float** (KA 10.2).

**The underlying principle.** SV and SPI weight every activity by its budgeted value, not by its schedule
criticality. A large-budget activity with abundant float can post a large adverse SV with no consequence for
the finish date, since the shortfall is absorbed by consuming float; a small-budget activity on the critical
path with zero float can threaten the finish date the moment it slips at all. An aggregate SV/SPI blends
these two very different risk profiles into one number.

**Mini-case 10.4.3 — Identical SV, opposite schedule risk.**

*Scenario.* Activities Q and R each carry a $150,000 budget, Month-3 PV of $90,000 and EV of $60,000, so each
shows an identical adverse `SV = EV − PV = 60,000 − 90,000 = −$30,000`. Q sits on the critical path with zero
total float; R has 4 weeks of total float before it would threaten the finish date.

*Required.* Interpret the schedule risk each SV represents.

*Working.* SV alone: both `−$30,000`, indistinguishable. Adding float (KA 10.2): Q has no float to absorb
further slip — any additional delay pushes the finish date out day-for-day; R's shortfall can, up to 4 weeks,
be absorbed without moving the finish date, provided that float is not later needed elsewhere on R's path.

*Answer.* **Q's $30,000 adverse SV is an active threat to the finish date; R's identical $30,000 adverse SV,
on current information, is not.**

*Interpretation.* A control-account or project-level SV/SPI that nets Q-type and R-type shortfalls together
cannot make this distinction. This is the specific discipline scheduling brings to EVM: SV/SPI identify *that*
value is being earned more slowly than planned; the CPM schedule's float report identifies *whether it
matters*.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Escalating every adverse SV/SPI with equal urgency regardless of proximity to the critical path.
- Assuming a favourable aggregate SPI means no activity is at risk — it can conceal a critical-path activity
  that is behind (the aggregation pitfall of KA 6.1.5, applied to schedule risk).
- Forgetting float is a finite, shared resource — float apparently available may already be committed
  elsewhere on the same path.

**AI in this topic.** A schedule-analytics tool can cross-reference every account's SV against the current
float report and flag only shortfalls near the critical path for priority review (cross-reference KA 13.5) —
a valuable filter on a large schedule. It cannot judge whether float apparently available on paper is
realistically usable — that judgement remains the scheduler's.

**Cross-references.** KA 6.2.2, 6.2.4 (SV, SPI, not repeated here); KA 6.1.5 (aggregation masking detail);
KA 10.2 (critical path, total/free float); KA 10.3 (compression once a critical-path threat is confirmed).

### 10.4.4 Earned schedule: ES, SV(t) and SPI(t) as time-based measures

**Definition & purpose.** Earned Schedule (ES) is a technique developed by Walt Lipke and now referenced
within AACE International's forecasting practice alongside conventional, PMBOK-aligned EVM reporting, that
re-expresses schedule performance in **time units** rather than currency: "as of today, how far — in weeks
or months — is earned progress from where it should be?" ES is derived directly from the PV curve and EV
already established in KA 6.1–6.3, requiring no new data collection, only a different calculation.

**The underlying principle.** SV and SPI (KA 6.2) compare EV against PV **at the same fixed data date**, in
currency. Earned Schedule instead asks: at what point *on the PV time axis* does the baseline curve reach
today's EV value? That point — read off the curve, not from today's date — is **ES**. Comparing ES to
**Actual Time (AT)**, the real elapsed time to the data date, gives schedule performance in genuine time
units, fixing the completion-point weakness of value-based SPI (KA 6.2.7): because ES is read from a
time-based axis, it is not mechanically pinned to a fixed ceiling the way PV-bounded SPI is.

**Formula(e).**
`ES = N + (EV − PV(N)) / (PV(N+1) − PV(N))`, where N is the latest whole period for which cumulative
`PV(N) ≤ EV` (linear interpolation to the next known baseline point).
`AT` = actual elapsed time to the data date, in the same units as ES.
`SV(t) = ES − AT` (time units; positive = ahead, negative = behind).
`SPI(t) = ES / AT` (dimensionless ratio; > 1.00 favourable, < 1.00 adverse — read exactly as SPI in KA 6.2.4,
but in time rather than value terms).

**Example 10.4.4 — Physical percent complete, ES, SV(t) and SPI(t): Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5.**

*Scenario.* The Pipeline Segment 4B PV baseline (KA 6.1, Fig 6.1.1) and the Month-5 EVM data set (KA 6.3.2:
BAC $600,000; EV $405,000; AC $506,250; CPI 0.80):

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PV, cumulative ($) | 60,000 | 150,000 | 270,000 | 420,000 | 540,000 | 600,000 |

*Required.* (a) Physical percent complete at Month 5; (b) ES; (c) SV(t) and SPI(t); interpretation against
KA 6.3's value-based SPI = 0.75.

*Working.*
(a) `Physical % complete = EV/BAC = 405,000/600,000 = 67.5%`
(b) EV ($405,000) falls between `PV(3) = 270,000` and `PV(4) = 420,000`, so N = 3:
`ES = 3 + (405,000 − 270,000)/(420,000 − 270,000) = 3 + 135,000/150,000 = 3 + 0.90 = 3.90 months`
(c) `AT = 5 months`. `SV(t) = 3.90 − 5 = −1.10 months`. `SPI(t) = 3.90/5 = 0.78`

*Answer.* **Physical % complete = 67.5%. ES = 3.90 months. SV(t) = −1.10 months (adverse). SPI(t) = 0.78
(adverse).**

*Interpretation.* At Month 5, the project has earned only as much value as the baseline expected it to earn by
month 3.90 — it is running **1.10 months behind plan** in genuine calendar terms, not merely a value ratio.
This corroborates the value-based `SPI = 0.75` (KA 6.3.2/6.3.3) while adding what SPI alone cannot: an answer
in the units a scheduler and a sponsor actually plan around.

**Mini-case — the same technique at Month 3, for comparison.** Applying the identical method to the Month-3
data (KA 6.1.6: EV $330,000): `ES = 3 + (330,000 − 270,000)/150,000 = 3.40 months`; `AT = 3`;
`SV(t) = +0.40 months` (favourable); `SPI(t) = 3.40/3 = 1.13` (favourable) — agreeing directionally with KA
6.2's value-based `SPI = 1.22` (also favourable), and confirming the project's schedule position genuinely
reversed from favourable at Month 3 to adverse at Month 5, visible in both measures alike.

**Fig 10.4.4 — Earned schedule construction: Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5.**
Chart type: cumulative-cost S-curve, USD (thousands) vertical axis, Month 1–6 horizontal axis. The PV baseline
(grey, solid) is plotted for all six months. A horizontal dashed line runs from the Month-5 EV value
($405,000) to its intersection with the PV curve (between Month 3 and 4); a vertical dashed line drops from
there to the horizontal axis at **ES = 3.90**. A second vertical marker at **AT = 5** appears in brand blue
`#1D4ED8`, the gap between the two bracketed and labelled "SV(t) = −1.10 months."

*Underlying data:* the PV table above; EV (Month 5) = $405,000; ES = 3.90; AT = 5.00; SV(t) = −1.10;
SPI(t) = 0.78.

**Animation 10.4.4 — "Reading time off the value curve" [digital-only].** A horizontal line extends from the
EV point to the PV curve; a vertical line drops to the time axis at ES = 3.90; a second vertical line rises
from AT = 5, and the gap is measured and labelled "1.10 months behind." Narration cue: "SPI tells you a ratio
of value. Earned Schedule tells you a distance in time — and that distance still means something in the
project's final weeks."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Computing ES from calendar dates directly rather than validated PV/EV data — ES inherits PV's stale-baseline
  risk (KA 6.1.2).
- Reporting SV(t)/SPI(t) as a replacement for, rather than a complement to, value-based SV/SPI and the
  float/critical-path check of 10.4.3.
- Interpolating ES across a period where the baseline changed without first adjusting the PV series (10.4.5).

**AI in this topic.** A dashboarding tool can compute ES, SV(t) and SPI(t) automatically at every data date
from the same PV/EV feed used for value-based EVM (cross-reference KA 13.5). It cannot judge which reading
should drive a decision when the two diverge, nor verify the PV baseline feeding both is current — **AI
proposes; the professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 6.1 (data source); KA 6.2.4, 6.2.7 (SPI's completion-point weakness, which ES
addresses); KA 6.3.2, 6.3.3 (the Month-5 data reused here); KA 9.3, 9.5 (sprints/releases as an analogous
schedule increment in adaptive delivery).

### 10.4.5 Forecasting the completion date and maintaining the baseline

**Definition & purpose.** Once ES, AT and the planned duration (PD) are known, they support a **time-based
forecast of the completion date** — the schedule's equivalent of the EAC family (KA 6.3) — using the
Independent Estimate at Completion (time), **IEAC(t)**. This topic also covers the companion discipline any
forecast depends on: **baseline maintenance**, the rule that ES/SV(t)/SPI(t) are only trustworthy against a
current, formally approved baseline.

**The underlying principle.** IEAC(t) mirrors the EAC family exactly, substituting a schedule **performance
factor (PF)** for CPI: PF = 1.00 assumes the remaining schedule proceeds at the *originally planned* rate
(parallel to EAC Method 1); PF = SPI(t) assumes *current* schedule efficiency continues (parallel to
Method 2); a composite **Schedule Cost Index, SCI(t) = SPI(t) × CPI**, assumes schedule *and* cost pressure
continue together (parallel to Method 3) — the same one-off-versus-trend diagnostic (KA 6.3.6) governs the
choice between them.

**Formula.** `IEAC(t) = AT + (PD − ES) / PF`, where PD = the baselined total planned duration and PF is the
chosen performance factor (1.00, SPI(t), or SCI(t) = SPI(t) × CPI).

**Example 10.4.5 — Forecast completion date for Pipeline Segment 4B, Month 5.**

*Scenario.* Continuing 10.4.4 (AT = 5, ES = 3.90, SPI(t) = 0.78), with PD = 6 months (baselined project
duration) and CPI = 0.80 (KA 6.3.2).

*Required.* IEAC(t) under PF = 1.00, PF = SPI(t), and PF = SCI(t).

*Working.*
`PD − ES = 6 − 3.90 = 2.10 months`
PF = 1.00: `IEAC(t) = 5 + 2.10/1.00 = 7.10 months`
PF = SPI(t) = 0.78: `IEAC(t) = 5 + 2.10/0.78 = 5 + 2.69 = 7.69 months`
PF = SCI(t) = 0.78 × 0.80 = 0.624: `IEAC(t) = 5 + 2.10/0.624 = 5 + 3.37 = 8.37 months`

*Answer.* **IEAC(t) ranges from 7.10 months (PF = 1.00) to 8.37 months (PF = SCI(t))** against a 6-month
baseline. Taking, for illustration only, a Month-1 baseline start of 1 January 2026 (baseline finish
30 June 2026; not fixed elsewhere in KA 6.1–6.3), the PF = SPI(t) forecast of 7.69 months is a 1.69-month
slip — roughly 51 days (1.69 × an illustrative 30.4-day average month) — a completion around
**20 August 2026**.

*Interpretation.* This is the schedule-side counterpart of KA 6.3.7's multi-method EAC comparison: the same
data yields a materially different forecast depending only on the stated assumption. As in KA 6.3.6, the
correct response is to diagnose which assumption the evidence supports and disclose the method alongside the
date — never report a single, unqualified completion date.

**Baseline maintenance and change control.** ES, SV(t), SPI(t) and IEAC(t) are only as trustworthy as the PV
baseline they are read against — KA 6.1.2's stale-PV lesson, applied to the time axis. A baseline may only be
changed following an approved scope, schedule or commercial change (cross-reference KA 5.4, KA 8.4): if
Pipeline Segment 4B's sponsor approves a two-week extension in Month 5 for an added valve station, PD moves
from 6 to 6.5 months and the PV curve is re-issued; every subsequent calculation must use the revised
baseline, with prior-period figures marked as calculated against the superseded one. Re-baselining **to
absorb an approved change** is legitimate; re-baselining **to erase an adverse variance with no approved
change behind it** is a control breach that destroys the credibility of every later SV(t)/SPI(t) reading.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating IEAC(t) as a single, precise date rather than a range conditional on a stated assumption, mirroring
  the equivalent EAC pitfall (KA 6.3.7).
- Re-baselining informally, without a documented, approved change — removing the evidence a later SV(t)/
  SPI(t) reading would need to demonstrate a real trend.
- Using a stale PD in the IEAC(t) formula after a formal extension has been granted, double-counting or
  omitting the approved change.

**AI in this topic.** A predictive-scheduling model can compute IEAC(t) across many projects, trend SPI(t)/
SCI(t) period-on-period, and flag where the spread between PF assumptions is widening (cross-reference
KA 13.5) — a low-risk automation of deterministic arithmetic, exactly as in KA 6.3.7's EAC fan chart. It
cannot confirm a baseline change was properly approved, nor judge which PF assumption the evidence supports —
both remain the professional's responsibility — **AI proposes; the professional disposes.**

**Cross-references.** KA 6.3.1–6.3.7 (the EAC family this topic mirrors on the time axis); KA 6.1.2
(stale-baseline pitfall); KA 5.4, 8.4 (change control); KA 10.3 (compression/resource responses to a
confirmed slip).

### Key terms

- **Physical percent complete** — the measured proportion of an activity's/roll-up's scope accomplished, via
  the methods defined in KA 6.1.4.
- **Weighted % complete roll-up** — per-activity percentages combined on a stated weighting basis (budget,
  duration, labour-hours); budget-weighting reduces to EV/BAC.
- **Statusing** — entering actual progress into the schedule as of a data date to recalculate the network.
- **Remaining Duration (RD)** — forecast time left on an activity; via **retained logic**
  (`OD × (1 − % complete)`) or **bottom-up/progress override** (an independent re-estimate).
- **Earned Schedule (ES)** — the point on the PV baseline's time axis where cumulative PV equals current EV;
  the time-based analogue of EV.
- **Actual Time (AT)** — real elapsed time from project start to the data date, in ES's units.
- **SV(t), SPI(t)** — ES's variance (`ES − AT`) and index (`ES/AT`); schedule performance in time, not
  currency.
- **IEAC(t)** — Independent Estimate at Completion (time): `AT + (PD − ES)/PF`, PF being 1.00, SPI(t), or
  SCI(t) = SPI(t) × CPI.
- **Total float / critical path** — schedule-network context (KA 10.2) needed to judge whether SV/SPI
  threatens the finish date.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 10.4.1 [topic 10.4.1, level: application — numerical]**
Three activities: A — budget $100,000, 80% complete; B — budget $150,000, 40% complete; C — budget $250,000,
60% complete. What is the budget-weighted overall percent complete?
A. 60.0%
B. **58.0%** ✔
C. 50.0%
D. 65.0%
*Rationale:* `(100,000×0.80+150,000×0.40+250,000×0.60)/500,000 = 290,000/500,000 = 58.0%`. A is the unweighted
average of the three percentages; C and D are arithmetic slips.

**MCQ 10.4.2 [topic 10.4.2, level: application — numerical]**
An activity has an original duration of 12 weeks and is 30% physically complete. Using the retained-logic
method, what is the remaining duration?
A. 3.6 weeks
B. **8.4 weeks** ✔
C. 8.0 weeks
D. 12.0 weeks
*Rationale:* `RD = 12 × (1 − 0.30) = 8.4 weeks`. A mistakenly reports the elapsed portion (12 × 0.30).

**MCQ 10.4.3 [topic 10.4.3, level: analysis]**
Two activities each show an identical adverse SV of −$40,000. Activity M has 6 weeks of total float; Activity
N sits on the critical path with zero float. Which statement is correct?
A. Both activities pose an identical threat to the project finish date, since their SV is identical
B. **Activity N's shortfall is the more urgent schedule risk, because it has no float to absorb further
   slippage** ✔
C. Activity M's shortfall is more urgent, because float always indicates higher-risk work
D. SV cannot be compared between two activities with different float values
*Rationale:* SV alone cannot distinguish schedule risk; float does. Zero-float, critical-path slippage
threatens the finish date directly (10.4.3), while float on M can absorb the same shortfall.

**MCQ 10.4.4 [topic 10.4.4, level: application — numerical]**
A project's PV baseline is: Month 1 $100,000; Month 2 $220,000; Month 3 $360,000; Month 4 $500,000. At a
Month-4 data date, EV = $300,000. What is the Earned Schedule (ES)?
A. 2.00 months
B. 3.00 months
C. **2.57 months** ✔
D. 2.29 months
*Rationale:* EV falls between `PV(2)=220,000` and `PV(3)=360,000`: `ES = 2 + 80,000/140,000 = 2.57`. A and B
pick a period boundary without interpolating.

**MCQ 10.4.5 [topic 10.4.5, level: application — numerical]**
A project has a baselined duration (PD) of 10 months. At the data date, ES = 6 months and AT = 7 months. Using
PF = 1.00, what is IEAC(t)?
A. 10 months
B. **11 months** ✔
C. 13 months
D. 7 months
*Rationale:* `IEAC(t) = AT + (PD − ES)/PF = 7 + 4/1.00 = 11`. A wrongly reports PD unchanged; D reports AT
alone.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using the Pipeline Segment 4B PV baseline (Month 1–6: $60,000 / $150,000 / $270,000 / $420,000 / $540,000
   / $600,000) and an EV of $486,000 at a Month-5 data date, calculate ES, SV(t) and SPI(t).
2. Explain, in one or two sentences, why SPI(t) (Earned Schedule) does not suffer from the same "drift toward
   1.00 near completion" weakness as value-based SPI (KA 6.2.7).
3. Using the WP3 mini-case in 10.4.2, explain why a bottom-up, observed-productivity remaining-duration
   estimate can be more defensible than the simple retained-logic formula, and state which of the two RD
   figures a professional should generally prefer to report if the productivity shortfall is expected to
   persist.

**Answers**

1. EV ($486,000) falls between `PV(4) = 420,000` and `PV(5) = 540,000`:
   `ES = 4 + (486,000 − 420,000)/(540,000 − 420,000) = 4 + 66,000/120,000 = 4 + 0.55 = 4.55 months`.
   `SV(t) = 4.55 − 5 = −0.45 months` (adverse). `SPI(t) = 4.55/5 = 0.91` (adverse).
2. Value-based SPI is bounded because PV and EV cannot exceed BAC, so it is pulled mechanically toward 1.00
   regardless of true lateness. ES compares two genuinely time-based quantities: at completion, ES equals the
   full planned duration (PD) while AT equals the true elapsed time, so SPI(t) = PD/AT keeps reflecting real
   calendar lateness even at completion, unlike value-based SPI.
3. Retained logic assumes remaining work reverts to the *originally planned* rate, optimistic where realised
   productivity has been below plan throughout, as in WP3 (150 m/week observed versus 200 m/week planned). The
   bottom-up estimate captures the persisting trend, giving a longer, better-evidenced RD (7.33 versus 5.5
   weeks); where the shortfall is expected to persist, the bottom-up figure should generally be reported.
