# Domain 12 — Risk Management for Project Controls

## KA 12.3 — Contingency & management reserve

KA 12.1 sets out the risk framework (ISO 31000's principles; risk versus uncertainty; risk appetite and
tolerance); KA 12.2 works through the full risk process — identification, qualitative and quantitative
analysis, response planning and monitoring — and produces a quantified project risk register. This KA picks
up where that register ends and asks the question a project controls professional is actually accountable
for: how does a quantified risk register become a funded, governed budget line? **Contingency reserve** funds
the risks the register has already identified, assessed and, in effect, pre-approved a response budget for —
it sits *inside* the cost baseline and is drawn down within delegated authority when a specific named risk
occurs. **Management reserve** funds what the register could not have listed — genuinely unforeseeable
events, or sponsor-approved scope growth — and sits *outside* the cost baseline, reachable only through
formal change authorisation. KA 3.1 §3.1.5–3.1.6 already defines this distinction and its place in the
budgeting hierarchy in full, using the Northgate Water Treatment Upgrade; this KA does not repeat that
derivation. It instead goes deeper on three things Domain 3 only previewed: the methods used to **size**
contingency (deterministic %, expected value, and probabilistic/Monte Carlo to a stated confidence level);
the **governance of drawdown** once execution begins, including what happens when contingency runs out
before its risks stop occurring; and how both reserves sit against the **control-account** structure cost
management is built on. The worked example continues **Pipeline Segment 4B** (BAC $600,000, SAR 2,250,000 at
an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) from KA 6.1–6.3, so the reserves built here reconcile exactly to the BAC
and EAC figures those chapters already report.

### 12.3.1 Contingency reserve and management reserve: known-unknowns, unknown-unknowns and who authorises each

**Definition & purpose.** Contingency reserve is the funded counterpart of a **retained risk** in ISO 31000's
risk-treatment vocabulary: a risk the organisation accepts and explicitly budgets for rather than avoiding,
transferring or fully mitigating. AACE International's Recommended Practice 40R-08, *Contingency Estimating —
General Principles*, describes contingency as addressing cost variation from foreseeable, quantifiable risk
within the current scope — exactly what a completed KA 12.2 risk register represents. Management reserve is
the counterpart of everything the risk process cannot enumerate — true unknown-unknowns — plus approved scope
growth never in the register. PMI's PMBOK Guide describes reserve analysis, within Cost Management, as
producing this same two-tier structure with differing levels of authority.

**The underlying principle.** The risk register (KA 12.2) is the audit trail connecting every contingency
dollar to a named risk. An amount that cannot be traced to a register line is either an unrecorded padding
of an estimate (a pitfall below) or genuinely belongs in management reserve.

**Quick reference — governance roles.**

| | Contingency reserve | Management reserve |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | Known-unknowns in the register | Unknown-unknowns; approved scope growth |
| Sits | Inside the cost baseline (BAC) | Outside the cost baseline |
| Sized by | The register (12.3.2) | Sponsor/PMO policy, typically % of BAC |
| Drawn down by | PM/controls professional, delegated authority | Sponsor/steering committee, formal change |
| Trigger | A named risk materialises | Unforeseen event, scope growth, or contingency exhaustion (12.3.3) |

**Example 12.3.1.** Pipeline Segment 4B's BAC, already reported as $600,000 in KA 6.1, is not one
undifferentiated figure: 12.3.2 shows it is base estimate $540,000 plus a $60,000 contingency sized from a
three-line risk register. A further $30,000 of management reserve (5.0% of BAC) sits outside that $600,000,
giving a total project budget of $630,000. Every EVM figure in KA 6.1–6.3 uses the $600,000 BAC, never
$630,000 — the KA 3.1 §3.1.5 convention, restated here for a Domain-12-only reader.

**Mini-case — a provision is not a contingency reserve.** Meridian Rail Contracting LLC's rail-signalling
contract (KA 1.4) recognises a workmanship-warranty **provision** under IAS 37 once handover triggers a
present obligation — a recognised balance-sheet liability. Contingency reserve looks superficially similar
(both use expected-value thinking) but is a budgeting/cost-control allowance for funding identified risks
*before* they crystallise, not itself a liability. Once a contingency-funded risk occurs and creates a real
obligation, *that* may separately need an IAS 37 provision assessment — contingency is simply where the cash
was expected to come from. Treating "we hold contingency for this" as the same as "we have provided for this
in the accounts" is a common, avoidable confusion between a project-controls concept and a reporting one.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Confusing contingency with an IAS 37 provision. (2) Double-counting a risk inside a
padded estimate *and* the formal contingency line (cross-reference KA 3.1 §3.1.6). (3) Sizing contingency
before the register (KA 12.2) is materially complete. (4) Treating contingency as available for any cost
pressure, not just its named risks — developed as the "slush fund" pitfall in 12.3.3.

**AI in this topic.** Risk-register/budgeting tools can flag a contingency line with no linked register entry,
or a register entry with no funded response — a genuine traceability control at programme scale (cross-
reference KA 13.4). They cannot judge whether an item is properly a retained risk (contingency) or should
escalate to management reserve; that classification remains a professional/PMO judgement.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 §3.1.1, §3.1.5–3.1.6; KA 1.4 (IAS 37); KA 12.1 (risk appetite); KA 12.2 (the
register this KA assumes complete); KA 6.1 (BAC).

### 12.3.2 Sizing contingency: deterministic, expected-value and probabilistic methods

**Definition & purpose.** AACE RP 40R-08 describes a spectrum running from a simple percentage allowance
through expected-value analysis to full probabilistic (Monte Carlo-type) simulation. This topic works all
three on the same register.

**The underlying principle.** The **deterministic** method asks what a comparable project historically
needed — fast, but not linked to this project's actual exposure. The **expected-value** method asks what
probability-weighted average this specific register implies — traceable, but a mean states no confidence
level. The **probabilistic** method asks at what funding level a chosen confidence can be stated (a
"P-value," e.g. P80: an 80% likelihood the funded amount is not exceeded) — the only one that uses the full
shape of the outcome distribution, not just its mean.

**Formula(e).** Deterministic: `Contingency = x% × Base estimate`. Expected value: `Contingency = Σ
(Probability_i × Impact_i)`. Probabilistic: the value `c` such that `Cumulative probability [Total risk cost
≤ c] ≥ P`, read from the distribution of combined-risk outcomes.

**Example 12.3.2 — sizing Pipeline Segment 4B's contingency.**

*Scenario.* KA 12.2's register for Pipeline Segment 4B (base estimate $540,000):

| Risk | Work package | Probability | Impact ($) | Expected value ($) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 — Adverse ground conditions | WP3 Trenching | 30.0% | 100,000 | 30,000 |
| R2 — Weld defect rework | WP4 Pipe-laying & welding | 40.0% | 50,000 | 20,000 |
| R3 — Subcontractor mobilisation delay | WP2 Procurement | 25.0% | 40,000 | 10,000 |
| **Total** | | | | **60,000** |

Policy applies a flat 10.0% contingency rate to the base estimate as a cross-check.

*Required.* (a) Expected-value contingency; (b) deterministic contingency; (c) P80 contingency, treating the
three risks as independent binary events.

*Working.*

(a) `Contingency = (0.30×100,000) + (0.40×50,000) + (0.25×40,000) = 30,000+20,000+10,000 = 60,000`

(b) `Contingency = 10.0% × 540,000 = 54,000`

(c) With three independent risks there are 2³ = 8 outcomes:

| Risks occurring | Cost ($) | Probability | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | 0 | .70×.60×.75=.315 | .315 |
| R3 only | 40,000 | .70×.60×.25=.105 | .420 |
| R2 only | 50,000 | .70×.40×.75=.210 | .630 |
| R2+R3 | 90,000 | .70×.40×.25=.070 | .700 |
| R1 only | 100,000 | .30×.60×.75=.135 | .835 |
| R1+R3 | 140,000 | .30×.60×.25=.045 | .880 |
| R1+R2 | 150,000 | .30×.40×.75=.090 | .970 |
| R1+R2+R3 | 190,000 | .30×.40×.25=.030 | 1.000 |

Probabilities sum to 1.000. Cumulative probability first reaches 80% at **$100,000** (cumulative 83.5%,
versus 70.0% at $90,000), so **P80 = $100,000**. As a check, the probability-weighted mean of this table is
`0(.315)+40,000(.105)+50,000(.210)+90,000(.070)+100,000(.135)+140,000(.045)+150,000(.090)+190,000(.030) =
0+4,200+10,500+6,300+13,500+6,300+13,500+5,700 = 60,000`, matching (a) exactly.

*Answer.* **Deterministic = $54,000; expected value = $60,000; P80 = $100,000.** (P50, the median, is
$50,000 — below the mean, because the distribution is right-skewed.)

*Interpretation.* The organisation adopts the **expected value, $60,000**, as baselined contingency — it
reconciles exactly to KA 6.1's $600,000 BAC ($540,000 + $60,000). The deterministic figure (10.0% lower) is a
policy cross-check only. The P80 figure (66.7% higher than the mean) is disclosed to the sponsor as
risk-appetite context and informs management reserve (12.3.4), but is not itself baselined — doing so would
inflate BAC beyond KA 6.1's published figure.

**Mini-case — an honest simplification.** The eight-scenario enumeration is exact for its assumptions, but
those assumptions simplify real probabilistic analysis: each impact is a single fixed number rather than a
range (e.g. triangular), and the three risks are assumed independent, when ground conditions and weld
defects could plausibly share a cause (e.g. weak supervision). A genuine Monte Carlo simulation samples
continuous ranges and models correlation across thousands of iterations, producing a smoother curve than this
eight-point step function — the enumeration here is a hand-reproducible teaching approximation, not a
substitute for a properly specified simulation.

cumulative probability (0–100%), stepping at each of the eight outcomes. Brand-blue `#1D4ED8` vertical

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating a deterministic % as sufficient once a register exists. (2) Reporting the
expected value as if it stated a confidence level. (3) Running a probabilistic analysis on single-point
impacts without disclosing the simplification. (4) Ignoring correlation, which typically widens the true
distribution.

**AI in this topic.** Monte Carlo software, often built into scheduling/cost platforms, samples continuous
distributions, models correlation and runs thousands of iterations well beyond a hand calculation (cross-
reference KA 13.5) — a genuine advance at programme scale. It cannot supply the probability, impact-range or
correlation assumptions themselves; those remain engineering/commercial judgements, and a simulation built on
poor inputs is precise-looking but ungrounded (cross-reference KA 13.2). The confidence level funded to (P50,
P80 or another) is a risk-appetite decision for the sponsor, not a software output.

**Cross-references.** KA 12.1 (risk appetite); KA 12.2 (the register); KA 3.1 §3.1.6; KA 3.2 (AACE estimate
classes); KA 6.1 (BAC).

### 12.3.3 Contingency draw-down: monitoring, curves and governance

**Definition & purpose.** A **contingency drawdown curve** tracks the reserve's remaining balance over the
project, plotted against a planned reference profile — the risk-reserve equivalent of KA 6.1's PV S-curve —
so an unusual acceleration in actual consumption is flagged before the reserve is exhausted.

**The underlying principle.** Governance rests on two mechanisms: a **delegated authority** letting the
controls professional/PM approve a draw without new sponsor sign-off, provided it is against a specific,
named, already-budgeted risk; and an **escalation trigger** — typically contingency exhaustion — that forces
a formal request into management reserve, under the same change-control discipline as any other access to
that fund (KA 3.1 §3.1.6).

**Formula.** `Contingency remaining(t) = Contingency reserve − Σ(drawdowns to date)`. A planned reference is
commonly modelled against physical progress: `Planned remaining(t) = Contingency reserve × (1 − % complete
(t))`.

**Example 12.3.3 — drawing down Pipeline Segment 4B's contingency.**

*Scenario.* Contingency = $60,000 (R1 $30,000, R2 $20,000, R3 $10,000), monitored in aggregate rather than
ring-fenced per risk. Using KA 6.1's % complete (Month 1: 10.0%; Month 4: 70.0%), planned remaining =
`60,000 × (1 − % complete)`. At Month 1, R3 occurs, actual cost $23,000 (below its $40,000 impact). At Month
4, R1 occurs, actual cost $42,000 (below its $100,000 impact). R2 never occurs (avoided via extra QA
inspection).

*Required.* Planned vs actual balance by month, and the Month-4 governance action.

*Working.* `Planned(1) = 60,000×0.90 = 54,000`; `Planned(4) = 60,000×0.30 = 18,000`.

Month 1: `Actual = 60,000 − 23,000 = 37,000` — already below the $54,000 planned reference, an early warning.

Month 4: draw needed $42,000; available $37,000; `Shortfall = 42,000 − 37,000 = 5,000`. R1 is pre-identified
and in-scope, so the draw itself needs no new authorisation up to the available balance — but the $5,000
excess does, because contingency is now exhausted. Formal change control transfers $5,000 from management
reserve into contingency (i.e. into BAC):

`New contingency ceiling = 60,000+5,000 = 65,000`; `Total drawn = 23,000+42,000 = 65,000`; `Remaining =
65,000−65,000 = 0`
`New BAC = 600,000+5,000 = 605,000`; `Management reserve remaining = 30,000−5,000 = 25,000`
`Check: 605,000+25,000 = 630,000 = Total project budget (unchanged)` ✓

*Answer.* **BAC = $605,000; contingency remaining = $0; management reserve remaining = $25,000; total
project budget = $630,000 (unchanged).**

*Interpretation.* A draw against a named, in-scope risk does not change BAC (Month 1) — an internal
reallocation within a fund the risk already belonged to. Once that fund is exhausted, replenishing it *does*
raise BAC, because the transfer is mechanically the same baseline-change event KA 3.1 §3.1.6 describes for
scope growth, even though the cause here (an underfunded but correctly identified risk) differs in kind. At
close, R2 never materialises, but because R1 and R3 already consumed the full $65,000 pool, there is no
unused contingency to release — unlike KA 3.1's Northgate mini-case. Total utilisation: $65,000 against an
original $60,000 allocation (108.3%).

**Mini-case — aggregate monitoring avoided a premature escalation.** Had the $10,000 allocated to R3 been
ring-fenced, the $23,000 Month-1 draw would have shown a $13,000 deficit against R3 alone — before R1 had
even been tested — forcing an escalation the pooled approach avoided (the pool still held $37,000 after that
draw). Rigid per-risk ring-fencing is simpler to explain but can force artificial escalations whenever actual
cost differs from assessed impact, which — as both realised risks here show — is the normal case.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Using contingency as a general-purpose **slush fund** rather than restricting draws
to named risks — this destroys traceability and can mask a genuine overrun as an "already funded" event. (2)

(3) Informally "borrowing" from management reserve without formal approval. (4) Rigid per-risk ring-fencing
that manufactures premature escalations.

**AI in this topic.** Dashboard tools can compare actual drawdown against the planned profile automatically
across a portfolio and flag divergence — such as the Month-1 gap — before a monthly cycle would (cross-

realisation or an unauthorised slush-fund use; that check against the actual register entry remains the
professional's responsibility.

### 12.3.4 Reserves in the budget hierarchy: control account → project budget → management reserve

**Definition & purpose.** KA 3.1 §3.1.1 built the hierarchy top-down. This topic builds the same structure
bottom-up, from the control accounts (KA 1.5, KA 5.3) where cost, schedule and scope are integrated and
where KA 6.1's earned value is actually measured.

**The underlying principle.** **Distributed contingency** is allocated to the control account whose scope the
risk relates to — used throughout this KA, since each risk maps to one work package. **Undistributed
contingency** is instead held centrally for cross-cutting or not-yet-assigned risks. Either way, control-
account budgets (base + distributed contingency) plus any undistributed contingency must sum exactly to BAC;
BAC plus management reserve must sum exactly to the total project budget.

**Formula.** `BAC = Σ(control-account budgets) + Undistributed contingency`. `Total project budget = BAC +
Management reserve`.

**Example 12.3.4 — distributing contingency across control accounts.**

*Scenario.* The $60,000 contingency from Example 12.3.2 is fully distributed to the three affected work
packages; KA 6.1.4's five work-package totals are unchanged.

| Work package | Base scope ($) | Distributed contingency ($) | Total (= KA 6.1.4, $) |
|---|---|---|---|
| WP1 Design | 80,000 | 0 | 80,000 |
| WP2 Procurement | 140,000 | 10,000 (R3) | 150,000 |
| WP3 Trenching | 170,000 | 30,000 (R1) | 200,000 |
| WP4 Pipe-laying | 100,000 | 20,000 (R2) | 120,000 |
| WP5 Supervision | 50,000 | 0 | 50,000 |
| **Total (= BAC)** | **540,000** | **60,000** | **600,000** |

*Required.* Confirm reconciliation to BAC and build the total project budget.

*Working.* `Σ base = 540,000`; `Σ contingency = 60,000`; `BAC = 540,000+60,000 = 600,000` ✓ (matches KA 6.1
exactly). `Total project budget = 600,000+30,000 = 630,000`.

*Answer.* **BAC = $600,000 (SAR 2,250,000); Total project budget = $630,000 (SAR 2,362,500).**

*Interpretation.* WP1 (complete by KA 6.1.4's Month-3 status) and WP5 (Level of Effort) carry no
contingency — a deliberate, risk-based allocation, not an oversight. Spreading contingency evenly across all
five accounts would fund WP1/WP5 for risks that do not apply to them while under-funding WP3, which carries
the largest single risk.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Allocating contingency evenly regardless of actual risk exposure. (2) A
control-account owner treating distributed contingency as general working capital rather than a

**AI in this topic.** ERP/EPC platforms can map identified risks to affected control accounts and propose a
distributed-versus-undistributed split (cross-reference KA 1.5, KA 13.4). They cannot decide the right split
for a risk spanning several accounts, nor detect an owner quietly treating distributed contingency as general
funding — both remain for the professional and PMO to review.

### Key terms

- **Contingency reserve** — budgeted allowance, inside the cost baseline, for identified risks retained in
  the register; drawn down when the named risk occurs.
- **Management reserve** — budgeted allowance, outside the cost baseline, for unforeseeable events or
  approved scope growth; accessible only through formal baseline change.
- **Known-unknowns / unknown-unknowns** — identified, quantified register risks versus events the register
  could not list.
- **Deterministic method** — `x% × Base estimate`; policy-based, not register-specific.
- **Expected-value method** — `Σ(Probability_i × Impact_i)`; a probability-weighted mean with no stated
  confidence level.
- **Probabilistic (Monte Carlo) method** — sizing to a stated confidence/percentile (P50, P80) from the full
  outcome distribution.
- **P50 / P80** — funding level at which cumulative probability of not being exceeded reaches 50%/80%; P80
  is typically above the mean for a right-skewed distribution.
- **Contingency drawdown curve** — plotted remaining balance versus a planned reference profile.
- **Distributed / undistributed contingency** — allocated to a specific control account versus held
  centrally at project level.
- **Contingency exhaustion / escalation** — the point at which a further named risk needs funding beyond
  remaining contingency, triggering a formal transfer from management reserve.
- **Contingency as a slush fund (pitfall)** — misusing contingency for cost pressure unrelated to its named
  risks.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 12.3.1 [topic 12.3.1, level: recall]**
Which statement correctly distinguishes contingency reserve from management reserve?
A. Contingency reserve is always larger than management reserve
B. Contingency reserve funds known, identified risks and sits inside the cost baseline; management reserve
   funds unforeseeable events and sits outside it ✔
C. Management reserve can be drawn without formal approval, while contingency always needs sponsor sign-off
D. Contingency reserve and management reserve are two names for the same fund
*Rationale:* B is the governing distinction (12.3.1). A is not guaranteed — relative size depends on risk
profile and policy. C reverses the authority levels. D collapses a distinction the KA depends on.

**MCQ 12.3.2 [topic 12.3.2, level: application — numerical]**
A register has two risks: Risk A, probability 20%, impact $250,000; Risk B, probability 50%, impact $80,000.
What is the expected-value contingency?
A. $40,000
B. $90,000 ✔
C. $130,000
D. $330,000
*Rationale:* `(0.20×250,000)+(0.50×80,000) = 50,000+40,000 = 90,000`. A uses only Risk B; C wrongly halves
Risk A's impact; D sums raw impacts unweighted.

**MCQ 12.3.3 [topic 12.3.2, level: analysis]**
A register produces an expected-value contingency of $60,000 and a P80 contingency of $100,000. Why do the
two figures differ?
A. The P80 figure must be a calculation error
B. The expected value is a mean with no stated confidence level; P80 states the funding level with an 80%
   probability of not being exceeded — typically higher than the mean for a right-skewed distribution ✔
C. P80 is always the sum of the highest-impact risks
D. The two figures should always be identical
*Rationale:* B correctly distinguishes a mean from a percentile (12.3.2). A and D wrongly assume agreement;
C misdescribes how a percentile is derived.

**MCQ 12.3.4 [topic 12.3.3, level: application — numerical]**
Contingency reserve is $60,000. Two pre-identified risks occur, actual costs $23,000 and $42,000, drawn from
the same pooled contingency. What amount must be escalated to management reserve?
A. $0
B. $5,000 ✔
C. $23,000
D. $65,000
*Rationale:* Total drawn = `23,000+42,000 = 65,000`; shortfall = `65,000−60,000 = 5,000` (Example 12.3.3). A
ignores the excess; C mistakes the first draw for the shortfall; D mistakes total drawn for the escalation.

**MCQ 12.3.5 [topic 12.3.4, level: recall]**
Where does management reserve sit in the budget hierarchy?
A. Inside a specific control account, alongside distributed contingency
B. Inside the cost baseline (BAC), but outside any single control account
C. Outside the cost baseline (BAC) entirely, added on top to form the total project budget ✔
D. It replaces contingency reserve once contingency is exhausted
*Rationale:* C is the governing convention: `Total project budget = BAC + Management reserve` (12.3.4). A and
B wrongly place it inside BAC; D misdescribes escalation (12.3.3), which transfers funds, not roles.

   probability-weighted mean, fully traceable, with no confidence level attached; the P80 figure ($100,000)
   states the funding level with an 80% probability of not being exceeded. The expected value was baselined,
   because it reconciles exactly to KA 6.1's $600,000 BAC and is directly traceable to the register; P80 was
   disclosed separately as risk-appetite context rather than baselined.
2. Cost baseline = `900,000+81,000 = 981,000`. Management reserve = `981,000×0.04 = 39,240`. Total project
   budget = `981,000+39,240 = 1,020,240`.
3. Advantage: ring-fencing shows precisely, per control account, how much of a specific risk's reserve
   remains, supporting tight accountability. Disadvantage: because actual realised cost often differs from
   assessed impact (as both R1 and R3 did), rigid ring-fencing can force an apparent deficit — and premature
   escalation — even while the project's total contingency pool still has ample balance elsewhere.
