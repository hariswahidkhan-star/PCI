# Domain 12 — Risk Management for Project Controls

## KA 12.3 — Contingency & management reserve

KA 12.1 sets out the risk framework (ISO 31000's principles, risk versus uncertainty, risk appetite and
tolerance); KA 12.2 works through the full risk process — identification, qualitative and quantitative
analysis, response planning and monitoring — and produces a quantified project risk register. This KA picks
up exactly where that register ends and asks the question a project controls professional is actually
accountable for: **how does a quantified risk register become a funded, governed budget line?** The answer
has two parts, and keeping them separate is the single most important discipline in this KA. **Contingency
reserve** funds the risks the register has already identified, assessed and, in effect, pre-approved a
response budget for — it sits *inside* the cost baseline and can be drawn down by the project controls
professional or project manager, within delegated authority, when a specific named risk occurs.
**Management reserve** funds what the register could not have listed — genuinely unforeseeable events, or
scope the sponsor later approves — and sits *outside* the cost baseline, reachable only through formal
change authorisation. KA 3.1 §3.1.5–3.1.6 already introduced this distinction and its place in the budgeting
hierarchy in full, using the Northgate Water Treatment Upgrade; this KA does not repeat that derivation. It
instead goes deeper on three things Domain 3 only previewed: the methods actually used to **size**
contingency (deterministic percentage, expected value from the register, and probabilistic/Monte Carlo
analysis to a stated confidence level); the **governance of drawdown** once execution begins, including what
happens when contingency runs out before the risks it was meant to cover have finished occurring; and how
both reserves sit against the **control-account structure** the rest of cost management is built on. The
worked examples continue the **Pipeline Segment 4B** project (Meridian Rail Contracting LLC's illustrative
entity family; BAC $600,000, SAR 2,250,000 at an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) already carried through KA
6.1–6.3, so the contingency and management reserve built here reconcile exactly to the BAC and EAC figures
those chapters already report.

### 12.3.1 Contingency reserve and management reserve: known-unknowns, unknown-unknowns and who authorises each

**Definition & purpose.** Framed from the risk-management side rather than the budgeting side (cross-
reference KA 3.1 §3.1.6 for the full budgeting-hierarchy definition), **contingency reserve** is the funded
counterpart of a **retained risk** in ISO 31000's risk-treatment vocabulary: a risk the organisation has
chosen neither to avoid, transfer nor fully mitigate, and has instead accepted and explicitly budgeted for.
AACE International's Recommended Practice 40R-08, *Contingency Estimating — General Principles*, describes
contingency as addressing cost variation from foreseeable, quantifiable risk within the current scope of
work — which is exactly what a completed KA 12.2 risk register represents. **Management reserve** is the
budgetary counterpart of everything the risk process, by construction, cannot enumerate — true unknown-
unknowns — plus sponsor-approved scope growth that was never part of the register in the first place. PMI's
PMBOK Guide describes reserve analysis as a technique within Cost Management that produces this same
two-tier structure and the differing levels of authority attached to each tier.

**The underlying principle.** The project risk register (KA 12.2) is the single audit trail that should
connect every dollar of contingency to a specific, named risk. If a reserve amount cannot be traced back to
a register line — a probability, an impact, a response — it is not contingency in any defensible sense; it
is either an unrecorded padding of an estimate (a pitfall below) or it genuinely belongs in management
reserve because it responds to something the register does not cover. This traceability is what lets a
professional answer, at any point in the project, "what specifically is this money for, and who decided we
needed it?" — the exact question Contractor A could not answer in KA 3.1's mini-case.

**Quick reference — contingency reserve vs management reserve, by governance role.**

| | Contingency reserve | Management reserve |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | Known-unknowns: risks identified, assessed and retained in the register | Unknown-unknowns: unforeseeable events, or approved scope growth |
| Sits | Inside the cost baseline (BAC) | Outside the cost baseline |
| Sized by | The risk register (12.3.2): deterministic %, expected value, or probabilistic analysis | Sponsor/PMO policy judgement, typically a % of BAC (KA 3.1 §3.1.6) |
| Drawn down by | Project controls professional/PM, within delegated authority, when the *named* risk occurs | Sponsor/steering committee, via formal, approved baseline change |
| Traceable to | A specific risk-register line | A documented, approved change record |
| Typical trigger | A pre-identified risk in the register materialises | An unforeseen event, approved scope growth, or contingency exhaustion (12.3.3) |

**Example 12.3.1 — the reserves this KA will build.** Pipeline Segment 4B's approved cost baseline, already
reported as BAC $600,000 in KA 6.1, is not a single undifferentiated figure: 12.3.2 shows it is built from a
base estimate of $540,000 plus a $60,000 contingency reserve sized from a three-line risk register. A further
$30,000 of management reserve — set by the sponsor at 5.0% of BAC — sits outside that $600,000, giving a
total project budget of $630,000. Every EVM calculation reported in KA 6.1–6.3 (PV, EV, AC, CPI, SPI, EAC,
VAC) uses the $600,000 BAC figure, never the $630,000 total project budget — the same convention established
in KA 3.1 §3.1.5, restated here because a Domain 12 reader arriving without Domain 3 or 6 in hand must not
confuse the two.

**Mini-case — a provision is not a contingency reserve.** Meridian Rail Contracting LLC's rail-signalling
contract (KA 1.4) recognises a workmanship-warranty **provision** under IAS 37 once handover triggers a
present obligation — a recognised liability on the statement of financial position, measured at the expected
value of future defect-rectification cost. A project's contingency reserve looks superficially similar
(both use expected-value-style thinking) but serves an entirely different purpose: contingency is a
*budgeting and cost-control* allowance, held to fund identified risks *before* they crystallise into an
obligation, and it is not itself a balance-sheet liability. Once a contingency-funded risk occurs and a
genuine present obligation results (for example, a warranty claim), *that* obligation may separately need to
be assessed for an IAS 37 provision (cross-reference KA 1.4) — the contingency reserve is simply where the
cash to settle it was expected to come from. Treating "we hold a contingency reserve for this" as
interchangeable with "we have provided for this in the accounts" is a common, avoidable confusion between a
project-controls concept and a financial-reporting one.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Confusing contingency reserve with an IAS 37 provision (mini-case above). (2)
Double-counting a risk — pricing it once inside a padded work-package estimate and again inside the formal
contingency line (cross-reference KA 3.1 §3.1.6, pitfall 4; not repeated here). (3) Sizing contingency before
the risk register (KA 12.2) is materially complete, producing a reserve untethered to any actual identified
risk. (4) Treating contingency as available for *any* cost pressure rather than the specific risks it was
sized against — developed fully as the "slush fund" pitfall in 12.3.3.

**AI in this topic.** Risk-register and budgeting tools can be configured to flag any contingency line that
has no linked risk-register entry, or any risk-register entry with no funded response — a genuinely useful
traceability control at the volume of a large programme register (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). Such a
tool cannot judge *whether* a specific item is properly a known, retained risk (contingency) or should
instead be escalated as an unknown-unknown (management reserve); that classification is a professional and
PMO judgement, made once and then defended through the register, not inferred by a model from pattern-
matching alone.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 §3.1.1, §3.1.5–3.1.6 (the budgeting hierarchy and full contingency/management-
reserve definitions — do not duplicate); KA 1.4 (IAS 37 provisions); KA 12.1 (ISO 31000, risk appetite); KA
12.2 (the risk-identification and quantitative-analysis process this KA assumes is complete); KA 6.1 (BAC).

### 12.3.2 Sizing contingency: deterministic, expected-value and probabilistic methods

**Definition & purpose.** AACE RP 40R-08 describes a spectrum of contingency-sizing methods running from a
simple percentage allowance through expected-value analysis to full probabilistic (Monte Carlo-type)
simulation, each more analytically demanding than the last, and each appropriate to a different stage of
project definition and data availability. This topic works through all three on the same risk register so
their outputs can be compared directly.

**The underlying principle.** The three methods answer three different questions. The **deterministic**
method asks "what has a comparable project historically needed?" — fast, requires no register, but carries
no link to *this* project's actual risk exposure. The **expected-value** method asks "what is the
probability-weighted average cost this specific, quantified register implies?" — traceable and defensible,
but a mean is not a stated level of confidence: funding to the expected value gives no assurance about how
often that funding will actually prove sufficient. The **probabilistic** method asks "at what funding level
can we state a chosen confidence that cost will not be exceeded?" — the only one of the three that produces
a genuine confidence statement (a "P-value," e.g. P80: an 80% likelihood the funded amount will not be
exceeded), because it works with the full shape of the possible-outcome distribution, not just its mean.

**Formula(e).**

*Deterministic:* `Contingency = x% × Base estimate`, where x is a policy rate (KA 3.1 §3.1.6).

*Expected value:* `Contingency = Σ (Probability_i × Impact_i)`, summed across risks i in the register.

*Probabilistic (P-value):* the contingency value `c` such that `Cumulative probability [Total risk cost ≤ c]
≥ P`, read from the full probability distribution of combined risk-cost outcomes (in practice, generated by
Monte Carlo simulation across thousands of iterations; illustrated below by direct enumeration for a small
register).

**Example 12.3.2 — sizing Pipeline Segment 4B's contingency, all three methods.**

*Scenario.* KA 12.2's completed risk register for Pipeline Segment 4B (base estimate $540,000) identifies
three retained risks:

| Risk | Affected work package (KA 6.1.4) | Probability | Impact if it occurs ($) | Expected value ($) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 — Adverse ground conditions | WP3 Trenching & earthworks | 30.0% | 100,000 | 30,000 |
| R2 — Weld defect rework | WP4 Pipe-laying & welding | 40.0% | 50,000 | 20,000 |
| R3 — Subcontractor mobilisation delay | WP2 Pipe & valve procurement | 25.0% | 40,000 | 10,000 |
| **Total** | | | | **60,000** |

The organisation's estimating policy for this project stage applies a flat 10.0% contingency rate to the
base estimate as a cross-check.

*Required.* (a) Contingency by the expected-value method; (b) contingency by the deterministic method; (c)
contingency at an 80% confidence level (P80), using a simplified probabilistic analysis that treats the
three risks as independent and each either fully occurs or does not.

*Working.*

(a) *Expected value:* `Contingency = (0.30 × 100,000) + (0.40 × 50,000) + (0.25 × 40,000) = 30,000 + 20,000 +
10,000 = 60,000`

(b) *Deterministic:* `Contingency = 10.0% × 540,000 = 54,000`

(c) *Probabilistic.* With three independent risks, there are 2³ = 8 possible combined outcomes. Each
outcome's probability is the product of each risk's individual probability (of occurring or not); each
outcome's cost is the sum of the impacts of only the risks that occur in it:

| Risks occurring | Combined cost ($) | Probability | Cumulative probability |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | 0 | 0.70 × 0.60 × 0.75 = 0.315 | 0.315 |
| R3 only | 40,000 | 0.70 × 0.60 × 0.25 = 0.105 | 0.420 |
| R2 only | 50,000 | 0.70 × 0.40 × 0.75 = 0.210 | 0.630 |
| R2 + R3 | 90,000 | 0.70 × 0.40 × 0.25 = 0.070 | 0.700 |
| R1 only | 100,000 | 0.30 × 0.60 × 0.75 = 0.135 | 0.835 |
| R1 + R3 | 140,000 | 0.30 × 0.60 × 0.25 = 0.045 | 0.880 |
| R1 + R2 | 150,000 | 0.30 × 0.40 × 0.75 = 0.090 | 0.970 |
| R1 + R2 + R3 | 190,000 | 0.30 × 0.40 × 0.25 = 0.030 | 1.000 |

Sorting by cost, the cumulative probability first reaches 80% at the **$100,000** outcome (cumulative
83.5%, against 70.0% at the $90,000 outcome) — so **P80 contingency = $100,000**. As a check, the
probability-weighted mean of this same eight-outcome table is `(0×0.315) + (40,000×0.105) + (50,000×0.210) +
(90,000×0.070) + (100,000×0.135) + (140,000×0.045) + (150,000×0.090) + (190,000×0.030) = 0 + 4,200 + 10,500 +
6,300 + 13,500 + 6,300 + 13,500 + 5,700 = 60,000`, exactly matching (a) — confirming the enumeration is
consistent with the simple expected-value sum, as it must be, since expectation is additive regardless of
correlation.

*Answer.* **Deterministic contingency = $54,000; expected-value contingency = $60,000; P80 (probabilistic)
contingency = $100,000.** The median (P50) outcome of the same distribution is $50,000 — below the $60,000
expected value, because the outcome distribution is right-skewed (a few high-cost combined-risk scenarios
pull the mean above the middle outcome).

*Interpretation.* The organisation adopts the **expected-value figure, $60,000**, as the contingency
baselined into BAC — it reconciles exactly to the $600,000 BAC already established in KA 6.1 ($540,000 base
+ $60,000 contingency) and is the figure with a direct, auditable link to the register. The deterministic
figure ($54,000, 10.0% lower) is used only as a policy cross-check, confirming the register-based figure is
in a reasonable range rather than an outlier. The P80 figure ($100,000, 66.7% higher than the expected value)
is disclosed separately to the sponsor as risk-appetite context — it answers "how much would we need to be
80% confident of not overrunning against this register?" and is one legitimate input to how the sponsor sets
management reserve (12.3.4), but it is not itself baselined as contingency here, precisely because doing so
would silently inflate BAC beyond what KA 6.1's Pipeline Segment 4B figures already report.

**Mini-case — why the discrete method is a simplification, honestly stated.** The eight-scenario enumeration
above is mathematically exact for the assumptions given, but those assumptions are themselves a
simplification of real probabilistic risk analysis: it treats each risk's impact as a single fixed number
(occurs at exactly $100,000 or not at all) rather than a *range* (e.g., a triangular distribution between a
low, most-likely and high cost), and it assumes the three risks are fully independent, which is often not
true in practice — adverse ground conditions and weld defects, for instance, could plausibly be correlated
through a shared cause such as poor site supervision. Genuine Monte Carlo simulation samples continuous
impact ranges and can model such correlations across thousands of iterations, producing a smoother
probability curve than the eight-point step function above; the enumeration here is a hand-reproducible
teaching approximation of the same underlying logic, not a substitute for a properly specified simulation on
a real project.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating a deterministic percentage as sufficient once a quantified register exists
— it is a cross-check, not a substitute (§3.1.2's Class 5/4 caution about premature precision applies
equally here). (2) Reporting the expected value as if it were a stated confidence level — it is a mean, with
no probability statement attached to it at all. (3) Running a probabilistic analysis on single-point impact
estimates without disclosing that simplification (the mini-case above). (4) Ignoring correlation between
risks, which — where it exists — typically widens the true probability distribution beyond what an
independence assumption produces.

**AI in this topic.** Monte Carlo simulation software, often integrated into scheduling or cost-estimating
platforms, can sample continuous probability distributions across a large risk register, model correlations
between risks, and run thousands of iterations in seconds — well beyond what the eight-scenario hand
calculation above can do, and a genuine analytical advance for programme-scale registers (cross-reference KA
12.2, KA 13.5). These tools cannot supply the underlying probability, impact-range or correlation
assumptions themselves; those inputs still come from engineering and commercial judgement, and a simulation
built on poorly estimated inputs produces a precise-looking but ungrounded output — "garbage in, garbage
out" applies to contingency simulation exactly as it does to any other model (cross-reference KA 13.2). The
choice of which confidence level (P50, P80, or another) to fund to also remains a risk-appetite decision for
the sponsor, not an output the software selects on its own.

### 12.3.3 Contingency draw-down: monitoring, curves and governance

**Definition & purpose.** A **contingency drawdown curve** tracks the reserve's remaining balance over the
project's duration, plotted against a planned reference profile — the risk-reserve equivalent of the cost
S-curve used for PV in KA 6.1. Its purpose is early warning: comparing the *actual* rate at which
contingency is being consumed against the *planned* rate flags whether risk realisation is tracking as
expected or running materially ahead of it, well before the reserve is fully exhausted.

**The underlying principle.** Contingency is not meant to be spent evenly, nor is it meant to survive
untouched to project close — it is meant to be drawn down specifically and only when the named risks it was
sized against actually occur, and to be monitored against a plan so any unusual acceleration triggers
review rather than surprise. Governance therefore rests on two mechanisms: (1) a **delegated authority**
that lets the project controls professional or PM approve a drawdown *without* new sponsor sign-off, provided
it is against a specific, named, already-budgeted risk; and (2) an **escalation trigger** — typically,
contingency exhaustion, or a still-open risk with no remaining reserve behind it — that forces a formal
request into management reserve, following the same change-control discipline KA 3.1 §3.1.6 established for
any other access to that fund.

**Formula.** `Contingency remaining (t) = Contingency reserve − Σ (drawdowns to date, period 1 → t)`. A
planned reference profile is commonly modelled as declining in proportion to project physical progress (e.g.
% of PV complete, KA 6.1), reflecting that risk exposure typically crystallises — resolves either into an
occurrence or a close-out — as the associated scope is executed: `Planned remaining (t) = Contingency
reserve × (1 − % complete (t))`.

**Example 12.3.3 — drawing down Pipeline Segment 4B's contingency through execution.**

*Scenario.* Continuing Example 12.3.2: contingency reserve = $60,000, distributed against the three named
risks (R1 $30,000, R2 $20,000, R3 $10,000), monitored in aggregate rather than ring-fenced per risk, so that
unused allocation against one risk remains available to a different risk if it is exceeded. Using KA 6.1's
% complete profile (Month 1: 10.0%; Month 2: 25.0%; Month 3: 45.0%; Month 4: 70.0%; Month 5: 90.0%; Month 6:
100.0%), the planned reference balance is `60,000 × (1 − % complete)`.

At Month 1, R3 (subcontractor mobilisation delay) occurs; the actual cost is $23,000 (below its $40,000
assessed impact). At Month 4, R1 (adverse ground conditions) occurs; the actual remediation cost is $42,000
(below its $100,000 assessed impact). R2 (weld defect rework) never occurs — it is avoided through additional
quality-assurance inspection during welding.

*Required.* The planned and actual contingency balance at each month, and the governance action required at
Month 4.

*Working.*

`Planned(1) = 60,000 × (1 − 0.10) = 54,000`; `Planned(4) = 60,000 × (1 − 0.70) = 18,000`

Month 1: `Actual = 60,000 − 23,000 = 37,000` — already below the $54,000 planned reference, an early-warning
signal.

Month 4: draw required = $42,000; contingency available before this draw = $37,000. `Shortfall = 42,000 −
37,000 = 5,000.` Because R1 is a pre-identified, quantified, in-scope risk, the draw itself needs no new
sponsor authorisation up to the available balance — but the $5,000 that exceeds it does, because contingency
is now exhausted. Following formal change control, $5,000 is transferred from management reserve into
contingency (mechanically, into the cost baseline, since contingency sits inside BAC):

`New contingency ceiling = 60,000 + 5,000 = 65,000`
`Total drawn = 23,000 + 42,000 = 65,000`
`Contingency remaining = 65,000 − 65,000 = 0`
`New BAC = 600,000 + 5,000 = 605,000`
`Management reserve remaining = 30,000 − 5,000 = 25,000`
`Check: New BAC + Management reserve remaining = 605,000 + 25,000 = 630,000 = Total project budget
(unchanged)` ✓

*Answer.* **After both events: Cost baseline (BAC) = $605,000; Contingency remaining = $0; Management
reserve remaining = $25,000; Total project budget = $630,000 (unchanged throughout).**

*Interpretation.* Two lessons follow the same shape as KA 3.1 §3.1.6's mini-case, extended to a case Domain
3 did not cover: a contingency draw against a named, in-scope risk does not by itself change BAC — it is an
internal reallocation within a fund the risk already belonged to (as at Month 1). But once that fund is
*exhausted* and a further named risk still needs funding, replenishing it *does* raise BAC — because the
formal transfer from management reserve is, mechanically, exactly the same baseline-change event as funding
approved scope growth, even though the underlying cause here (an underfunded but correctly identified risk)
is different in kind from new scope. At project close, R2 never materialises, so no further draw occurs
against it — but because R1 and R3 together already consumed the full $65,000 pool, there is no unused
contingency to release at closeout, unlike KA 3.1's Northgate mini-case where a risk that did not occur left
a clean, releasable balance. Total contingency utilisation on Pipeline Segment 4B is $65,000 against an
original $60,000 allocation — 108.3% of the baselined reserve.

**Mini-case — why aggregate monitoring, not per-risk ring-fencing, avoided a premature escalation.** Had the
$10,000 allocated specifically against R3 been ring-fenced rather than pooled, the $23,000 Month-1 draw would
have shown a $13,000 deficit against R3's own allocation immediately — before R1's larger, later risk had
even been tested — forcing an escalation to management reserve in Month 1 that the pooled, aggregate approach
avoided entirely (the pool still had $37,000 available after that draw). Rigid ring-fencing of contingency
per named risk is simpler to explain but can force exactly this kind of artificial, premature escalation
whenever one risk's actual cost differs from its assessed impact — which, as both realised risks in this
example show, is the normal case, not the exception. Monitoring contingency at the aggregate project (or
major risk-category) level, while still requiring that every draw be traceable to a *specific* named risk
(12.3.1), captures the benefit of pooling without losing the traceability that keeps contingency defensible.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Treating contingency as a general-purpose **slush fund** for any cost pressure,
rather than restricting draws to the specific named risks it was sized against — the single most damaging
misuse of contingency, because it destroys the traceability that makes the reserve defensible and can mask a
genuine underlying overrun as a routine, "already funded" event. (2) Not tracking drawdown against a planned
profile at all, so depletion is discovered only when a request for funds is refused — the Month-1 early
warning in the worked example above is exactly the signal this practice is meant to surface in time. (3)
Informally "borrowing" from management reserve to cover a contingency shortfall without the formal change
approval the transfer requires, defeating the sponsor-level control the two-tier structure exists to provide.
(4) Rigid per-risk ring-fencing that manufactures premature escalations (the mini-case above).

**AI in this topic.** Dashboard and business-intelligence tools can compare actual contingency drawdown
against the planned reference profile automatically, across every control account or project in a
portfolio, and flag divergence — such as the Month-1 gap in the worked example — well before a monthly
reporting cycle would surface it (cross-reference KA 4.3, KA 4.4, KA 13.5), a genuine, low-risk automation
given the arithmetic involved is fully deterministic. It cannot determine whether a specific draw request is
a legitimate realisation of a named register risk or an attempt to use contingency as an unauthorised slush
fund for an unrelated overrun; that determination requires checking the request against the actual register
entry, which remains the professional's responsibility before any drawdown is approved.

### 12.3.4 Reserves in the budget hierarchy: control account → project budget → management reserve

**Definition & purpose.** KA 3.1 §3.1.1 built the budgeting hierarchy from the top down (base estimate →
contingency → cost baseline → management reserve → total project budget). This topic builds the same
structure from the bottom up — from the **control accounts** that KA 1.5 and KA 5.3 (once written) define as
the level at which cost, schedule and scope are integrated — because that is where contingency is actually
allocated and, in KA 6.1's EVM system, where earned value is actually measured.

**The underlying principle.** Contingency can be held in one of two ways once sized (12.3.2). **Distributed
contingency** is allocated to the specific control account whose scope the underlying risk relates to — the
approach used throughout this KA, since each of Pipeline Segment 4B's three risks maps cleanly to a single
work package. **Undistributed contingency** is instead held centrally, at project level, unassigned to any
single control account — typically used for risks that are genuinely cross-cutting (affecting several
control accounts at once) or not yet clearly tied to one. Whichever approach is used, the sum of every
control account's budget (base scope plus any distributed contingency) plus any undistributed contingency
must equal the cost baseline (BAC) exactly; BAC plus management reserve must equal the total project budget
exactly — the same reconciliation discipline KA 3.1 §3.1.5 applied top-down.

**Formula.** `BAC = Σ (control-account budgets, each = base scope + distributed contingency) + Undistributed
contingency`. `Total project budget = BAC + Management reserve.`

**Example 12.3.4 — distributing Pipeline Segment 4B's contingency across its control accounts.**

*Scenario.* The $60,000 contingency sized in Example 12.3.2 is fully distributed to the three work packages
whose risk it addresses; KA 6.1.4's five work packages, and their totals, are unchanged.

| Work package (control account) | Base scope ($) | Distributed contingency ($) | Total (= KA 6.1.4 budget, $) |
|---|---|---|---|
| WP1 — Design & engineering | 80,000 | 0 | 80,000 |
| WP2 — Pipe & valve procurement | 140,000 | 10,000 (R3) | 150,000 |
| WP3 — Trenching & earthworks | 170,000 | 30,000 (R1) | 200,000 |
| WP4 — Pipe-laying & welding | 100,000 | 20,000 (R2) | 120,000 |
| WP5 — Site supervision (LOE) | 50,000 | 0 | 50,000 |
| **Total (= cost baseline, BAC)** | **540,000** | **60,000** | **600,000** |

*Required.* Confirm the control-account roll-up reconciles to BAC, and build the total project budget.

*Working.*
`Σ base scope = 80,000 + 140,000 + 170,000 + 100,000 + 50,000 = 540,000`
`Σ distributed contingency = 0 + 10,000 + 30,000 + 20,000 + 0 = 60,000`
`BAC = 540,000 + 60,000 = 600,000` ✓ (matches KA 6.1's Pipeline Segment 4B BAC exactly)
`Total project budget = BAC + Management reserve = 600,000 + 30,000 = 630,000`

*Answer.* **BAC = $600,000 (SAR 2,250,000); Total project budget = $630,000 (SAR 2,362,500).**

*Interpretation.* This table is the same $600,000 KA 6.1 has used throughout, now shown built from thirteen
traceable figures — five base-scope amounts and three distributed-contingency amounts — rather than asserted
as a single total, exactly the level of traceability KA 3.1 §3.1.3's bottom-up mini-case expected of a base
estimate. Note that WP1 (design, already complete by KA 6.1.4's Month-3 status) and WP5 (supervision,
measured by Level of Effort) carry no contingency at all — a deliberate, risk-based allocation, not an
oversight. Spreading contingency evenly across every control account regardless of its actual risk exposure,
by contrast, would fund WP1 and WP5 for risks that do not apply to them while potentially underfunding WP3,
which carries the largest single risk.

**Mini-case — the undistributed alternative.** Had the organisation instead judged that $15,000 of R1's
ground-conditions exposure was not clearly confined to WP3 alone (for example, because it could also delay
WP4's mobilisation), it could have held that $15,000 as **undistributed** contingency at project level rather
than folding it into WP3's control-account budget. WP3 would then show a distributed contingency of only
$15,000 (base $170,000 + distributed $15,000 = $185,000), with the remaining $15,000 held centrally and
reported as a separate "Undistributed contingency" line. The two views reconcile to the identical $200,000
WP3 total and $600,000 BAC either way ($185,000 + $15,000 = $200,000); the choice affects *where* the reserve
is visible and who can authorise its release to a control account, not the total amount funded.

**Common pitfalls.** (1) Allocating contingency evenly across every control account regardless of actual risk
exposure (this topic's worked example). (2) Once distributed, a control-account owner treating their share of
contingency as generally available working capital for their account rather than reserved for the specific
named risk it was allocated against — a control-account-level version of the slush-fund pitfall in 12.3.3.
(3) Reporting the total project budget ($630,000) where the BAC ($600,000) is required — the same confusion
KA 3.1 §3.1.5 flagged, restated here because it recurs whenever reserves are shown at the control-account
level and a reader loses track of which subtotal they are looking at.

**AI in this topic.** ERP and EPC-suite platforms can automatically map identified risks to the control
accounts their scope affects and propose a distributed-versus-undistributed split (cross-reference KA 1.5,
KA 13.4). They cannot decide the right split policy for a risk that genuinely spans several control accounts,
nor detect that a control-account owner is quietly treating distributed contingency as general funding rather
than a risk-specific reserve — both remain matters for the professional and PMO to review and, where
necessary, correct.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1 §3.1.1, §3.1.5 (the top-down hierarchy this topic mirrors from the bottom up);
KA 1.5, KA 5.3 (control accounts and cost coding, once written); KA 6.1 (BAC and control-account-level EV
measurement); §12.3.1 (traceability of every contingency dollar to a named risk).

### Key terms

- **Contingency reserve** — the budgeted allowance, inside the cost baseline, for identified, quantified
  risks retained in the project risk register; drawn down when the specific named risk occurs.
- **Management reserve** — the budgeted allowance, outside the cost baseline, for unforeseeable events or
  approved scope growth; accessible only through formal, approved baseline change.
- **Known-unknowns / unknown-unknowns** — risks the register has identified and quantified (contingency)
  versus risks or events it could not reasonably have listed (management reserve).
- **Deterministic (percentage) contingency method** — `x% × Base estimate`; fast, policy-based, not
  register-specific.
- **Expected-value contingency method** — `Σ (Probability_i × Impact_i)` across the risk register; a
  probability-weighted mean, with no stated confidence level.
- **Probabilistic (Monte Carlo) contingency method** — sizing contingency to a stated confidence/percentile
  (a "P-value," e.g. P50, P80) read from the full distribution of possible combined-risk-cost outcomes.
- **P50 / P80 (confidence level)** — the funding level at which cumulative probability of not being exceeded
  reaches 50% / 80% respectively; P80 is generally higher than the expected value for a right-skewed risk
  distribution.
- **Contingency drawdown curve** — the plotted balance of contingency remaining over time, compared against
  a planned reference profile, used as an early-warning governance tool.
- **Distributed contingency** — contingency allocated to the specific control account whose risk it responds
  to.
- **Undistributed contingency** — contingency held centrally at project level, not assigned to a single
  control account.
- **Contingency exhaustion / escalation** — the point at which a further named, in-scope risk requires
  funding beyond the remaining contingency, triggering a formal transfer from management reserve.
- **Contingency as a slush fund (pitfall)** — the misuse of contingency to fund cost pressure unrelated to
  the specific named risks it was sized against.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 12.3.1 [topic 12.3.1, level: recall]**
Which statement correctly distinguishes contingency reserve from management reserve?
A. Contingency reserve is always larger than management reserve
B. Contingency reserve funds known, identified risks and sits inside the cost baseline; management reserve
   funds unforeseeable events and sits outside it ✔
C. Management reserve can be drawn down by the project controls professional without formal approval, while
   contingency reserve always requires sponsor sign-off
D. Contingency reserve and management reserve are two names for the same fund
*Rationale:* B is the governing distinction (12.3.1, KA 3.1 §3.1.6): known-unknowns/inside-baseline versus
unknown-unknowns/outside-baseline, with correspondingly delegated versus formal authority. A is not
necessarily true — relative size depends on project risk profile and sponsor policy. C reverses the
authority levels. D collapses a distinction the whole KA depends on.

**MCQ 12.3.2 [topic 12.3.2, level: application — numerical]**
A risk register contains two risks: Risk A, probability 20%, impact if it occurs $250,000; Risk B,
probability 50%, impact if it occurs $80,000. What is the contingency reserve using the expected-value
method?
A. $40,000
B. $90,000 ✔
C. $130,000
D. $330,000
*Rationale:* `(0.20 × 250,000) + (0.50 × 80,000) = 50,000 + 40,000 = 90,000`. A takes only Risk B's expected
value; C wrongly halves Risk A's impact; D sums the two raw impacts without weighting by probability.

**MCQ 12.3.3 [topic 12.3.2, level: analysis]**
A project's risk register produces an expected-value contingency of $60,000 and a Monte Carlo-derived P80
contingency of $100,000 on the same register. Which statement best explains why the two figures differ?
A. The P80 figure must be a calculation error, since it does not match the expected value
B. The expected value is a probability-weighted mean with no stated confidence level, while P80 states the
   funding level at which there is an 80% probability of not being exceeded — for a right-skewed
   distribution, P80 is typically higher than the mean ✔
C. P80 is always exactly the sum of the highest-impact risks in the register
D. The two figures should always be identical; a difference indicates the register is incomplete
*Rationale:* B correctly distinguishes a mean (no confidence statement) from a stated percentile (12.3.2).
A and D wrongly assume the two methods should agree; C misdescribes how a percentile is derived from a full
outcome distribution, not simply the largest impacts.

**MCQ 12.3.4 [topic 12.3.3, level: application — numerical]**
A project's contingency reserve is $60,000. Two pre-identified risks subsequently occur, with actual costs of
$23,000 and $42,000 respectively, drawn from the same pooled contingency. What amount, if any, must be
formally escalated to management reserve?
A. $0 — the draws are within the original reserve
B. $5,000 ✔
C. $23,000
D. $65,000
*Rationale:* Total drawn = `23,000 + 42,000 = 65,000`; against a $60,000 reserve, the shortfall is `65,000 −
60,000 = 5,000` (12.3.3, Example 12.3.3). A ignores that the reserve has been exceeded; C mistakes the first
draw for the shortfall; D mistakes the total drawn for the amount requiring escalation.

**MCQ 12.3.5 [topic 12.3.4, level: recall]**
In the budget hierarchy from control account to total project budget, where does management reserve sit?
A. Inside a specific control account, alongside distributed contingency
B. Inside the cost baseline (BAC), but outside any single control account
C. Outside the cost baseline (BAC) entirely, added on top of it to form the total project budget ✔
D. It replaces contingency reserve once contingency is exhausted
*Rationale:* C is the governing convention used throughout Domains 3, 6 and 12: `Total project budget = BAC +
Management reserve` (12.3.4). A and B both wrongly place management reserve inside the cost baseline; D
misdescribes escalation (12.3.3) — a shortfall triggers a *transfer* from management reserve into
contingency/BAC, it does not make management reserve "become" contingency generally.

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using Pipeline Segment 4B's risk register (Example 12.3.2), explain in your own words why the
   deterministic (10.0%), expected-value and P80 contingency figures — $54,000, $60,000 and $100,000 — are
   all "correct," and state which one was actually baselined into BAC, and why.
2. A project has a base estimate of $900,000. A risk register produces an expected-value contingency of
   $81,000. Management reserve is set at 4.0% of the resulting cost baseline. Calculate the cost baseline
   (BAC) and the total project budget.
3. Explain why, in Example 12.3.3, the $23,000 draw against contingency at Month 1 required no new sponsor
   approval, while the $5,000 shortfall identified at Month 4 did.
4. A colleague proposes ring-fencing each control account's distributed contingency so it can only be used
   against the specific risk it was allocated to. Using the mini-case in 12.3.3, explain one advantage and
   one disadvantage of this approach compared with monitoring contingency in aggregate.

**Answers**

1. Each figure answers a different question from the same register: the deterministic 10.0% ($54,000) is a
   fast, policy-based cross-check with no direct link to the specific risks identified; the expected value
   ($60,000) is the probability-weighted average cost the register implies, fully traceable to the three
   named risks, but carries no stated confidence level; the P80 figure ($100,000) states the funding level
   at which there is an 80% probability the register's combined risk cost will not be exceeded. The
   expected-value figure, $60,000, was the one baselined into BAC, because it reconciles exactly to the
   $600,000 BAC already established for Pipeline Segment 4B in KA 6.1 and is directly traceable to the risk
   register; the P80 figure was disclosed separately as risk-appetite context rather than baselined, since
   baselining it would have inflated BAC beyond the already-reported figure.
2. Cost baseline = `900,000 + 81,000 = 981,000`. Management reserve = `981,000 × 0.04 = 39,240`. Total
   project budget = `981,000 + 39,240 = 1,020,240`.
3. The $23,000 draw was against Risk R3, a specific, pre-identified, already-quantified risk fully covered
   by the available $60,000 contingency reserve — exactly the situation contingency exists to fund within
   the project controls professional's delegated authority, without renegotiating the budget (12.3.1). The
   $5,000 identified at Month 4, by contrast, exceeded the *remaining* contingency entirely — the pool was
   exhausted — so funding it required a formal transfer from management reserve into the cost baseline,
   which (unlike a routine contingency draw) changes BAC itself and therefore needs the same change-control
   authorisation as any other baseline change (12.3.3, KA 3.1 §3.1.6).
4. Advantage: ring-fencing makes it immediately visible, at the control-account level, exactly how much of
   the reserve tied to a specific risk remains, which can support very precise accountability for the owner
   of that risk. Disadvantage: because actual realised risk cost frequently differs from its originally
   assessed impact (as both R1 and R3 did in Example 12.3.3), rigid ring-fencing can force a control account
   into an apparent deficit — and a premature escalation to management reserve — even while the project's
   *total* contingency pool still has ample balance elsewhere, as the mini-case in 12.3.3 demonstrates.
