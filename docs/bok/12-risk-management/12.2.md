# Domain 12 — Risk Management for Project Controls

## KA 12.2 — Risk process

KA 12.1 established *why* a project controls professional manages risk — ISO 31000's principles, the
distinction between risk and uncertainty, and the idea of risk appetite and tolerance set by leadership.
This KA turns that framework into a repeatable **process**: a sequence of activities that takes a project
from "we don't yet know what could go wrong" to a funded, owned, monitored risk position. The process
described here follows the same logical sequence found in ISO 31000 (risk assessment — comprising
identification, analysis and evaluation — followed by risk treatment, and run inside an ongoing
monitoring-and-review loop) and in PMI's PMBOK Guide risk management process (Identify Risks; Perform
Qualitative Risk Analysis; Perform Quantitative Risk Analysis; Plan Risk Responses; Implement Risk
Responses; Monitor Risks). Both describe the same underlying discipline in slightly different language;
this KA uses that discipline, not either standard's exact process-group labels, as its organising spine.

The chapter carries a single running illustration — **Meridian Rail Contracting LLC's Pipeline Segment 4B**,
the same project used throughout Domain 6's earned value worked examples (BAC $600,000 / SAR 2,250,000 at
an illustrative SAR 3.75 = USD 1) — through identification, qualitative scoring, quantitative analysis
(expected monetary value, a decision tree, three-point estimates and a Monte Carlo simulation) and response
planning, before closing with monitoring and control. A central continuity thread runs through the chapter:
KA 3.1.6 already told the reader that Pipeline Segment 4B's $600,000 BAC embeds a risk-based contingency
"cross-reference KA 12.2 for the full quantitative risk-analysis treatment" — this KA is where that
promise is kept, showing exactly how the $600,000 figure was built up from a base estimate and a
risk-quantified contingency reserve.

### 12.2.1 The risk management process and the risk register

**Definition & purpose.** The risk management process is the structured, repeatable sequence of activities
by which a project identifies, analyses, responds to and monitors the threats and opportunities that could
affect its objectives. ISO 31000 frames this, at the project level, as an iterative cycle run inside a wider
governance framework (KA 12.1): risk assessment (identification → analysis → evaluation), risk treatment,
and continuous monitoring and review, with communication and recording running throughout. PMI's PMBOK Guide
describes a closely equivalent process for project risk management, moving from identification through
qualitative and quantitative analysis to response planning, response implementation and ongoing monitoring.
The two describe the same discipline; this KA follows that discipline topic by topic.

**The underlying principle.** No project can eliminate risk, and treating every identified risk with the
same depth of analysis would waste scarce controls effort on issues that do not warrant it. The process
therefore has a built-in **funnel**: identification casts a wide net (12.2.2); qualitative analysis quickly
screens and prioritises what has been found (12.2.3); only the risks that matter most proceed to the
heavier, more precise work of quantitative analysis (12.2.4–12.2.6); every risk — quantified or not — gets
a response strategy (12.2.7–12.2.8); and monitoring keeps the whole picture current as the project
progresses and new information arrives (12.2.9). Skipping a stage, or quantifying everything regardless of
materiality, is a common and costly misapplication of the process.

**The risk register.** The single artefact that threads through every stage is the **risk register**: a
living log of each identified risk, its category, probability, impact, score, owner, planned response and
current status. Every topic in this KA adds a column, or a row, to the same register for Pipeline Segment
4B, so that by 12.2.9 the reader has seen one complete, internally consistent worked risk register from
identification to monitoring — not a set of disconnected illustrations.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating "the risk register" as a one-off document produced once at project start-up, rather than a live
  artefact reassessed at every reporting cycle (12.2.9).
- Conflating the risk *management process* (this KA) with the risk *governance framework* — appetite,
  tolerance, roles and policy — which is set once, at a higher level, and covered in KA 12.1.
- Running quantitative analysis on every entry in the register regardless of materiality, which is both
  wasteful and, because it dilutes attention, often less rigorous where it matters most.

**AI in this topic.** Workflow and register-management tools can automate much of the process mechanics —
routing a newly logged risk to its likely owner, prompting a reassessment when a review date falls due,
and maintaining version history of the register (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). They cannot decide
whether a given risk has been adequately identified in the first place, nor substitute for the professional
judgement applied at each stage of the funnel described above; the register's completeness and accuracy
remain the accountable risk owner's responsibility, consistent with **"AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable."**

**Cross-references.** KA 12.1 (ISO 31000 principles, risk appetite and tolerance — the governance this
process operates within); KA 3.1 (the budgeting hierarchy and contingency reserve this KA's quantitative
output feeds); KA 12.3 (linking the quantified risk position to the budget in full); KA 8.4 (Monitoring &
Controlling within the wider project management lifecycle).

### 12.2.2 Risk identification: techniques and building the register

**Definition & purpose.** Risk identification is the systematic process of finding and describing the
threats and opportunities that could affect a project's objectives, and recording each as a distinct entry
in the risk register before any scoring or analysis is attempted. Because a risk that is never identified
can never be analysed, responded to or monitored, identification is the stage on which the entire rest of
the process depends — and it is deliberately run using **several different techniques**, because no single
technique reliably surfaces every category of risk on its own.

**The underlying principle.** Different techniques are good at surfacing different kinds of risk, because
each draws on a different source of insight:

- **Checklists** — a structured list of risk categories or historically common risk sources (drawn from a
  risk breakdown structure or lessons learned from comparable past projects), worked through systematically
  so that common, foreseeable risk types are not simply forgotten.
- **Interviews** — one-to-one or small-group conversations with stakeholders, subject-matter experts or
  site personnel who hold specific knowledge (a permitting authority's known turnaround times; a
  subcontractor's current workload) that a generic checklist could never surface.
- **Brainstorming** — a facilitated group session, deliberately drawing on diverse perspectives across
  disciplines (design, procurement, construction, commercial) to generate a wide set of candidate risks
  quickly, before any filtering is applied.
- **Assumptions analysis** — systematically testing the assumptions the project plan and estimate are built
  on (e.g. "the specialist welding subcontractor has adequate crew available when needed"); an assumption
  that later proves invalid is, by definition, a risk that identification should have captured.

**Mini-case — the Pipeline Segment 4B risk identification workshop.** Ahead of finalising the project
budget, Meridian's project controls team runs a half-day risk identification workshop combining all four
techniques. The workshop produces six candidate risks, each traceable to the technique that surfaced it:

| ID | Risk description | Category | Identified via |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Adverse ground conditions during trenching | Technical / geotechnical | Site-condition checklist |
| R2 | Third-party utility clash requiring design rework | Technical / interface | Design-review interview with the utility authority |
| R3 | Pipe/valve material price escalation | Commercial / market | Procurement team brainstorming |
| R4 | Weather-related delay costs | External / environmental | Historical-project checklist |
| R5 | Welding subcontractor resource shortage | Resourcing | Assumptions analysis (tested the "adequate crew available" assumption) |
| R6 | Permit / right-of-way delay from the local authority | External / regulatory | Stakeholder interview with the permitting authority |

*Interpretation.* Note how each technique contributed something the others would not have: the checklist
(R1, R4) drew on generic, historically common categories; the interviews (R2, R6) drew on specific,
current knowledge held by named stakeholders; brainstorming (R3) drew on cross-functional pattern-matching
in the room; and assumptions analysis (R5) deliberately interrogated a plan input that would otherwise have
gone unquestioned. This six-risk list is carried forward, unscored, into 12.2.3.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using only brainstorming (the most common technique in practice) and skipping checklists and assumptions
  analysis, which tends to surface only risks the room happens to think of, missing well-documented
  historical categories and untested plan assumptions.
- Recording a risk description too vaguely to be analysed or owned (e.g. "weather" rather than "weather-
  related delay costs to the trenching programme") — a poorly worded entry cannot be scored consistently at
  12.2.3.
- Confusing a risk (an uncertain event with a potential effect on objectives) with an *issue* (something
  that has already happened) or with a general project *concern* that has no discrete triggering event.

**AI in this topic.** GenAI tools can draft a first-pass checklist of risk categories from a description of
the project and its industry, and can summarise interview notes or workshop transcripts into candidate
register entries far faster than manual minute-taking (cross-reference KA 13.3, KA 13.4). Such a tool has no
access to the specific, current knowledge an interview or a genuine assumptions test surfaces — it cannot
know that *this* permitting authority is currently running six weeks behind, or that *this* subcontractor's
crew is already committed elsewhere. An AI-drafted candidate list is a starting point for the workshop, not
a substitute for running it with the people who hold that knowledge.

**Cross-references.** KA 12.1 (risk categories and the risk breakdown structure); KA 3.2 (estimating basis
and assumptions, a natural input to assumptions analysis); KA 12.2.3 (qualitative screening of this list);
KA 8.2 (Planning, where risk identification typically first occurs in the project lifecycle).

### 12.2.3 Qualitative risk analysis: probability–impact scoring and prioritisation

**Definition & purpose.** Qualitative risk analysis assesses each identified risk's **probability of
occurrence** and **impact if it occurs** on an ordinal (ranked) scale, multiplies the two into a risk score,
and uses that score to prioritise which risks warrant the heavier, more precise work of quantitative
analysis. It is deliberately fast and approximate — its job is triage, not final measurement.

**The underlying principle.** A large risk register cannot be quantified item by item without consuming far
more controls effort than the exercise is worth; qualitative analysis is the funnel that concentrates that
effort where it matters. Probability and impact are each scored on a simple ordinal scale (commonly 1–5),
multiplied to give a risk score, and plotted or tabulated on a **probability–impact (P–I) matrix** so that
high-priority risks are visually and numerically obvious.

**Formula.** `Risk score = Probability score × Impact score`, where each score is an ordinal value (1–5)
assigned from a pre-agreed banding table (below), not a raw probability or dollar figure — that translation
into monetary terms is reserved for quantitative analysis (12.2.4).

*Illustrative banding (fixed in advance, applied consistently across the register):*

| Score | Probability band | Impact band (cost, $) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ≤ 10% | < 10,000 |
| 2 | 11–25% | 10,000–25,000 |
| 3 | 26–40% | 25,001–50,000 |
| 4 | 41–60% | 50,001–100,000 |
| 5 | > 60% | > 100,000 |

**Example 12.2.1 — scoring and prioritising the Pipeline Segment 4B risk register.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's project controls team assesses the six risks identified in 12.2.2, estimating a
probability and a cost impact for each and converting both to the ordinal bands above.

*Required.* The risk score for each risk, and a prioritisation decision: which risks proceed to quantitative
analysis, and which remain on a qualitative watchlist.

*Working.*

| ID | Risk | Probability | P score | Impact ($) | I score | Risk score | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Adverse ground conditions | 30% | 3 | 70,000 | 4 | 12 | High — quantify |
| R2 | Utility clash rework | 20% | 2 | 60,000 | 4 | 8 | Medium-High — quantify |
| R3 | Material price escalation | 25% | 2 | 40,000 | 3 | 6 | Medium — quantify |
| R4 | Weather-related delay | 35% | 3 | 20,000 | 2 | 6 | Medium — quantify |
| R5 | Welding subcontractor shortage | 15% | 2 | 18,000 | 2 | 4 | Low-medium — watchlist |
| R6 | Permit/right-of-way delay | 8% | 1 | 45,000 | 3 | 3 | Low — watchlist |

*Answer.* Using a Low (1–4) / Medium (5–9) / High (10–25) heat-map banding, **R1 is High priority**, **R2 is
Medium-High**, **R3 and R4 are Medium**, and **R5 and R6 are Low**. The four risks scoring 6 or above
(**R1–R4**) proceed to full quantitative analysis (12.2.4–12.2.6); **R5 and R6** remain on the qualitative
watchlist, re-screened at every register review (12.2.9) rather than dropped.

*Interpretation.* Prioritisation is not a judgement that R5 and R6 do not matter — it is a proportionate
allocation of quantitative effort to the risks that most threaten the budget and schedule *today*. A
watchlist risk can be promoted to full quantification the moment new information changes its score (for
example, if the permitting authority's turnaround worsens materially).

**Common pitfalls.**
- Using a probability–impact score (an ordinal ranking) as if it were a monetary figure — a risk scoring 12
  is not "worth $12"; the score only orders risks relative to each other.
- Scoring impact on cost alone when a risk also has a material schedule, quality, safety or reputational
  impact — a full qualitative treatment scores each affected objective and takes the most severe.
- Letting the risk owner who benefits from a low score (e.g. a subcontract manager wanting a supplier risk
  minimised) also set that risk's probability and impact banding, without independent review.

**AI in this topic.** A model trained on a library of historical risk registers from comparable projects can
suggest a plausible probability band for a newly logged risk (cross-reference KA 13.5) — useful as a
starting anchor, particularly for a risk type the team has limited direct experience of. It cannot know the
site-specific or supplier-specific detail that should move that estimate up or down for *this* project, and
a probability suggested by pattern-matching against other projects is not a substitute for the assessing
team's own judgement, which remains accountable for the figure entered in the register.

**Cross-references.** KA 12.1 (risk appetite — the threshold that determines how conservative the
Low/Medium/High bands are set); KA 12.2.2 (the register this topic scores); KA 12.2.4–12.2.6 (quantitative
analysis of the four prioritised risks); KA 4.4 (visualisation practice, applied here to the heat map).

### 12.2.4 Quantitative risk analysis: expected monetary value and the risk-based contingency

**Definition & purpose.** Expected Monetary Value (EMV) analysis converts each quantified risk's
probability and cost impact into a single monetary figure — its **expected value** — and sums those figures
across the register to produce a statistically defensible basis for the project's **contingency reserve**
(introduced generally in KA 3.1.6, which named this exact method and cross-referred forward to this KA).
This is the first of the three quantitative techniques covered in this KA (decision trees, 12.2.5, and
Monte Carlo simulation, 12.2.6, follow).

**The underlying principle.** A risk's expected value is not a prediction of what will happen on this
specific project — a risk with a 30% probability either occurs (at its full impact) or it does not; there is
no world in which it "partially happens" at 30% of its impact. Expected value is instead a **portfolio
concept**: summed across many risks and, notionally, across many similar projects, the expected value is the
average cost the risk category would impose over the long run, and it is this long-run average that a
contingency reserve — itself a shared fund covering many risks, only some of which will occur — is
statistically sized to cover.

**Formula.** `EMV(risk i) = Probability_i × Impact_i` (in $); `Contingency (EMV method) = Σ EMV(risk i)`,
summed across the risks selected for quantification. This is the identical formula introduced in KA 3.1.6.

**Example 12.2.2 — sizing Pipeline Segment 4B's contingency from the quantified risk register.**

*Scenario.* Continuing Example 12.2.1, Meridian's project controls team calculates the expected monetary
value of each of the four prioritised risks (R1–R4):

| ID | Risk | Probability | Impact ($) | EMV = P × I ($) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Adverse ground conditions | 30% | 70,000 | 21,000 |
| R2 | Utility clash rework | 20% | 60,000 | 12,000 |
| R3 | Material price escalation | 25% | 40,000 | 10,000 |
| R4 | Weather-related delay | 35% | 20,000 | 7,000 |
| **Total** | | | | **50,000** |

*Required.* (a) The total EMV-based contingency reserve; (b) the base estimate and the ratio of contingency
to base estimate, reconciled against Pipeline Segment 4B's known BAC of $600,000 (KA 6.1).

*Working.*
(a) `Contingency = 21,000 + 12,000 + 10,000 + 7,000 = 50,000`
(b) Pipeline Segment 4B's cost baseline (BAC) is fixed at $600,000 throughout Domain 6's worked examples;
applying KA 3.1's hierarchy (`BAC = Base estimate + Contingency reserve`):
`Base estimate = BAC − Contingency = 600,000 − 50,000 = 550,000`
`Ratio of contingency to base estimate = 50,000 / 550,000 = 9.1%`
*Check:* `550,000 + 50,000 = 600,000 = BAC` ✓

*Answer.* **Contingency reserve = $50,000** (SAR 187,500 at the illustrative rate), built from a base
estimate of **$550,000**, a ratio of **9.1%** — reconciling exactly with the $600,000 BAC already
established for this project in KA 6.1.

*Interpretation.* This worked example completes the story KA 3.1.6 promised: Pipeline Segment 4B's $600,000
BAC was never an arbitrary round number — $550,000 of it is the cost of the defined scope with no risk
allowance, and $50,000 is a specific, risk-register-derived allowance for four named, quantified threats.
Because each dollar of contingency is traceable to a specific risk, it can be drawn down individually as
each risk resolves (as the Northgate mini-case in KA 3.1.6 demonstrates for a comparable project) and any
unused balance can be released with equal precision at project close.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Summing EMV across *all* identified risks (including the unquantified watchlist items, R5–R6) rather than
  only those carried forward from qualitative screening — this either double-counts effort or produces a
  false sense of precision for risks that were never rigorously assessed.
- Presenting a single EMV total as a guarantee that the project will not overrun by more than that amount —
  EMV is an average outcome across the portfolio of risks, not a worst-case ceiling (12.2.6 shows how a
  Monte Carlo simulation expresses that uncertainty as a range instead of one number).
- Sizing contingency from a flat percentage policy *and* an EMV build-up without reconciling the two —
  KA 3.1.6 recommends using one as a cross-check on the other, not silently picking whichever is larger.

**AI in this topic.** Spreadsheet and risk-register software can compute EMV automatically across a register
of any size, instantly re-totalling the contingency figure whenever a probability or impact is updated
(cross-reference KA 13.4) — a genuine, low-risk automation of otherwise repetitive arithmetic. It cannot
judge whether a given probability or impact estimate is itself realistic, nor decide which risks belong in
the quantified set versus the watchlist; those remain the assessing professional's calls, checked before the
contingency figure is reported or funded.

**Cross-references.** KA 3.1.1 (the budgeting hierarchy this contingency sits within); KA 3.1.6 (the general
EMV and percentage contingency-sizing methods, and the Northgate worked example this KA's method mirrors);
KA 6.1 (Pipeline Segment 4B's $600,000 BAC, reconciled here); KA 12.3 (linking the quantified risk position
to the budget in full, including drawdown mechanics).

### 12.2.5 Quantitative risk analysis: expected monetary value in a decision tree

**Definition & purpose.** A decision tree extends EMV analysis from scoring a *given* risk to choosing
**between response options** under uncertainty: it lays out a decision (a choice the project controls team
actually makes), the chance events that follow each option, and the cost or value of each resulting outcome,
then calculates the expected monetary value of each option so that the lower-EMV (or higher-value) choice
can be identified and recommended.

**The underlying principle.** A decision tree is read from right to left ("rolling back" the tree): at each
chance node, the expected value of that branch is the probability-weighted sum of its possible outcomes;
at each decision node, the option with the better EMV (here, the lower expected cost) is selected, and its
EMV becomes the value carried back to the point of decision. The technique makes explicit what an informal
judgement often leaves implicit: the cost of an option is not just its own upfront cost, but its upfront
cost *plus* the expected cost of what happens afterwards.

**Formula.** At a chance node: `EMV(node) = Σ (Probability_j × Value_j)`, summed across each possible
outcome j of that node. At a decision node: `EMV(decision) = Upfront cost of the option + EMV(chance node
following it)`; the option with the lowest total EMV (for a cost-minimisation decision) is preferred.

**Example 12.2.3 — should Meridian commission a targeted geotechnical survey?**

*Scenario.* R1 (adverse ground conditions, Example 12.2.1) is the highest-scored risk on Pipeline Segment
4B. Before finalising the response strategy for the highest-risk 300-metre sub-reach of the trenching
alignment, Meridian's project controls team evaluates a specific, tactical decision: commission a targeted
geotechnical survey of that sub-reach now, or proceed without one and respond to ground conditions as they
are encountered during trenching. (This tactical decision uses more granular, sub-reach-level cost data
gathered by the estimating team; it is a separate analysis from the register-level EMV in Example 12.2.2,
which prices the risk across the *whole* alignment for contingency-sizing purposes — the two must not be
added together.) The known facts:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Cost of targeted survey | $8,000 |
| Probability ground conditions are adverse in this sub-reach | 30% |
| If adverse, and survey was commissioned: pre-planned mitigation cost | $45,000 |
| If adverse, and no survey was commissioned: emergency mid-trenching mobilisation cost | $90,000 |
| If favourable (70%), either way | $0 additional cost |

*Required.* The EMV of each option (commission the survey / do not commission the survey), and a
recommendation.

*Working.*

*Option A — commission the survey.* The $8,000 survey fee is paid regardless of outcome; the chance node
that follows has two branches:
`EMV(chance node, Option A) = (0.30 × 45,000) + (0.70 × 0) = 13,500 + 0 = 13,500`
`EMV(Option A) = 8,000 + 13,500 = 21,500`

*Option B — do not commission the survey.* No upfront cost; the chance node that follows:
`EMV(chance node, Option B) = (0.30 × 90,000) + (0.70 × 0) = 27,000 + 0 = 27,000`
`EMV(Option B) = 0 + 27,000 = 27,000`

*Answer.* **EMV(Option A, commission the survey) = $21,500**; **EMV(Option B, do not commission the survey)
= $27,000**. Option A has the lower expected cost, by **$5,500**.

*Interpretation.* The survey is worth commissioning **in expected-value terms**: its $8,000 cost is more
than offset by the $45,000 (versus $90,000) difference in mitigation cost when conditions do turn out
adverse. Note what the analysis does *not* say: it does not guarantee a better outcome on this specific
project — if conditions turn out favourable (the 70% branch), the $8,000 survey fee was, with hindsight, an
unnecessary cost under either option. EMV compares options on their probability-weighted average outcome,
which is the defensible basis for a repeatable decision policy, not a promise about this one instance. A
risk-averse organisation might commission the survey even if its EMV were *not* the lower figure, purely to
reduce the range of possible outcomes (the variance) — a legitimate consideration EMV alone does not
capture (12.2.6 returns to this point).

branch (survey, $21,500) is highlighted and boxed, narration cue: "Roll back from the leaves. The cheapest
path to the decision node is the answer — but check what it assumes before you commit to it."

**Common pitfalls.**
- Comparing only the upfront costs of each option ($8,000 vs $0) without rolling back the chance nodes that
  follow — this is the single most common error in decision-tree analysis and produces exactly the wrong
  recommendation here.
- Treating the EMV recommendation as certain — a decision tree encodes the team's best current probability
  and cost estimates, which themselves carry uncertainty and should be sensitivity-tested, not treated as
  fixed inputs.
- Adding a decision-tree result to a register-level EMV figure (Example 12.2.2) as if they were additive —
  they analyse the same underlying risk at different levels of granularity and for different purposes
  (contingency-sizing versus tactical response choice) and must not be summed.

**AI in this topic.** Decision-analysis software can build and roll back a decision tree automatically once
probabilities and outcome values are entered, and can run a rapid sensitivity analysis showing how the
recommended option changes as key inputs (probability, mitigation cost) vary (cross-reference KA 13.5) — a
genuinely useful check on how robust the recommendation is. It cannot supply the probability and cost inputs
themselves; those come from site knowledge, market data and estimating judgement, and a tree built on
unverified inputs will confidently roll back to the wrong answer. The recommendation is signed off by the
professional who owns the risk, not generated and accepted automatically.

**Cross-references.** KA 12.2.4 (register-level EMV, the contingency-sizing use of the same underlying
technique); KA 12.2.7 (response planning, of which this decision is one worked instance); KA 3.2 (cost
estimating, supplying the mitigation and emergency-response cost inputs); KA 12.1 (risk appetite — how much
weight to give variance reduction beyond pure EMV).

### 12.2.6 Quantitative risk analysis: three-point estimates and Monte Carlo simulation

**Definition & purpose.** Where EMV and decision trees analyse risks one at a time (or one decision at a
time), Monte Carlo simulation analyses the **combined effect of many uncertain inputs at once**, producing
not a single forecast figure but a full probability distribution of possible total project cost (or
duration) outcomes. It is the quantitative technique named in ISO 31000 and widely used in AACE
International's and PMI's quantitative risk-analysis practice for major projects, and it is the technique
that generates the P50/P80-style outputs referenced throughout project controls literature.

**The underlying principle — three-point estimates as simulation inputs.** Monte Carlo simulation needs each
uncertain input expressed as a **probability distribution**, not a single number. The simplest and most
widely used way to build one from an estimator's judgement is the **three-point estimate**: an optimistic
value (O), a most-likely value (M) and a pessimistic value (P) for the same cost or duration element. The
PERT (Programme Evaluation and Review Technique) weighting is a common way to derive a single expected value
and a spread from the three points, and — more importantly for Monte Carlo — the three points also define
the shape of a distribution (commonly triangular or Beta/PERT-shaped) that can be sampled repeatedly.

**Formula(e).**
`PERT expected value: E = (O + 4M + P) / 6`
`PERT standard deviation (approx.): SD = (P − O) / 6`
where O, M and P are the optimistic, most-likely and pessimistic values for a single cost or duration
element, in consistent units ($ for cost, days/weeks for duration).

**Monte Carlo simulation, in principle.** The simulation software samples a value at random from each
uncertain input's distribution (each three-point-estimated cost or duration element in the model), combines
the sampled values using the same logic that would combine them deterministically (summing costs; for
schedule, propagating durations through the network logic and critical path, KA 10.2), and records the
resulting total. This is repeated many thousands of times (a typical run uses 5,000–10,000+ iterations).
Across all iterations, the recorded totals build up a **cumulative probability distribution** — conventionally
displayed as an S-curve — from which any percentile (P10, P50, P80, P90…) of the total outcome can be read
directly: the cost or date value below which that percentage of simulated iterations fell.

**Example 12.2.4a — a three-point cost estimate for the trenching work package (WP3).**

*Scenario.* WP3 (Trenching & earthworks), first introduced in KA 6.1.4, carries a deterministic budget of
$200,000 for 2,000 metres at $100/metre. Reflecting the ground-conditions uncertainty behind R1, the
estimating team develops a three-point cost estimate for the same work package: optimistic (favourable
ground throughout) $180,000; most likely (as-budgeted) $200,000; pessimistic (adverse ground across the full
reach) $260,000.

*Required.* The PERT expected value and standard deviation for WP3's cost.

*Working.*
`E = (180,000 + 4 × 200,000 + 260,000) / 6 = (180,000 + 800,000 + 260,000) / 6 = 1,240,000 / 6 = 206,667`
(rounded to the nearest dollar)
`SD = (260,000 − 180,000) / 6 = 80,000 / 6 = 13,333` (rounded to the nearest dollar)

*Answer.* **PERT expected value ≈ $206,667; standard deviation ≈ $13,333.**

*Interpretation.* The PERT expected value ($206,667) sits above the deterministic budget ($200,000) because
the pessimistic tail (up to $260,000) is wider than the optimistic tail (down to $180,000) — the estimate is
not symmetric, which is typical where the main driver of cost growth (adverse ground) has more room to make
things worse than better. This three-point estimate — one input among several — feeds the simulation in
Example 12.2.4b, rather than being used on its own to reset WP3's deterministic budget.

**Example 12.2.4b — an illustrative Monte Carlo simulation of Pipeline Segment 4B total cost.**

*Scenario.* Meridian's project controls team builds a cost-risk model combining the deterministic base
estimate ($550,000, Example 12.2.2), three-point cost ranges for each of the risk-affected elements
(including WP3's, above) and the register's four quantified risks (R1–R4), and runs a 10,000-iteration
Monte Carlo simulation of total project cost.

*Required.* An illustrative cumulative distribution of total cost, and the P50 and P80 values.

*Working/output (illustrative simulation result).*

| Cumulative probability | Total cost ($) |
|---|---|
| 2% | 540,000 |
| 10% (P10) | 565,000 |
| 25% | 580,000 |
| 50% (P50) | 600,000 |
| 68% | 620,000 |
| 80% (P80) | 645,000 |
| 90% (P90) | 672,000 |
| 97% | 700,000 |

*Answer.* **P50 (median) total cost ≈ $600,000; P80 total cost ≈ $645,000** — a spread of **$45,000**
between the two commonly reported confidence levels.

*Interpretation.* Two points matter for a project controls professional reading this output. First, the
P50 figure ($600,000) coincides closely with the deterministic EMV-based BAC calculated in Example 12.2.2 —
a feature of this particular, roughly symmetric combined distribution, not a general rule; the two figures
will not always align this closely on a real project. Second, and more importantly, **P50 is not automatically
"the" number to fund** — it is, by definition, the value the simulation shows a 50% chance of being exceeded.
An organisation with a lower risk appetite (KA 12.1) may set policy to fund contingency to **P80** instead
(here, $45,000 more than the EMV/P50 figure), accepting a higher funding level in exchange for a higher
confidence of not overrunning it. Reporting a Monte Carlo output as a single number, rather than the
percentile and the policy behind choosing it, discards exactly the information the simulation was run to
provide.

**A brief note on schedule Monte Carlo.** The identical technique applies to duration: each schedule
activity is given a three-point duration estimate, durations are sampled and propagated through the network
logic (KA 10.2's forward/backward pass, repeated per iteration), and the simulation produces a distribution
of possible **completion dates** rather than costs — the schedule-risk concept referenced briefly in
KA 10.3. Illustratively, for Pipeline Segment 4B's six-month baseline (KA 6.1, Fig 6.1.1), a schedule Monte
Carlo might show a P50 completion at essentially Month 6 and a P80 completion around three weeks later —
the schedule equivalent of the cost P50/P80 spread above, and the basis for schedule contingency (float
beyond the deterministic critical path) in the same way cost contingency is basis for the EMV figure above.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reporting a Monte Carlo P50 alone, without stating the confidence level, exactly as KA 6.3's caution
  against reporting a bare EAC applies equally here.
- Building three-point estimates only for the "big" risks already in the quantified register and ignoring
  smaller, more numerous uncertainties elsewhere in the estimate — their combined variance can still move
  the P50/P80 spread materially, even though no single one would score highly enough for individual EMV
  treatment.
- Treating simulation inputs (the three-point ranges) as if they were as reliable as the simulation's
  arithmetic — the simulation is only as good as the O/M/P judgements feeding it; "garbage in, garbage out"
  applies here exactly as it does to any other model.
- Correlating risks incorrectly (or not at all) — if two risk-affected elements are driven by the same
  underlying cause (e.g. two work packages both exposed to the same ground conditions), treating them as
  statistically independent in the model understates the width of the resulting distribution.

**AI in this topic.** Monte Carlo simulation is itself a long-established computational (not generative-AI)
technique, but modern risk-analysis platforms increasingly use machine-learning methods to suggest
three-point ranges from historical project data, to detect likely correlations between risk-affected
elements that a manual model might miss, and to run and re-run simulations far faster than earlier
generations of software (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5) — collectively, a genuine advance in both speed
and, where correlation detection is concerned, model realism. None of this changes where the accountability
sits: the three-point inputs, the correlation assumptions, and the funding percentile chosen from the output
are all professional and sponsor-level decisions, not outputs the model can validate on its own. An
AI-accelerated Monte Carlo run is a faster route to the same range of numbers a manual analysis would
eventually reach — never a reason to skip reviewing the assumptions behind it.

### 12.2.7 Risk response planning: strategies for threats

**Definition & purpose.** Risk response planning selects and plans a deliberate course of action for each
identified risk, converting analysis into a documented plan the project can actually execute. For threats
(risks with a potential adverse effect), the four standard strategies are **avoid, transfer, mitigate** and
**accept** — each changing a risk's probability, impact, ownership, or the project's exposure to it, in a
different way.

**The underlying principle.**
- **Avoid** — change the plan to eliminate the threat (or its cause) entirely, so the risk can no longer
  occur. This is the only strategy that removes probability altogether, but it is not always possible or
  economic — avoidance often means giving up some scope, method or schedule advantage to remove the risk.
- **Transfer** — shift the financial consequence (not the underlying event itself) to a third party better
  placed to bear or price it — insurance, a fixed-price subcontract, a warranty, or a contractual clause
  allocating the risk elsewhere (cross-reference KA 7.1's risk-allocation-by-contract-type treatment).
- **Mitigate** — take action in advance to reduce the risk's probability, its impact, or both, without
  eliminating it entirely — the most commonly used strategy for risks that cannot be fully avoided or
  transferred at reasonable cost.
- **Accept** — take no proactive action to change the risk, either because the cost of a response would
  exceed the risk's own expected cost (**passive acceptance**, typically covered from contingency) or, for a
  higher-priority risk, while holding a prepared **contingency plan** ready to execute only if the risk
  actually occurs (**active acceptance**).

**Example 12.2.5 — response strategies for Pipeline Segment 4B's quantified threats.**

*Scenario/Required.* A response strategy for each of R1–R4 (Example 12.2.1), stating the chosen strategy and
its rationale.

| ID | Risk | Strategy | Action | Residual position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Adverse ground conditions | **Mitigate** | Commission the targeted geotechnical survey (Example 12.2.3) and pre-plan the lower-cost mitigation response it enables | EMV reduces from the $21,000 register figure toward the survey-informed $21,500 total cost of the mitigate option, with materially less cost variance than the no-survey path |
| R2 | Utility clash rework | **Avoid** | Reroute a short section of the alignment during detailed design to eliminate the clash entirely | Risk removed from the register once the design change is approved, subject to the secondary-risk note below |
| R3 | Material price escalation | **Transfer** | Lock in a fixed-price supply agreement with the pipe/valve supplier before ordering | Price-escalation exposure passes to the supplier; residual risk is supplier non-performance, tracked separately |
| R4 | Weather-related delay | **Accept (active)** | No proactive action taken beyond normal seasonal allowance already in the schedule; the $7,000 EMV remains funded from contingency and the risk is actively monitored | Full residual exposure retained, covered by contingency and by schedule float where available |

*Interpretation.* Note that three of the four risks receive a proactive response (mitigate, avoid, transfer)
while only R4 is accepted — consistent with R4's comparatively lower score (Medium) and the practical
reality that weather is not a risk a controls team can meaningfully avoid, transfer or mitigate at this
project's scale; acceptance, backed by contingency, is the professionally correct strategy here, not a
failure to act.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Choosing "accept" by default for a high-scoring risk simply because a proactive response looks costly,
  without comparing that cost against the risk's own EMV (as Example 12.2.3 does explicitly for R1).
- Ignoring **secondary risks** a response itself introduces — R2's avoidance reroute, for example, moves the
  alignment through ground not previously surveyed, which should itself be screened through identification
  (12.2.2) rather than assumed risk-free.
- Confusing "transfer" with "eliminate" — R3's fixed-price agreement removes *price-escalation* exposure but
  creates a new, smaller exposure to the supplier's ability to perform at that price; transfer changes who
  bears a risk, it does not make the underlying uncertainty disappear from the project entirely.

**AI in this topic.** Contract-analytics and clause-library tools can flag standard risk-allocation clauses
appropriate to a transfer strategy (cross-reference KA 7.1, KA 13.4), and historical-project databases can
suggest which strategy has proven effective for a similar risk type in the past. Neither can weigh the
specific commercial trade-off (cost of avoidance vs residual exposure; supplier's actual capacity to bear a
transferred risk) that only the accountable professional, informed by current market and site conditions,
can properly judge; the chosen strategy and its rationale are signed off by that professional, not
auto-selected.

**Cross-references.** KA 12.2.5 (the decision-tree analysis behind R1's mitigate strategy); KA 7.1 (contract
types and risk allocation, underpinning the transfer strategy); KA 3.1.6 (contingency funding of accepted
risk); KA 12.2.9 (monitoring the response and any secondary risk it introduces).

### 12.2.8 Risk response planning: strategies for opportunities

**Definition & purpose.** Not every uncertain event threatens a project — some create a potential *upside*.
An opportunity is an uncertain event that, if it occurs, would benefit the project's objectives (lower cost,
shorter schedule, improved quality). The response strategies mirror the threat strategies but aim to
*increase*, not reduce, the chance and scale of the benefit: **exploit, share, enhance** and **accept**.

**The underlying principle.**
- **Exploit** — take deliberate action to make the opportunity certain to happen, capturing its full benefit
  — the positive-risk mirror of "avoid."
- **Share** — allocate ownership of the opportunity, in whole or in part, to a third party better placed to
  capture it (e.g. a joint venture or partnering arrangement structured so both parties benefit if the
  opportunity is realised) — the mirror of "transfer."
- **Enhance** — take action to increase the opportunity's probability or the size of its benefit, without
  making it certain — the mirror of "mitigate," and, like mitigate, the most commonly used of the four in
  practice.
- **Accept** — take no proactive action, capturing the benefit only if the opportunity happens to occur on
  its own — the mirror of threat acceptance.

**Mini-case — an accelerated delivery opportunity on Pipeline Segment 4B.** An alternate pipe supplier
offers Meridian an accelerated delivery slot for WP2 (pipe & valve procurement) that would shorten the
procurement lead time by three weeks at no additional unit cost, but the offer is open for only five working
days. Meridian's project controls team assesses the response options:

| Strategy | Action | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Exploit | Confirm the order within the offer window, securing the accelerated slot with certainty | Full three-week benefit captured; downstream trenching and pipe-laying activities can start earlier |
| Enhance | Pre-clear internal approval authority in advance so any future similar offer can be actioned within its window | Increases the *probability* that this type of opportunity is captured next time, without guaranteeing this specific offer |
| Share | Explore whether a neighbouring Meridian project could jointly commit to the supplier's capacity, improving both parties' terms | Only relevant if a genuine joint-commitment partner exists; not applicable to this single-project offer |
| Accept | Take no action; proceed on the original procurement schedule and accept the slot only if it is still available later | Likely forfeits the opportunity, given the five-day window |

Given the short window and the clear, certain benefit, Meridian's team **exploits** the opportunity by
confirming the order immediately. A secondary, favourable effect follows: shifting WP2's completion three
weeks earlier moves the exposed trenching work earlier in the season, which Meridian's estimators judge
reduces R4's (weather-related delay) probability from 35% to 25% — reducing R4's EMV from $7,000 to $5,000
(`0.25 × 20,000 = 5,000`), a further $2,000 reduction in the project's expected risk exposure as a direct
consequence of capturing the opportunity.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Treating "accept" as the automatic default for opportunities, as it often is for low-priority threats —
  because an opportunity has a limited window (as here), passive acceptance frequently means forfeiting the
  benefit entirely, not merely retaining a residual exposure.
- Failing to recognise an opportunity at all — teams accustomed to logging only threats can miss a genuine,
  register-worthy positive risk such as this one.
- Overstating an opportunity's benefit without the same probability-based rigour applied to threats — an
  opportunity's expected value is calculated identically (`Probability × Benefit`) and should be scrutinised
  with the same discipline, not simply welcomed uncritically.

**AI in this topic.** Procurement and market-intelligence tools can flag time-sensitive supplier offers or
favourable market movements as they arise, giving a controls team more of the short window such opportunities
often carry to decide (cross-reference KA 13.4, KA 13.5). They cannot make the exploit/share/enhance/accept
judgement itself, nor authorise the commercial commitment an exploit strategy typically requires — that
remains a time-critical professional decision, made faster with an AI-generated alert, not made by it.

**Cross-references.** KA 12.2.7 (the threat strategies this topic mirrors); KA 12.2.4 (R4's EMV,
recalculated here following the opportunity); KA 7.1 (procurement and contract strategy); KA 12.2.9
(monitoring realised opportunities and their knock-on effects on the rest of the register).

### 12.2.9 Risk monitoring and control

**Definition & purpose.** Risk monitoring and control is the ongoing activity of tracking identified risks,
watching for new ones, re-evaluating the register as facts change, and confirming that planned responses are
actually being executed and are having the intended effect. It is what keeps everything built in 12.2.2–
12.2.8 current rather than a one-time snapshot frozen at project start-up.

**The underlying principle.** A risk register is only as valuable as its last honest update. Monitoring
closes the loop on several distinct questions, each of which needs a routine answer, not just an initial
one:

- **Is each planned response actually being implemented**, and is it achieving the intended reduction in
  probability or impact (or, for opportunities, the intended increase)?
- **Has a risk's probability or impact changed** as new information arrives (a survey result, a market
  movement, a schedule update), requiring the register entry — and, if material, its priority — to be
  revised?
- **Has a new risk emerged** that identification (12.2.2) did not originally capture, including any
  **secondary risk** introduced by a response itself (as noted for R2's reroute in 12.2.7)?
- **Has a watchlist risk's score moved** enough to warrant promotion into full quantitative treatment, or has
  a previously quantified risk's exposure fallen enough to justify releasing part of its contingency?

**Practice.** Monitoring is run on a fixed cadence aligned with the project's wider reporting cycle (commonly
monthly, alongside the EVM and management reporting cycle, KA 4.3, KA 8.4), with each risk assigned a named
**owner** accountable for tracking it, and, where useful, an early-warning **trigger condition** defined in
advance (e.g. "if the geotechnical survey in Example 12.2.3 reports adverse conditions, escalate R1
immediately rather than waiting for the next scheduled review"). A simple, standing risk-exposure trend
(total quantified EMV, period on period) gives a single, trackable indicator of whether the project's overall
risk position is improving, worsening or holding steady, in the same spirit as a CV/SV trend in KA 6.2.

**Mini-case — a Pipeline Segment 4B risk register review, one reporting period later.** At the next monthly
review, three things have changed: (1) the geotechnical survey (Example 12.2.3) confirms *favourable*
ground conditions in the assessed sub-reach, so R1's probability is revised down from 30% to 15%, reducing
its EMV from $21,000 to `0.15 × 70,000 = 10,500`; (2) the accelerated WP2 delivery (12.2.8) has been
confirmed, so R4's EMV is formally updated from $7,000 to $5,000 as calculated there; (3) R6 (permit delay,
previously on the watchlist at a score of 3) is re-scored after the permitting authority confirms a
six-week backlog, moving its probability band up and lifting its score to 9 (Medium-High) — R6 is promoted
into the quantified register with a new EMV that the team must now calculate before the next review.

`Revised quantified EMV total (R1 revised + R2 + R3 + R4 revised) = 10,500 + 12,000 + 10,000 + 5,000 =
37,500`, a fall of `50,000 − 37,500 = 12,500` from Example 12.2.2's figure — before R6's newly promoted EMV
is added at the next cycle. This is exactly the kind of period-on-period movement a standing risk-exposure
trend indicator is designed to surface, and it is the evidence base a controls professional would use to
recommend releasing part of the $50,000 contingency reserve, or holding it pending R6's now-material exposure
— a funding decision addressed in full in KA 12.3.

**Common pitfalls.**
- Reviewing the register only when a risk actually occurs, rather than on a fixed forward cadence — by
  definition this catches problems only after the fact.
- Closing out a risk once its response is *planned* rather than once its response is *confirmed effective*
  (or the risk itself has definitively passed) — R1 here is not closed merely because a survey was
  commissioned, but re-scored once the survey's result is known.
- Failing to promote a watchlist risk when new information genuinely changes its score, leaving a now-material
  risk (R6, above) outside the quantified contingency basis.

**AI in this topic.** Anomaly-detection and natural-language monitoring tools can scan project correspondence,
site reports and market data feeds for signals relevant to logged risks (a permitting delay mentioned in an
email; a supplier price movement in a market feed) and flag them for the risk owner's attention faster than a
purely manual review cycle would (cross-reference KA 13.5) — a genuine early-warning benefit, particularly
for a large register. It cannot decide whether a flagged signal is material enough to trigger a re-score, nor
can it authorise the resulting change to a risk's probability, impact, or the contingency reserve built on it
— those decisions, and the accountability for making them promptly, remain with the risk owner and the
project controls professional, consistent with **"AI proposes; the professional verifies, decides and remains accountable"** throughout this
KA.

**Cross-references.** KA 4.3 (management reporting cadence, into which risk monitoring is typically folded);
KA 6.2 (the CV/SV trend concept this topic's EMV-trend indicator mirrors); KA 8.4 (Monitoring & Controlling
in the wider project lifecycle); KA 12.3 (contingency drawdown and release decisions arising from a

### Key terms

- **Risk register** — the living log of identified risks (and opportunities), their probability, impact,
  score, owner, planned response and status; the single artefact this KA builds and updates throughout.
- **Risk identification** — finding and describing threats and opportunities using techniques including
  checklists, interviews, brainstorming and assumptions analysis.
- **Qualitative risk analysis** — ordinal probability–impact scoring (`Risk score = Probability score ×
  Impact score`) used to prioritise risks for deeper, quantitative treatment.
- **Probability–impact (P–I) matrix** — the grid used to visualise and rank qualitatively scored risks.
- **Expected Monetary Value (EMV)** — `EMV = Probability × Impact`, in $, the standard basis for a
  risk-derived contingency reserve (`Contingency = Σ EMV`).
- **Decision tree** — a technique for choosing between response options by rolling back the EMV of each
  option's chance-node outcomes.
- **Three-point estimate (O, M, P) and PERT** — an estimate expressed as optimistic, most-likely and
  pessimistic values; `PERT expected value = (O + 4M + P)/6`; used as the basis for Monte Carlo simulation
  inputs.
- **Monte Carlo simulation** — repeated random sampling of uncertain cost or duration inputs, combined many
  thousands of times, producing a cumulative probability distribution (S-curve) of possible total outcomes.
- **P50 / P80 (and other percentiles)** — the value on a Monte Carlo cumulative distribution below which
  that percentage of simulated outcomes fell; the basis for choosing a contingency funding confidence level.
- **Threat response strategies** — avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept.
- **Opportunity response strategies** — exploit, share, enhance, accept.
- **Secondary risk** — a new risk introduced by a risk response itself.
- **Risk monitoring and control** — the ongoing cycle of tracking responses, re-scoring risks as facts
  change, identifying new risks, and maintaining the register on a fixed reporting cadence.

### Sample MCQs

**MCQ 12.2.1 [topic 12.2.2, level: recall]**
Which risk identification technique is specifically designed to test whether the assumptions underlying the
project plan and estimate are actually valid?
A. Brainstorming
B. Checklists
C. **Assumptions analysis** ✔
D. Interviews
*Rationale:* Assumptions analysis systematically tests plan assumptions, treating an invalid assumption as a
risk in its own right. Brainstorming and interviews surface risks from group and individual knowledge
respectively; checklists surface known historical categories — neither specifically targets stated
assumptions.

**MCQ 12.2.2 [topic 12.2.3, level: application — numerical]**
A risk has an estimated probability of 35% (probability score 3 on a 1–5 ordinal scale) and an estimated
cost impact of $60,000 (impact score 4). What is its qualitative risk score, and how would it be classified
under a Low (1–4) / Medium (5–9) / High (10–25) banding?
A. $21,000 — High
B. **12 — High** ✔
C. 7 — Medium
D. 12 — Medium
*Rationale:* `Risk score = Probability score × Impact score = 3 × 4 = 12`, which falls in the High (10–25)
band. A wrongly computes a monetary EMV figure instead of an ordinal score; D applies the correct
arithmetic but the wrong band.

**MCQ 12.2.3 [topic 12.2.4, level: application — numerical]**
A risk register contains three quantified risks: Risk X (probability 20%, impact $50,000), Risk Y
(probability 40%, impact $25,000), Risk Z (probability 10%, impact $80,000). What is the total EMV-based
contingency reserve?
A. $18,000
B. **$28,000** ✔
C. $155,000
D. $8,000
*Rationale:* `EMV(X) = 0.20 × 50,000 = 10,000`; `EMV(Y) = 0.40 × 25,000 = 10,000`; `EMV(Z) = 0.10 × 80,000 =
8,000`; `Total = 10,000 + 10,000 + 8,000 = 28,000`. A and D are partial sums; C wrongly sums the raw impacts
without weighting by probability.

**MCQ 12.2.4 [topic 12.2.5, level: application — numerical]**
In a decision-tree analysis, Option A costs $10,000 upfront and, if an adverse event occurs (probability
25%), costs a further $40,000; if the event does not occur, no further cost is incurred. What is the total
EMV of Option A?
A. $10,000
B. $40,000
C. **$20,000** ✔
D. $50,000
*Rationale:* `EMV(chance node) = 0.25 × 40,000 = 10,000`; `EMV(Option A) = 10,000 (upfront) + 10,000 =
20,000`. A omits the chance-node contribution; B reports only the adverse outcome's cost; D wrongly sums the
full adverse cost to the upfront cost without probability-weighting it.

**MCQ 12.2.5 [topic 12.2.7, level: analysis]**
A project team locks in a fixed-price supply agreement with a subcontractor specifically to remove the
project's exposure to future material price escalation. Which risk response strategy does this best
illustrate?
A. Mitigate
B. Avoid
C. **Transfer** ✔
D. Accept
*Rationale:* Transfer shifts the financial consequence of a risk to a third party better placed to bear or
price it, without eliminating the underlying uncertainty — exactly what a fixed-price agreement does for
price-escalation risk. Mitigate would reduce (not shift) the exposure; avoid would remove the risk's cause
entirely (e.g. redesigning to use a different material); accept takes no proactive action.

**MCQ 12.2.6 [topic 12.2.6, level: analysis]**
A Monte Carlo cost simulation produces a P50 of $600,000 and a P80 of $645,000 for a project. Which
statement best describes what these figures mean?
A. The project will definitely cost between $600,000 and $645,000
B. **There is a 50% chance total cost will not exceed $600,000, and an 80% chance it will not exceed
$645,000** ✔
C. $600,000 is the minimum possible cost and $645,000 is the maximum possible cost
D. The project should always be funded at exactly the P50 figure
*Rationale:* A percentile from a Monte Carlo cumulative distribution states the probability that the
outcome will not exceed that value — it is not a guaranteed range (A), not the absolute minimum/maximum of
the distribution (C), and the choice of funding percentile is a policy decision, not automatically P50 (D).

### Self-check questions (answers in Appendix C)

1. Using the six-risk Pipeline Segment 4B register in Example 12.2.1, explain in one or two sentences why
   R5 and R6 are placed on the qualitative watchlist rather than carried into full quantitative analysis,
   and describe the circumstance that would justify promoting one of them later.
2. A risk has probability 45% and cost impact $30,000. Calculate its EMV, and explain in one sentence what
   that EMV figure does — and does not — tell you about the outcome of this specific project.
3. Using Example 12.2.3's decision tree, recompute the EMV of "commission the survey" if the pre-planned
   mitigation cost were instead $55,000 (all other figures unchanged). Does the recommendation to commission
   the survey still hold?
4. Explain, using the mini-case in 12.2.9, why R1's probability revision (from 30% to 15%) after the
   geotechnical survey is a *monitoring* activity rather than a new *identification* activity.

**Answers**

1. R5 (score 4) and R6 (score 3) both fall below the Medium/High cut used to prioritise quantitative effort
   (Example 12.2.1's threshold of 6); they are lower priority today, not risk-free, and remain on the
   register to be re-scored at each review. R6 is, in fact, promoted later in the mini-case (12.2.9) once
   the permitting authority's confirmed six-week backlog materially raises its probability band — the kind
   of new information that justifies promotion.
2. `EMV = 0.45 × 30,000 = 13,500`. This figure tells you the long-run, probability-weighted average cost
   this risk category would impose across a portfolio of similar risks or projects — it does not tell you
   what will actually happen on this specific project, which will either incur the full $30,000 (if the
   risk occurs) or $0 (if it does not).
3. `EMV(chance node) = 0.30 × 55,000 = 16,500`; `EMV(commission survey) = 8,000 + 16,500 = 24,500`. Compared
   with `EMV(do not survey) = 27,000` (unchanged), commissioning the survey remains the lower-EMV option, by
   $2,500 — the recommendation still holds, though the margin has narrowed from the original $5,500.
4. The risk (adverse ground conditions on this specific sub-reach) was already identified and logged as R1
   in 12.2.2; the survey result is new *information about an already-identified risk's probability*, not the
   discovery of a previously unlisted risk. Revising an existing entry's score in light of new evidence is
   exactly what the monitoring-and-control stage (12.2.9) exists to do; identification (12.2.2) is only
   concerned with risks not yet on the register at all.
