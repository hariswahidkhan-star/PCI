## The post

Most honorary titles in this profession are bought.

Not in so many words. The mechanism is usually a nomination fee, an assessment fee, a
credential fee, a mandatory ceremony ticket — and a review process that has never once
declined an applicant who paid. The letters arrive. The distinction does not.

We built the Honorary Fellow (PCI) route to be the opposite of that, and the terms are
published so you can hold us to it.

**There is no fee.** No nomination fee, no assessment fee, no credential fee. Recognition is
awarded on merit following independent review. Optional charges exist only for a printed
certificate, international delivery, a replacement document, or attending a ceremony — and
payment does not influence the decision or create any entitlement to recognition. That
sentence is in our published terms, not in a post.

**There is a bar, and all of it must be met.** At least eight years of professional
experience in project controls, cost control, finance, project management or a closely
related field. At least three of those years at a managerial level — leading teams,
functions, programmes or budgets. And a demonstrable record of distinguished contribution to
the profession, evidenced rather than asserted.

Not any one of the three. All three.

**Meeting the bar does not entitle you to the recognition.** Conferral is at the board's sole
discretion. We may ask for more information. We may decline without stating reasons. An
honorary distinction that is granted to everyone who qualifies to be considered is not a
distinction — it is a mailing list.

**And it is not a certification.** This is the part most institutes blur, so let us be exact.
Honorary Fellow (PCI) is a board-conferred recognition of contribution. It involves no
examination. It is not accreditation, registration or a licence, and it does not certify
competence. Our examined credentials — PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI — are earned by passing the
examination, on every route, without exception. Nobody is given one for a distinguished
career, including people who have one.

Holding both is possible. Confusing them is not.

**What you send us.** A résumé or CV, your relevant experience and a professional summary in
your own words, and a suitability declaration. Academic qualifications, professional
certifications and career history if you have them. PDF, JPG, PNG or WEBP, three megabytes
each.

**What you do not send us.** Identity documents. We ask for one government ID and a
photograph only if the board shortlists you, through a secure one-time link. We record no ID
number and no document detail — the image only. Access is restricted to the board on a
need-to-know basis, and the documents are deleted after the decision, or sooner if you ask.
Most schemes collect all of that on day one, from everyone, and keep it indefinitely.

**What conferral carries.** An award number, publicly verifiable on the register — a
recognition nobody can check is worth nothing. Access to the Institute's learning resources
and study materials. And no examined credential, because those are earned by examination.

Review takes roughly fifteen to thirty days. That is an estimate, not a commitment.

If your record stands up, apply. If it does not yet, sit the examination — that route is
always open, and on the Founding route it is free.

## Editorial notes

**Why the post leads with the fee problem.** The market's honest objection to any honorary
title is that it was probably bought. Answering it in the first line is more credible than
answering it in paragraph nine, and the Institute's published terms genuinely support the
claim — the fee position is written into the programme terms, not invented for a post.

**Why the boundary is stated four times.** In the headline, in the routes table, in a
dedicated "what it is not" block, and in the footer. The single greatest reputational risk to
an institute that offers both honorary recognition and examined credentials is the two being
conflated in a reader's memory. Repetition is the cheapest available insurance, and it costs
nothing that matters.

**What is deliberately absent.** No claim of accreditation or external recognition. No
suggestion that recognition improves employment outcomes. No named honorary recipients. No
count of awards conferred. No implication that applying is likely to succeed.
