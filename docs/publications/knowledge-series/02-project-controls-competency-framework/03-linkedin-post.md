## The post

**A controls function of 24 people, supporting nine projects, with a reputation inside its own business for being strong.**

It was strong. Low turnover, experienced staff, a cost team other departments borrowed from.

When we assessed it properly, one number ended the conversation.

**One person held Level 3 or above in project risk. Across nine live projects.**

Which means eight of those projects were setting contingency by percentage rather than by quantified analysis. Not because anyone decided to. Because there was nobody available to do it any other way.

The function's own capability report described it as "stretched but functioning."

---

**That report was not dishonest. It was averaged.**

This is the part I would ask you to sit with, because almost every organisation does it.

You assess individuals. You aggregate the scores. You present a mean. And the mean is the single statistic most guaranteed to conceal the thing you needed to find.

A team that is excellent in three competencies and absent in three averages the same as a team that is adequate across the board. The first has three holes and does not know it. The second is uniformly fine.

Competencies are not substitutes for one another. Eight excellent cost engineers cannot produce a quantitative schedule risk analysis between them. There is no amount of cost competence that adds up to risk competence.

**Capability is set by coverage, not by average.**

The metric we use is deliberately crude, because crude metrics get used: professionals at Level 3 or above in a competency, divided by the number of concurrent projects that need it. Below 1.0 and you are sharing people. Below 0.5 and most projects go without. Below 0.2 the competency exists on an org chart and nowhere else.

Cost management in that function scored 0.89. Project risk scored 0.11.

---

**Now the more uncomfortable question: how does a gap like that survive for years inside a competent organisation?**

It had run cost training for four consecutive years. In its already-strongest competency.

Not through negligence. The internal trainers were cost engineers. The shared vocabulary was cost. The training proposals that reached approval were the ones with the most articulate advocates — and the advocates were, inevitably, the people who were already good at the thing.

Risk had one Level 3 professional and no advocate at all.

**Competence investment follows competence.** Organisations train where they are strong, because that is where the enthusiasm and the vocabulary and the credibility live. The strong cluster gets stronger. The gap widens quietly.

Naming that dynamic out loud is usually enough to interrupt it. Ask your function two questions in the same meeting: where did development spend go last year, and which competency are we weakest in. In most organisations those are different answers. In a few, they are opposites.

---

**Underneath all of this sits a definitional problem, and it is worth being blunt about.**

Most competency frameworks cannot support this kind of analysis, because they are written in adjectives. "Demonstrates strong analytical skills." "Collaborative." "Detail-oriented."

Those are real human qualities and they are not competencies, because two assessors will not agree on them and nobody can practise them deliberately on a Tuesday.

The standard we hold to is three parts, and all three are required. An **artefact** — the thing produced, that can be read. A **defence** — why this method rather than another, what assumptions it rests on, what would change the answer. And independent **verification** of both.

No artefact, no competency. Not for anyone, including senior people. Especially senior people.

The defence matters more than the artefact, incidentally, and increasingly so. An artefact can be inherited from a template, copied from last quarter, or — now — generated on request. The defence is what separates someone who produced the output from someone who understands it.

---

**One last thing, and it is the part I think the profession has not yet priced.**

Judgement has never been taught directly. It has been a by-product.

You build the artefact a hundred times. Somewhere in that hundred you meet the accrual that does not fit the rule, the progress claim that cannot possibly be right, the float that turns out to be an artefact of a constraint nobody remembers setting. Those encounters accumulate into pattern recognition — and pattern recognition is what senior judgement actually is.

AI removes a great deal of that volume.

The risk is not that junior people will have less to do. It is that **the volume was the mechanism**, and removing the mechanism does not remove the need for what it produced.

An organisation that automates its junior controls work will have perfectly adequate output next year, and no Level 3 professionals in eight.

The best answer I have found is to make verification the training route. Give developing professionals AI output and require them to check it — because checking demands knowing what right looks like, and unlike a training exercise, it is real work that someone needed done anyway.

---

Three questions I would genuinely like answered by people running these functions:

**Where did your development spend go last year, and which competency are you actually weakest in?**

**Can any of your people evidence their level with an artefact and a defence** — or does your matrix rest on tenure, job title and self-assessment?

**And if you automated your junior production work tomorrow, where would your next generation of judgement come from?**

I do not think the third question has a settled answer yet. I would like to hear how people are thinking about it.

## Publishing notes

**Hook.** Opens on a concrete organisation and a single damning number. Do not move the "one person, nine projects" line further down — it is what stops the scroll.

**Formatting.** Keep the short paragraphs and the section breaks. The first two lines must survive LinkedIn's truncation on their own.

**Comment strategy.** The third closing question is the genuinely open one. Answer it yourself in the thread if engagement stalls, and be honest that the Institute does not consider it solved. That candour is the reason this post will travel further than a framework announcement.

**Do not add a certification call to action.** The credential is not mentioned. The framework is the argument.
