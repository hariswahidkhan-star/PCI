# Report template and data tables

> The annual report's complete structure — sections, tables, charts, caveats and annexes — with every data cell empty.

**In one paragraph.** This is the full section structure of the Institute's annual salary and skills
report: every table with its columns defined and every cell carrying the placeholder `[DATA]`, every chart
specified in words, the mandatory methodology and limitations sections written out, and the standing
caveats that must appear in every edition. No data has been collected, so no table contains a value. This
is the shell into which field data will be dropped, and the rules that govern what may be dropped into it.

**Who this is for.** The analysts and editors who will produce the report; reviewers checking that the
published edition matches the structure that was committed to in advance; and readers who want to inspect
the apparatus before there is anything in it to argue about.

---

## 1. What this shell is, and the rule that governs it

Every table below is complete in its columns and empty in its cells. `[DATA]` marks a value that field
data will supply. Each table carries the label **structure only — no data collected**, and that label
stays until the survey has been run, analysed and reviewed.

**The governing rule: the structure is fixed before the data arrives.** An analyst who sees the results
first and designs the tables second will — without any dishonest intention — design tables that show what
the data happens to say. Fixing the shell in advance means the report's shape cannot be tuned to its
findings, and a reader can compare the published edition against this document to check.

Three consequences follow, and they are not negotiable:

- **A table whose cells are suppressed is still published, empty, with the suppression rule named.** Missing
  cells are the report's most honest content.
- **A table cannot be added after the data is seen** except in the "additional analysis" section (§11),
  which exists so that genuine discoveries have somewhere to go while remaining visibly separate from the
  pre-committed structure.
- **A table cannot be removed** because it turned out thin. It is published with its cells suppressed.

## 4. Section 1 — How to read this report (standing text)

This section is reproduced in every edition, adjusted only for the edition's specifics.

> Every figure in this report is a summary of what a defined group of respondents told us, over a defined
> period, about a defined component of their pay. It is not a rate, a recommendation or a target.
>
> Five questions to ask of any figure here, or anywhere else:
>
> 1. **Who was asked?** Look at the sample composition in §4 before reading any figure.
> 2. **How many answered this?** Every figure carries its own response count. A figure without one has been
>    quoted incorrectly.
> 3. **What exactly is being measured?** Base pay, total cash and a day rate are three different
>    quantities. The column heading tells you which.
> 4. **When?** Every figure refers to the reference period stated in §2.
> 5. **In what currency and on what basis?** Amounts are converted at a single reference date and are
>    stated gross. Converted figures compare nominal amounts, not living standards.
>
> Where a cell is empty, it is empty because publishing it would have breached one of our disclosure
> rules — most often because too few people answered. The rule applied is named in the cell and listed in
> Annex E. We do not fill those gaps with estimates.
>
> Guidance on using figures like these in a real pay conversation is published separately as
> `SAL-06 — Using market data honestly`.

## 11. Chart specifications

Charts are specified in words here so that the visual design cannot quietly change what the data says.
Every chart carries its response count in the subtitle — not the caption, not a footnote — and every chart
respects the suppression rules; a suppressed cell is drawn as an explicit gap with a label, never
interpolated, never omitted so that the axis closes over it.

| Chart | Question it answers | Mark and encoding | Axis rules | Must not |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 Sample composition | Who responded | Stacked horizontal bars, one bar per role, segments by level | Count axis from zero | Use a pie chart; imply the sample is the profession |
| C2 Base pay distribution by level | How spread out is pay within a level | Box plot per level: quartiles as the box, whiskers to the tenth and ninetieth percentiles where n permits | Value axis from zero; identical scale across panels | Draw whiskers where the distribution-detail threshold is not met |
| C3 Base pay by role group | How role groups compare | Grouped box plots, one panel per region meeting the threshold | Shared scale across panels | Rank groups by median without showing overlap |
| C4 Component prevalence | Which components a cell actually receives | Horizontal bars, one per component | Share axis from zero to whole | Stack components as though they sum to a total reward |
| C5 Day rate against days worked | Why a rate is not an income | Two adjacent distributions, rate and days, on separate axes | Separate panels, never a dual axis | Multiply one by the other, in the chart or in the caption |
| C6 Skills — required against held | Where the gaps are | Paired bars per skill, requirement and capability adjacent | Common share axis | Draw a connecting line implying a per-person gap; the gap has its own chart |
| C7 Capability gap | The within-respondent gap | Horizontal bars, ordered, with tied ranks shown as a shared band | Share axis from zero | Truncate the axis; show only the top of the ranking |
| C8 AI use by sanction status | Whether use is governed | Small multiples, one panel per sanction status | Identical scale across panels | Combine panels into a single total that hides the governance split |
| C9 Satisfaction distribution | How opinion is spread | Diverging stacked bars centred between the middle points | Symmetric about the centre | Reduce the distribution to a mean score |

**Accessibility rules for every chart.** No meaning is carried by colour alone — every encoding is also
carried by position, pattern or a direct label. Each chart has alt text describing what it shows and what
the reader should take from it, and each has an accompanying table so that the same content is available
linearly.

**Two prohibitions that apply to all charts.** No trend line is drawn through fewer than three comparable
cycles. No chart places pay against any respondent characteristic in a way that implies causation, because
a cross-sectional survey cannot support a causal claim in either direction.

## 12. Section 13 — Limitations (standing text)

Reproduced in every edition, extended where an edition has a specific additional limitation.

> **This is a volunteer sample.** Respondents chose to take part. It is not a random sample of project
> controls professionals, and no statistical technique in this report converts it into one. Figures
> describe the people who answered.
>
> **Composition drives everything.** Read §4 before any figure. A cell that is mostly one region, one
> sector or one employer describes that region, sector or employer.
>
> **Self-report.** Pay, skills and level are as reported by respondents. We validate internal consistency
> and correct unambiguous unit errors; we cannot verify a payslip.
>
> **Cross-sectional.** This report describes one period. It cannot establish that anything causes anything
> else — not experience, not sector, not certification, not skills.
>
> **Currency conversion compares nominal amounts.** It says nothing about purchasing power, tax, social
> contributions or what any of it buys where the respondent lives.
>
> **Jurisdictions differ.** Employment law, tax treatment, mandatory retirement provision, benefit norms
> and pay-transparency requirements vary by country. Nothing here should be read as describing any
> jurisdiction's rules.
>
> **Suppression is not absence.** An empty cell means we would not publish it, not that nobody works in
> that role. Annex E lists every suppression and the rule applied.
>
> **The gaps are where the need is greatest.** Smaller markets and specialist roles are the hardest to
> report and the most poorly served by existing data. We would rather leave the cell empty than fill it
> with something we cannot defend.

## 13. Section 14 — Standing caveats (verbatim block)

Reproduced without alteration in every edition, in the report, in the executive summary, and in any
extract or slide deck the Institute publishes from it.

> **What this report is.** A summary of what a self-selected group of project controls professionals told
> us about their roles, their pay and their skills, over one defined period, under the published
> methodology.
>
> **What it is not.** It is not a rate card, a pay recommendation, a benchmark for setting an individual's
> pay, or a forecast. It does not state what any role "should" pay.
>
> **On certification and pay.** Nothing in this report should be read as a claim that holding any
> certification — including the Institute's own — produces a pay outcome. PCI makes no claim of guaranteed
> employment or salary outcomes. Where certification status appears in this report it is a respondent
> characteristic, and a cross-sectional survey cannot establish causation.
>
> **On the Institute's status.** PCI's certifications are developed with reference to ISO/IEC 17024
> personnel-certification principles. PCI is not accredited by any ISO/IEC 17024 accreditation body.
>
> **On independence.** No sponsor, employer, recruitment business or vendor saw these results before
> publication, chose which analyses were published, or had any role in the methodology.
>
> **On correction.** If a figure here is wrong, we will correct it publicly, on the same channels that
> carried it, with a dated note in this document.

## 15. Completion rules for the analyst

1. Populate the shell in the order the sections appear. Composition tables are completed and reviewed
   before any reward table is populated.
2. Run the suppression tests before formatting anything. A suppressed cell keeps its row.
3. Never delete a row or column because it is empty.
4. Never add a table outside §12 (additional analysis).
5. Every figure carries its own n. If a figure cannot be given an n, it cannot be published.
6. Write the caption before the number is in the cell, so the caption describes the measurement rather
   than the result.
7. Reproduce §12 and §13 of the report verbatim. They are not edited for tone, length or edition.
8. Complete Table 33 before writing any sentence comparing this cycle with the last.
9. Hand the populated draft to a reviewer who has not seen the data, together with this document, and ask
   them to find any structural difference.

## 16. How this goes wrong

**A headline appears on the cover.** The single number that summarises nothing becomes the thing everyone
quotes. The rule in §2 exists to prevent it, and it will be argued about every year.

**Empty tables are quietly dropped.** The report looks tidier and the reader loses the most informative
content in it — the record of what could not be said.

**Components are summed into total reward** because a reader asked for one number. The result is a total
built from different components for different respondents, which is the exact error §7 exists to prevent.

**A day rate is annualised.** Someone multiplies a rate by a plausible number of days and prints it beside
a salary. Chart C5's prohibition is written down because this will be proposed.

**The chart improves the finding.** An axis is truncated to make a difference visible, or a suppressed
category is dropped so the bars sit neatly. Both are misrepresentations, and both look like design
decisions.

**The report is careful and the promotion is not.** The caveats are in the document, and the post says
"the most in-demand skill in project controls". The standing caveats bind every extract for this reason.
