# PFL-AI: the nineteen competencies

> Each competency of the PCI AI Project Finance Leader — defined, levelled, evidenced, and separated from
> its look-alike.

**In one paragraph.** The PCI AI Project Finance Leader credential publishes nineteen competencies. This
document takes each one in turn and states what it is in terms of decisions, what it looks like at each of
the four levels, the named artefacts that evidence it, and the common false positive — the thing that is
routinely mistaken for the competency and is not it. It is the working reference for anyone assembling
evidence, specifying a project finance role, or assessing a claim against the PFL-AI standard.

**Who this is for.** Candidates for PFL-AI; heads of project finance, transaction leads and lending teams
designing roles and development plans; assessors applying the standard to evidence they did not collect.

---

## 1. How to read this document

The nineteen competencies below are the published PFL-AI set, in the published order, unaltered. Each
entry follows the structure set out in *CMP-01* §3: **definition** (the decisions the competency governs),
**by level** (the four-level scale defined in *CMP-02* §2 and not restated here), **evidence** (named
artefacts), and **the false positive**.

One scope statement first, because candidates infer more than exists. **No examination blueprint has been
published for PFL-AI** — no domain weightings, no item counts
[CONFIRM: PFL-AI examination blueprint — weightings and item counts]. The competency set below is the
authoritative published statement of what the credential claims; it is not a syllabus with a weighting,
and it should not be read as one. Item counts for any PCI examination await a formal job-task analysis,
and the definitive pass mark will be set by a modified-Angoff standard-setting study; the platform's
configured mark is 65 % and scoring is criterion-referenced, not graded on a curve.

Acronyms used throughout are expanded on first appearance: special purpose vehicle (SPV), cash flow
available for debt service (CFADS), debt service cover ratio (DSCR), loan life cover ratio (LLCR),
project life cover ratio (PLCR), public–private partnership (PPP), environmental and social (E&S),
internal rate of return (IRR), net present value (NPV), weighted average cost of capital (WACC).

## 2. Foundations and appraisal

### 2.1 Project finance

**Definition.** Financing an asset on the strength of its own cash flows, in a ring-fenced vehicle, with
recourse to sponsors limited by agreement rather than by sentiment. The competency is the ability to place
a transaction correctly on the recourse spectrum and to work out who bears which risk once the structure
is built.

**By level.** *Foundation* — describes the structure of a financing accurately: the SPV, the sponsors, the
lenders, the principal contracts. *Practitioner* — works inside a live transaction, tracing how a
particular risk is allocated through the contract suite and into the financing documents.
*Professional* — decides whether a project should be financed on a limited-recourse basis at all, and
identifies the risks that the structure has failed to allocate to anyone. *Leader* — sets a firm's or a
lender's approach to structuring, and declines transactions whose risk allocation is unsound however
attractive the returns.

**Evidence.** A structure memorandum you authored; a risk allocation matrix tracing each material risk to
the party bearing it and the document that puts it there; a documented recommendation against a structure,
with reasoning and outcome.

**The false positive.** Vocabulary. Fluency in the terminology of project finance is acquired quickly and
proves nothing. The diagnostic question is the unglamorous one: *which risk in this structure is currently
unallocated, and who will actually pay if it happens?* Structures fail at the risks nobody was assigned.

### 2.2 Infrastructure finance

**Definition.** Applying project finance to infrastructure assets specifically — the sector economics,
the counterparties, the regulatory and political exposure, the revenue mechanisms (availability payment,
usage or demand, regulated return) and the investor base that buys these assets at each stage of life.

**By level.** *Foundation* — distinguishes the main revenue mechanisms and what each implies for risk.
*Practitioner* — analyses an asset within a sector they know: understands its demand drivers, regulatory
regime and comparable transactions. *Professional* — assesses an asset in a sector or jurisdiction that is
new to them by identifying what must be true for the revenue to arrive, and tests it. *Leader* — sets
sector strategy or lending appetite, and owns the concentration risk that follows.

**Evidence.** A sector or asset analysis you produced, naming demand drivers and regulatory exposures; a
revenue-mechanism comparison for a specific asset; a written view on a jurisdiction's regulatory risk that
was tested by subsequent events.

**The false positive.** Sector familiarity mistaken for sector analysis. Having worked on eight roads does
not mean a person can say what would make the ninth fail. The competency is analytical, not biographical.

### 2.3 Financial modelling

**Definition.** Building, auditing and using a model that represents a transaction faithfully enough to
support a decision — structure, integrity, transparency of assumptions, and the discipline that lets
someone else find and test any number in it. Depth in *CMP-06* §7.

**By level.** *Foundation* — navigates a model, traces a calculation and updates inputs without breaking
it. *Practitioner* — builds and maintains model sections to a defined standard: consistent formulae, no
hard-coded values in calculation blocks, working checks, documented inputs. *Professional* — designs a
model for a transaction: decides granularity, timing convention, circularity treatment and scenario
architecture; audits others' models and finds the errors that matter; states the model's limitations
alongside its output. *Leader* — sets modelling standards, decides what model risk the organisation
accepts, and requires independent review where reliance is material.

**Evidence.** A model you built, with its documentation, check sheet and assumption log; a model audit
report you wrote, with findings and materiality; a scenario architecture you designed; a modelling
standard in use in your organisation with your authorship.

**The false positive.** Speed and formula complexity. A rapidly built model with elaborate formulae and no
checks is a liability, and the more impressive it looks the longer the error survives. The competency is
visible in the checks, the assumption log and the auditability, which is exactly the part that takes the
time.

### 2.4 Investment appraisal

**Definition.** Deciding whether a project is worth doing, using discounted cash flow and its companions,
with the discount rate, the cash flow definition and the treatment of tax and inflation chosen
deliberately and stated. Includes knowing which metric answers which question and where each one misleads.

**By level.** *Foundation* — computes net present value (NPV), internal rate of return (IRR) and payback
correctly from a given cash flow. *Practitioner* — prepares an appraisal to procedure, applies the
organisation's discount rate, and presents results with sensitivities. *Professional* — decides the
appraisal basis: which cash flows are relevant, whose returns are being measured (project or equity), the
discount rate and its justification, and the consistent treatment of inflation and tax. Identifies where
IRR is a misleading answer — non-conventional cash flows, mutually exclusive projects of different scale
or life — and says so. *Leader* — sets the appraisal standard and hurdle-rate policy, and owns the
consequence of the incentives that policy creates.

**Evidence.** An appraisal you authored stating the cash flow definition, discount rate basis and
inflation/tax convention; a sensitivity and breakeven analysis naming the variables the decision turns on;
a documented case where you rejected a metric as unsuitable and explained what to use instead.

**The false positive.** Producing the metrics. The arithmetic is not the competency; the definition of
what is being discounted, at what rate, and whose return it represents is the competency. An appraisal
whose discount rate is "the one we always use" has not been appraised.

### 2.5 Capital structure

**Definition.** Deciding the shape of the funding — how much debt against equity, the tenor and
amortisation profile relative to the asset's cash generation, the sequencing of drawdowns, and the
consequences of the shape for resilience and for returns.

**By level.** *Foundation* — reads a sources-and-uses statement and a funding profile accurately.
*Practitioner* — models alternative gearing and amortisation profiles and reports their effects on
returns and cover ratios. *Professional* — recommends a structure for a specific transaction, stating the
trade-off between equity return and the project's ability to survive a downside, and the point at which
additional leverage stops being efficient. *Leader* — sets structuring policy, and decides the leverage the
organisation will not exceed regardless of what a market will bear.

**Evidence.** A structuring paper comparing profiles with the reasoning for the recommendation; a downside
resilience analysis at alternative gearing levels; a documented recommendation to reduce leverage against
commercial pressure to increase it.

**The false positive.** Optimisation to a target return. Solving for the gearing that hits an equity return
is an exercise in inversion. The competency is the judgement about what the structure does when revenue
disappoints, which is the question the model was not asked.

### 2.6 Debt and equity

**Definition.** The instruments themselves: senior and subordinated debt, mezzanine, sponsor equity and
shareholder loans, bonds and private placements, hedging arrangements — their terms, their ranking, their
protections, and the behaviour of their holders when the project underperforms.

**By level.** *Foundation* — identifies instruments and their ranking in a capital stack.
*Practitioner* — works with term sheets and facility documentation, tracks conditions and covenants, and
models drawdown, interest and repayment mechanics correctly. *Professional* — negotiates or advises on
terms with a view to how they will operate in a downside: which covenant bites first, what the cure rights
are, and how the intercreditor arrangement behaves when parties disagree. *Leader* — sets the terms the
organisation requires and concedes, and owns the portfolio consequence of that position.

**Evidence.** A term sheet you negotiated or reviewed with your annotations and rationale; a covenant
analysis showing which test binds under stress; a waiver or restructuring you worked on, with the
reasoning; hedging documentation and its modelled effect.

**The false positive.** Documentation familiarity. Knowing where a provision sits is administration.
The competency is anticipating the instrument's behaviour under stress — and the honest test is whether the
person has ever seen, or modelled, what happens when the covenant is breached.

## 3. Bankability, structures and the transaction

### 3.1 Project bankability

**Definition.** The composite judgement about whether lenders will fund a project on acceptable terms:
revenue certainty, contract quality, counterparty strength, risk allocation, sponsor capability, and the
resilience of cover under downside cases. Bankability is a conclusion assembled from the other
competencies, not a measure in itself.

**By level.** *Foundation* — lists the factors lenders assess. *Practitioner* — assembles a bankability
assessment against a checklist and identifies gaps in the documentation. *Professional* — forms and
defends a view on whether a project is bankable, names the two or three issues that will decide it, and
proposes the specific structural changes that would fix them. *Leader* — sets the institution's
bankability standard and declines transactions that meet the checklist but fail the judgement.

**Evidence.** A bankability assessment you authored, with the issues ranked and the remedies proposed; a
gap-closure plan that was implemented; a credit paper you wrote, and the outcome; a project you advised
against, with the reasons recorded.

**The false positive.** Checklist completion. A complete checklist tells you the documents exist. The
competency is knowing which two items on the list will actually determine the outcome, and being right
often enough to be worth asking.

### 3.2 DSCR, LLCR and PLCR

**Definition.** The cover ratios by which lenders measure protection — the debt service cover ratio (DSCR),
being cash flow available for debt service (CFADS) over scheduled debt service in the same period; the
loan life cover ratio (LLCR), being the present value of CFADS over the loan's remaining life against debt
outstanding; and the project life cover ratio (PLCR), the same construction over the project's full
remaining life. The competency includes knowing what each ratio protects against and how each is
manipulated.

**By level.** *Foundation* — calculates each ratio correctly and states its definition, including what is
and is not in CFADS. *Practitioner* — models the ratios across a transaction's life, identifies the
binding period, and reconciles ratio movements to their drivers. *Professional* — decides what the lock-up
and default levels should be for a given risk profile, defines CFADS precisely in the documentation, and
detects definitional games — items moved above or below the CFADS line, reserve accounts flattering a
period, a discount rate choice inflating LLCR. *Leader* — sets minimum cover standards by asset class and
owns the exceptions.

**Evidence.** A ratio analysis identifying the binding period and its driver; a negotiated CFADS
definition and your rationale; a documented challenge to a ratio presentation you judged misleading; a
downside case showing where cover is lost and what happens next.

**The false positive.** Calculation. The ratio is arithmetic once CFADS is defined; the competency is in
the definition, the base case behind it and the downside at which it fails. A person who can produce the
ratio but cannot say what would have to happen for it to breach has the arithmetic, not the competency.

### 3.3 PPPs and concessions

**Definition.** Structures in which a public authority contracts a private party to finance, build and
operate an asset over a long term — the payment mechanism, the risk transfer intended and the risk
transfer achieved, the performance and deduction regime, change and termination provisions, and handback.

**By level.** *Foundation* — describes the main PPP and concession models and how each is paid.
*Practitioner* — works within a concession: models the payment mechanism including deductions, tracks
performance obligations, and applies change provisions. *Professional* — assesses whether a proposed risk
transfer is real — whether the private party can price and manage the risk it is being given — and
identifies the provisions that will cause disputes in year eight. Advises on the termination and handback
regime as a financeable proposition. *Leader* — sets policy on which concession structures the
organisation will accept, and owns long-tenor political and regulatory exposure.

**Evidence.** A payment-mechanism model with the deduction regime implemented; an analysis of risk
transfer versus risk pricing for a specific project; commentary on termination compensation and its effect
on financeability; a lessons-learned note from an operating concession.

**The false positive.** Model familiarity. Knowing the taxonomy of PPP models is introductory material.
The competency shows in the payment mechanism — the one document that decides whether the asset earns —
and in whether the person has ever traced a deduction through to its cash effect.

### 3.4 Project contracts

**Definition.** The contract suite that makes the cash flows credible: the construction contract, the
operations and maintenance agreement, the offtake or revenue agreement, supply agreements, and the
interfaces between them. The competency is reading them as a financing package rather than as separate
documents, and finding the gaps between them.

**By level.** *Foundation* — identifies the principal contracts and their commercial purpose.
*Practitioner* — extracts and models the financial terms: liquidated damages, caps, indexation, payment
terms, availability regimes. *Professional* — identifies mismatches across the suite — a liability cap
below the loss it is meant to cover, an indexation basis that differs between revenue and cost, a force
majeure definition that leaves relief on one side of a back-to-back — and negotiates them closed.
*Leader* — sets the contractual conditions the organisation requires before it will lend or invest.

**Evidence.** A contract review memorandum identifying financial exposures with clause references; a
back-to-back gap analysis; a negotiated change that closed a mismatch you found; a model faithfully
implementing contractual mechanics.

**The false positive.** Legal review. Lawyers assess enforceability; this competency assesses financial
consequence. The distinguishing question is whether the person can say what a clause is worth in cash and
in which scenario it pays.

### 3.5 Financial close

**Definition.** Getting a transaction from agreed terms to funded: the conditions precedent, the diligence
workstreams and their sequencing, document negotiation, the closing model and its agreement between
parties, and the choreography of signing and first drawdown.

**By level.** *Foundation* — tracks conditions precedent and maintains the checklist accurately.
*Practitioner* — runs a workstream to close: manages diligence inputs, resolves routine conditions, and
maintains the closing model version control. *Professional* — runs or leads a close: sequences the
critical path of the transaction, decides which conditions can be waived or deferred and on what terms,
and holds the base-case model agreed across parties. *Leader* — decides whether a transaction is ready to
close, and stops one that is not despite the cost of delay.

**Evidence.** A conditions-precedent tracker you owned through to satisfaction; a closing checklist and
timetable you built; a documented decision to defer or waive a condition, with the mitigation; an agreed
final base-case model with version control and sign-off.

**The false positive.** Presence at closings. Attending a completed transaction is exposure. The competency
is in the critical path — knowing which condition would have delayed close and what was done about it two
weeks earlier.

### 3.6 Commercial and financial risk

**Definition.** Identifying, allocating, mitigating and pricing the risks that determine whether the cash
flows arrive: construction cost and delay, revenue and demand, counterparty credit, interest rate,
inflation, currency, refinancing, regulatory and political risk. The competency is the connection between
identifying a risk and doing something with it in the structure, the contracts or the price.

**By level.** *Foundation* — identifies the main risk categories and where each is usually allocated.
*Practitioner* — runs risk analysis for a transaction: builds sensitivities and downside cases, quantifies
exposures, and tracks mitigation into the documentation. *Professional* — decides the risk case: which
scenarios must be survived and which are tolerable, how correlated risks are treated together, and where
the residual sits after all mitigation. Says what the project cannot survive. *Leader* — sets the
institution's risk appetite for the asset class and owns the aggregate position.

**Evidence.** A downside and stress-case suite with the reasoning for each case; a risk allocation matrix
with the mitigation and residual named; a decision to price or decline a specific risk; a case where your
stress case was later approximated by events.

**The false positive.** Sensitivity tables. Flexing one variable at a time produces a grid, not a risk
view; real downsides arrive correlated, and the single-variable table is the one presentation that
guarantees they will not be seen. The competency is the combined case, and the willingness to name the
scenario in which the project fails.

### 3.7 Sustainable finance

**Definition.** Financing structures and obligations tied to environmental and social outcomes: use-of-
proceeds instruments and sustainability-linked pricing, lender E&S requirements and the diligence and
monitoring they require, transition and physical climate risk in the cash flows, and disclosure
obligations that vary by market.

**By level.** *Foundation* — describes the main instrument types and the difference between use-of-proceeds
and performance-linked structures. *Practitioner* — implements the requirements: assembles E&S diligence
inputs, models margin adjustments tied to performance indicators, and maintains reporting obligations.
*Professional* — designs the sustainability provisions so that they are measurable and meaningful:
selects indicators that are material and auditable, sets thresholds that require actual change, and
resists structures whose targets would be met by ordinary course of business. Assesses E&S risk against
recognised lender frameworks. *Leader* — sets the organisation's position, including declining
transactions that are compliant and not credible.

**Evidence.** A sustainability-linked mechanism you specified, with indicator definitions and thresholds;
an E&S diligence scope and its findings; a climate risk analysis integrated into cash flow rather than
appended; a documented rejection of a proposed indicator as unmeasurable or immaterial.

**The false positive.** Labelling. An instrument with a sustainability label, a framework document and a
second-party opinion may involve no change in behaviour whatsoever. The competency is in indicator design
and materiality — whether meeting the target requires the borrower to do something they would not
otherwise have done.

### 3.8 Islamic project finance

**Definition.** Structuring project financing consistent with Islamic finance principles — asset-backed or
asset-based structures such as ijara (lease), istisna'a (construction and manufacture), murabaha
(cost-plus sale), wakala (agency) and sukuk issuance — and running them alongside conventional tranches
where a transaction has both. The competency includes the governance interface: what Shariah approval
requires and how it constrains structuring.

**By level.** *Foundation* — describes the principal structures and how each generates a return without
interest. *Practitioner* — works within an Islamic tranche: models its payment mechanics, tracks its
documentation and maintains its distinctions from a conventional tranche. *Professional* — structures the
Islamic element of a transaction, resolves intercreditor and ranking issues between Islamic and
conventional tranches, and manages the approval process without compromising the financing's commercial
logic. *Leader* — sets the institution's approach and its relationships with Shariah governance bodies.

**Evidence.** An Islamic tranche you structured or modelled, with its mechanics documented; an
intercreditor analysis across Islamic and conventional tranches; a structuring paper responding to Shariah
board observations; a sukuk structure diagram you produced.

**The false positive.** Terminology substitution. Treating an Islamic structure as a conventional loan with
different labels misses the asset ownership, risk-sharing and permissibility constraints that determine
what may be done, and produces structures that fail at approval. The competency requires understanding why
the structure is shaped as it is, not only that it is.

## 4. The data, digital and AI group

Five competencies, forming the same chain described in *CMP-08*: analysis produces a proposal, the
workflow decides where it enters, governance constrains it, and a named human validates and owns it.

### 4.1 AI-enabled financial modelling

**Definition.** Using AI assistance within modelling work — generating or reviewing formulae, checking
structure, drafting documentation, interrogating a model in natural language, testing for errors — with a
clear position on what the model's integrity depends on and what may never be delegated.

**By level.** *Foundation* — uses approved assistance for defined tasks and checks output before use.
*Practitioner* — incorporates assistance into modelling routines: error scanning, documentation drafting,
consistency checking, with the results verified against the model itself. *Professional* — decides where
assistance is admitted and where it is excluded — typically excluding the definition of the transaction
logic, which is the part that has to be reasoned — and specifies the check that stands between assisted
output and reliance. *Leader* — sets the standard for AI-assisted modelling, including disclosure and
model-risk treatment, and owns the consequences.

**Evidence.** A documented workflow showing assisted steps and their checks; an error-detection routine
and what it found; a documented decision on which modelling tasks are not delegated; a model with an
assistance disclosure and validation record.

**The false positive.** Output volume. Producing model sections quickly with assistance and no
verification step increases model risk with excellent presentation. The competency is the check design and
the exclusions.

### 4.2 Predictive cash-flow analysis

**Definition.** Using data and quantitative technique to say something about future cash generation —
demand or usage forecasting, cost and revenue trend analysis, probabilistic distributions of CFADS,
early-warning indicators — and knowing when a data-driven forecast is inadmissible because the data does
not carry the question.

**By level.** *Foundation* — interprets a probabilistic output correctly, including its confidence and
its base. *Practitioner* — builds and runs the analysis, prepares data, tests fit, and reports central
case with dispersion. *Professional* — chooses the technique for the question, back-tests against held-out
periods, reports their own model's error and bias, and states where the analysis fails — typically for
assets without a comparable history, or where regulatory change breaks the series. *Leader* — sets what
the organisation will forecast quantitatively and owns the data investment that makes it possible.

**Evidence.** An analysis with its data basis, validation and error characteristics stated; a back-test of
your own forecasts; a documented refusal to produce a quantitative forecast where the data would not
support it, and the alternative offered.

**The false positive.** Fitting the past. High explanatory power over history says nothing about
predictive power, and a model with an impressive fit is the easiest thing in this discipline to sell to a
committee. The competency shows in the out-of-sample test.

### 4.3 Digital due diligence

**Definition.** Using data and automated tooling to run diligence better — completeness checking across
data rooms, contract term extraction and comparison, anomaly detection in historical financials, model
audit assistance — and knowing which diligence conclusions may never rest on automated extraction alone.

**By level.** *Foundation* — uses the tooling as directed and verifies extracted items against source.
*Practitioner* — runs digital diligence workstreams, reconciles extracted data to primary documents, and
maintains an audit trail from finding to source. *Professional* — designs the diligence approach: what is
automated, what sampling verifies the automation, and which findings require a human reading of the whole
document because the meaning is in the interaction between clauses. *Leader* — sets diligence standards
and owns the risk that automation created a false sense of completeness.

**Evidence.** A diligence plan distinguishing automated from manual scope with sampling rates; a
reconciliation of extracted terms to source documents with the error rate found; a finding that automated
extraction missed, and the process change that followed.

**The false positive.** Coverage claims. "All 400 contracts were reviewed" describes a process that ran,
not a conclusion that holds. The competency is in the verification design — the sample that proves the
extraction was right, and the honesty about what the sample can and cannot support.

### 4.4 Responsible AI

**Definition.** Governing AI use across the transaction lifecycle: what data may be entered into which
tool, particularly counterparty and confidential deal information; where AI-derived analysis must be
disclosed to a credit committee or investor; how bias is considered where models touch counterparty
assessment; and which uses are prohibited.

**By level.** *Foundation* — follows the policy, especially on confidential data. *Practitioner* — applies
it in live transactions, discloses assistance where required, and escalates outputs that cannot be
explained. *Professional* — sets the position for a transaction or team: data handling, disclosure
convention, explainability as a precondition of reliance, and named prohibitions. *Leader* — owns the
governance framework, its procurement criteria and its incident handling, and prohibits uses the
organisation would find profitable.

**Evidence.** A transaction-level AI use position you authored; a disclosure convention adopted in credit
or investment papers; a documented prohibition and its rationale; an incident review with the resulting
control change.

**The false positive.** Policy possession. A policy nobody has tested against a real decision is a
document. The evidence is a decision that cost something.

### 4.5 Human validation

**Definition.** Checking AI-derived or automated output before it is relied upon, and owning it as your
own work — designing a check capable of catching the error that matters, performing it, recording it, and
being answerable for what you signed. In a financing context the outputs at issue are model results,
extracted contract terms, forecast cash flows and drafted analysis.

**By level.** *Foundation* — performs the prescribed check and escalates failures. *Practitioner* —
validates against independent sources within their scope and records what was checked and found.
*Professional* — designs validation per output type: the independent source, the sample, the tolerance,
and the errors the check would not catch. Rejects output that cannot be validated in the time available.
*Leader* — sets the validation standard so it cannot be discharged by signature, and owns validated
failures as failures of the standard.

**Evidence.** A validation protocol naming source and tolerance per output type; signed validation records
including findings that stopped something; a rejection of unvalidatable output and its replacement; a
revised check following a miss.

**The false positive.** The confirmation tick. The house position is that AI proposes and the professional
disposes — which is only true where the disposal is an independent check by someone who could have caught
the error and would have said so.

## 5. Where the boundaries actually lie

**Project finance and infrastructure finance.** The first is a financing technique and a risk-allocation
discipline; the second applies it to a specific asset class with its own economics and counterparties.

**Capital structure and debt and equity.** Structure is the shape — how much, over what tenor, in what
sequence. Debt and equity are the instruments that implement the shape and behave in particular ways when
things go wrong.

**Investment appraisal and financial modelling.** Appraisal is the decision and its basis; the model is the
instrument. A flawless model can implement an incoherent appraisal basis, and usually does so invisibly.

**Bankability and the cover ratios.** The ratios are measures within the judgement. A project can present
strong ratios in a base case that no lender believes, and the competency is knowing that the base case is
the question.

**PPPs and project contracts.** A PPP or concession is an ownership and payment structure; project
contracts are the suite implementing it. The payment mechanism sits at the join and is where most value is
won or lost.

## 6. How this goes wrong

**All nineteen are claimed at leader level.** No individual has that spread; Islamic project finance and
sustainable finance in particular are specialisms that many strong practitioners have never touched. Say
so. An honest foundation-level statement costs nothing and buys credibility for the claims that matter.

**Modelling evidence is a model file with no reasoning.** A workbook demonstrates that a workbook exists.
Evidence is the check sheet, the assumption log, the audit findings and the version history.

**Cover-ratio competence is evidenced by a formula.** The formula is public. Evidence is a negotiated
CFADS definition, a binding-period analysis, or a challenge to a presentation that flattered a period.

**Transaction experience substitutes for competence.** "Worked on twelve closings" is a duration claim.
Which condition would have delayed close, and what did you do about it before it did?

**The false positive is the candidate's whole portfolio.** Checklist bankability assessments, sensitivity
tables in place of stress cases, extraction coverage in place of verification. Each of these is real work,
performed at practitioner level, presented as professional-level judgement — and an assessor will place it
correctly, so the candidate loses the credit they would have received for claiming it accurately.

**Islamic finance structures are described as conventional finance with different names.** This is the
single most common error in the set, and it is visible immediately to anyone who works in the field.

**Sustainability provisions are evidenced by the presence of a label.** Name the indicator, its
definition, its threshold and what the borrower had to change.

## 7. Checklist

For a candidate assembling evidence, or an assessor reviewing it:

- [ ] Have all nineteen competencies been addressed, including honest foundation-level statements?
- [ ] Does each have a **dated artefact** with an identifiable personal contribution?
- [ ] For modelling: is there a check sheet, an assumption log and an audit trail — not only a model?
- [ ] For appraisal: is the cash flow definition, discount rate basis and inflation/tax convention stated?
- [ ] For cover ratios: is there a CFADS definition, a binding period, and a downside at which cover fails?
- [ ] For bankability: are the two or three deciding issues named, or only a checklist?
- [ ] For risk: are there combined stress cases, not single-variable sensitivities?
- [ ] For the AI competencies: is the evidence a **decision** — where output enters, what check stands
      between it and reliance, what was excluded?
- [ ] Is human validation evidenced by a protocol and findings rather than a signature?
- [ ] Is every artefact dated, with anything older than three years flagged as historical?
