# Cost and commercial competencies in depth

> The chain from estimate to final account, and where competence actually fails along it.

**In one paragraph.** Cost and commercial competence is not a set of separate skills but one chain —
estimate, budget, commitment, accrual, actual cost, forecast, change, final account — in which each link
must reconcile to the last. This document takes the cost and commercial competencies that appear across
all three PCI credentials, sets out what distinguishes a practitioner from a professional at each link,
and shows with a worked example how a single error at one link is amplified by the time it reaches the
forecast. After reading it you should be able to identify which link in your own function is weakest and
what evidence would prove it.

**Who this is for.** Cost engineers, cost managers, quantity surveyors, commercial managers and project
controls leads; project finance analysts whose models depend on a construction cost position; and anyone
assessing competence claims in this cluster.

---

## 1. Why the cluster is one chain and not six skills

Cost and commercial work is organised in most businesses as separate roles: estimating, cost control,
commercial management, planning, finance. The competencies are assessed separately too. But the numbers
they produce are sequential, and each one inherits every error made upstream of it:

**estimate → budget → commitment → accrual → actual cost → forecast → change → final account**

A person can be flawless at their own link and still be part of a function that reports a wrong number,
because the failure is at a handoff. In practice the handoffs are where competence separates, and they are
where assessment should look:

| Handoff | The question competence answers | The failure when it is absent |
|---|---|---|
| Estimate → budget | What was in the estimate that is not in the budget, and who agreed to absorb it? | Budget set below the estimate to fit an approval, with no record of the gap |
| Budget → commitment | Is the committed value the whole obligation, including options and provisional items? | Commitments understated; the project appears to have contingency it has already spent |
| Commitment → accrual | What work has been done that has not been invoiced? | Accrual driven by invoices received rather than by progress achieved |
| Accrual → actual | Does the project cost report reconcile to the ledger, and are the differences explained? | Two cost positions in one organisation, neither owned |
| Actual → forecast | Which method extrapolates this performance, and what must be true for it to hold? | The tool's default formula, applied to unvalidated inputs |
| Forecast → change | Is agreed change in the baseline, and unagreed exposure disclosed outside it? | Change buried in the forecast, so the movement cannot be explained |
| Change → final account | Does the record substantiate the entitlement to someone who was not there? | A defensible entitlement lost for want of a contemporaneous record |

The competencies in this cluster, across the three credentials, are: cost management, earned value
management, forecasting and EAC, performance measurement and commercial and contract controls (PCL-AI);
investment appraisal, financial modelling, capital structure, debt and equity, project contracts and
commercial and financial risk (PFL-AI); business cases, procurement, contracts and commercial delivery and
cost, schedule and risk integration (PML-AI). They are defined and levelled in *CMP-03*, *CMP-04* and
*CMP-05*. This document goes underneath the definitions.

## 2. Cost management: the accrual is the competency

Most cost control work is mechanical and can be learnt in a quarter. One judgement in it cannot, and it is
the accrual: the value of work done that has not yet been invoiced.

A practitioner raises accruals from a defined process — usually a supplier statement, a goods-received
record or a progress claim. A professional decides the accrual where those inputs disagree with physical
reality, and can defend the number to a finance function that would prefer a smaller one at year end. The
specific judgements are:

- **Work done off site.** Fabrication in progress at a supplier's works represents cost incurred and, in
  most contracts, no payment obligation yet. Whether it is accrued, and on what evidence, is a decision.
- **Disputed valuations.** A contractor claims 400 units of progress; the project assesses 320. The
  accrual is not the average, and it is not zero because it is disputed. It is the project's honest
  assessment, with the difference disclosed as exposure.
- **Rework.** Cost incurred on work that will have to be done again is a real cost and a fictional
  progress. The two entries are separate, and conflating them is how a project reports on budget until the
  month the rework is discovered.
- **Cut-off.** The same rule, applied on the same date, every period. A month with a late cut-off flatters
  itself and steals from the next.

The evidence that distinguishes the levels is not the accrual value; it is the substantiation. A signed
accrual pack in which each line names its basis — progress record, supplier statement, physical
verification, assessed value with the difference disclosed — is professional-level evidence. A spreadsheet
of numbers is not, whatever the numbers are.

## 3. Earned value: the competency is validity, not arithmetic

The indices are arithmetic — see *BPG-08 — Earned value in practice* for the method. The competency
question is whether the earned value is true.

Three tests separate the levels, and an assessor can apply all three in ten minutes.

**Does the rule of credit reward work or activity?** A 0/100 rule on a six-month work package produces
five months of nothing and a month of everything, and hides the trend the system exists to reveal. A
percentage-complete rule based on the manager's opinion produces steady 8 % per month regardless of what
happened. A professional selects the technique per work type — units complete, milestone weighting, level
of effort where nothing else is honest — and can say why for each.

**Has anyone compared claimed progress to physical progress?** The single most valuable half-day in cost
control is walking the work with the progress claim in hand. A candidate who has never done this has been
operating a system, not validating one.

**What happens to level-of-effort scope?** Scope measured by time passing always earns exactly as planned
and therefore always shows perfect performance. When it is a large share of the baseline, the project's
indices are structurally optimistic. A professional knows their level-of-effort share and states it when
presenting the indices.

## 4. Forecasting: the method is the competency

An estimate at completion (EAC) is a claim about the future, and every forecasting method encodes an
assumption about how the past predicts it:

- **Performance-index extrapolation** (`EAC = BAC ÷ CPI`, equivalently `BAC × AC ÷ EV`) assumes remaining
  work will be performed at the efficiency achieved so far. Defensible when the remaining work resembles
  the completed work and the drivers of underperformance persist.
- **Remaining-work re-estimate** (`EAC = AC + a bottom-up estimate of what is left`) assumes the past
  performance does not predict the future — typically because the cause was one-off, or because the
  remaining work is different in kind. Defensible when you can say what changed.
- **Composite approaches** apply different treatments to different scopes: index-based on the
  like-for-like remainder, re-estimated on the parts that have changed.

Competence is visible in three habits, all of which leave evidence:

1. **The method is chosen and recorded, not inherited.** A dated note naming the method, the alternatives
   rejected and the reason is professional-level evidence and takes ten minutes to write.
2. **The assumption is stated with the answer.** "EAC 4.4 million, assuming the remaining civils are
   performed at the current cost performance index of 0.909 and no further scope is instructed" is a
   forecast. "EAC 4.4 million" is a number.
3. **The forecast is back-tested.** A professional knows whether their own forecasts have historically run
   low, and by roughly how much. This is the cheapest credibility available to a controls professional and
   almost nobody does it.

*BPG-09 — Estimate at completion* owns method selection in full, and *TPL-08* provides the scenario
comparison sheet.

## 5. Commercial control: the record is the competency

Commercial competence is often assessed by contractual knowledge, which is the wrong test. Contract
knowledge is available in the document. What is not available later is the record of what happened.

The professional-level behaviours, stated as artefacts:

- **A notice issued within its period.** Most contracts impose time limits on notification, and an
  entitlement that was real becomes unrecoverable when a date passes. The competency is a system that
  triggers the notice, not a memory that recalls the clause.
- **Contemporaneous records that a stranger can use.** Allocation sheets, delay records, photographs with
  dates and locations, minutes recording instructions. The test: could someone who was not on the project
  reconstruct entitlement from this file in eighteen months, when the people have left?
- **Exposure kept outside the forecast and visible.** An unagreed variation, a claim in preparation and a
  potential liability are exposure. Burying them in the forecast destroys the ability to explain a
  movement; omitting them entirely misleads. The professional reports the forecast and the exposure range
  separately, and says which is which.
- **A position that concedes what is not defensible.** Competence includes recognising a losing argument
  early and settling it, rather than carrying a fictional entitlement in the commercial report for a year.

*BPG-11 — Change orders and variations* and *BPG-12 — Claims and extension of time* own the mechanics.

## 6. The finance-side members of the cluster

For PFL-AI candidates the same chain runs through different artefacts. The construction cost position
feeds the drawdown profile; the contract suite determines whether a cost overrun is borne by the project
or by a contractor; and the commercial exposure that a controls function reports outside the forecast is
precisely what a lender's technical adviser is looking for. Two boundaries matter for assessment:

- **Financial modelling competence is not cost management competence.** A faultless model implementing an
  unvalidated cost position produces confident, wrong cover ratios. The competency to interrogate the
  underlying cost position is a separate one and is the more valuable of the two.
- **Investment appraisal competence is a decision competence.** It is evidenced by the statement of what
  is being discounted, at what rate and whose return it represents, not by the presence of a net present
  value in a paper. See *CMP-04* §2.4.

## 7. How this goes wrong

**The budget is set below the estimate and the gap is not recorded.** An approval is granted at a number
the estimator did not support, the difference is absorbed silently, and eight months later the overrun is
attributed to poor cost control. The competency is the recorded reconciliation between estimate and
budget, produced at the time by someone who was willing to have the conversation.

**Accruals are driven by invoices.** The cost report then measures the supplier's administrative
efficiency rather than the project's progress, and it looks best in the months when suppliers are slowest.

**Two cost positions coexist.** The project cost report and the general ledger differ, both are quoted in
different meetings, and nobody owns the reconciliation. The competent response is a monthly reconciliation
with each difference explained and aged — not a campaign to make the two systems identical, which will
fail.

**The forecast method changes without disclosure.** A project switches from index-based to a re-estimate
in the month the index-based number became unwelcome. The change may be entirely legitimate; not
disclosing it is not. State the method every period, and flag the period in which it changed.

**Exposure is either buried or omitted.** Buried, it destroys the explanation of forecast movement.
Omitted, it misleads. Separate reporting of forecast and exposure range is a professional-level habit and
the fastest way for a candidate to demonstrate this competency.

**Contemporaneous records exist but are unusable.** Photographs without dates, allocation sheets without
locations, minutes that record attendance rather than instructions. The record is only worth what a
stranger can reconstruct from it.

**Earned value is validated against the system that produced it.** Checking that the report agrees with
the tool confirms the export worked. Validation means comparing claimed progress to something outside the
system — physical inspection, an independent count, a supplier's own records.

**Cost competence is assessed on report quality.** The report is the last link and the easiest to make
attractive. Assess at the accrual, the reconciliation and the forecast note.

## 8. Worked example: how far one accrual error travels

*Illustrative figures.* A single control account, currency-neutral cost units (cu), status at month 8 of a
20-month scope. Monetary results rounded to the nearest 1,000 cu; indices shown to three decimal places
but computed from unrounded values.

**Reported position at month 8**

| Item | Value (cu) |
|---|---|
| Budget at completion (BAC) | 4,000,000 |
| Planned value to date (PV) | 2,400,000 |
| Earned value to date (EV) | 2,200,000 |
| Actual cost to date (AC) | 2,420,000 |

Cost performance index: `CPI = EV ÷ AC = 2,200,000 ÷ 2,420,000 = 0.909`
Schedule performance index: `SPI = EV ÷ PV = 2,200,000 ÷ 2,400,000 = 0.917`
Cost variance: `CV = EV − AC = 2,200,000 − 2,420,000 = −220,000`

Performance-index forecast, computed without rounding by using the equivalent form
`EAC = BAC × AC ÷ EV`:

`EAC = 4,000,000 × 2,420,000 ÷ 2,200,000 = 4,000,000 × 1.100 = 4,400,000`
`VAC = BAC − EAC = 4,000,000 − 4,400,000 = −400,000`

**The commercial layer.** Three items sit outside the baseline: variations agreed at 150,000 but not yet
incorporated; two instructed but unpriced variations assessed by the project at 90,000, against the
contractor's submitted 140,000; and an intimated extension-of-time claim with no cost quantification.

Incorporating only the agreed variations gives a revised BAC of `4,000,000 + 150,000 = 4,150,000`. The
forecast now depends on an assumption that must be stated:

- *If the variation work is assumed to perform to plan:* `EAC = 4,400,000 + 150,000 = 4,550,000`
- *If it is assumed to perform at the current efficiency:*
  `EAC = 4,150,000 × 2,420,000 ÷ 2,200,000 = 4,150,000 × 1.100 = 4,565,000`

The difference is `4,565,000 − 4,550,000 = 15,000`, which is simply `150,000 × 0.100`. Small here, and not
small when the variation value is a quarter of the budget. **Reporting either number without naming the
assumption is the failure.**

Taking the second treatment as the reported forecast:
`VAC = 4,150,000 − 4,565,000 = −415,000`

The unpriced variations are **not** in that number. They are disclosed as exposure of 90,000 to 140,000,
unresolved, plus an unquantified time-related cost. Assumptions the answer depends on: no further scope
instructed; the current efficiency persists across the remaining work; the disputed valuation is settled
at the project's assessment.

**Now the error.** Suppose the month-8 accrual was understated by 60,000 — one supplier's off-site
fabrication omitted because no invoice had arrived. The true actual cost is
`2,420,000 + 60,000 = 2,480,000`.

`CPI = 2,200,000 ÷ 2,480,000 = 0.887`
`EAC = 4,150,000 × 2,480,000 ÷ 2,200,000 = 4,150,000 × 1.127 = 4,678,000` (to the nearest 1,000 cu)

The forecast moves by `4,678,000 − 4,565,000 = 113,000` — roughly **1.9 times** the size of the original
error. That multiple is not a coincidence: an index-based forecast multiplies an error in actual cost by
`revised BAC ÷ EV`, here `4,150,000 ÷ 2,200,000 = 1.886`, and `60,000 × 1.886 = 113,000` to the nearest
1,000 cu.

The point for competence assessment: the person who owns the accrual is usually junior, the accrual is
usually the least reviewed number in the pack, and the forecast that the board argues about for an hour
inherits its error amplified. This is why cost management competence is assessed at the accrual and not at
the report.

## 9. Checklist

Take this into a cost and commercial review, or into an assessment of evidence in this cluster.

- [ ] Is there a written reconciliation between the estimate and the approved budget, with the gap named?
- [ ] Are commitments complete, including options, provisional items and unpriced instructions?
- [ ] Does each accrual line name its basis, and can any line be traced to physical evidence?
- [ ] Is the cut-off date the same as last period, and is adherence recorded?
- [ ] Does the project cost report reconcile to the ledger, with differences explained and aged?
- [ ] What proportion of the baseline is level-of-effort scope, and is that stated when indices are shown?
- [ ] Has anyone compared claimed progress to physical progress this period?
- [ ] Does the forecast note state the method, the alternatives rejected and the assumptions?
- [ ] If the method changed this period, is the change disclosed?
- [ ] Are agreed changes in the baseline and unagreed exposure reported separately as a range?
- [ ] Have all notices due under the contract been issued within their periods?
- [ ] Could a stranger substantiate the current entitlement position from the record file alone?
