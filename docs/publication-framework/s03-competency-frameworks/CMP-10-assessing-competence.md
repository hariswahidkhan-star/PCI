# Assessing competence — evidence, rubrics and moderation

> How a competency claim is tested, why rubrics fail, and what moderation is actually diagnosing.

**In one paragraph.** An assessment is an inference: from a sample of evidence, to a claim about what a
person can do in situations nobody has observed. It can fail in two directions with very different costs.
This document sets out the evidence types and what each can and cannot show, how to write a rubric
descriptor that two assessors read the same way, the difference between compensatory and conjunctive
scoring and when each is legitimate, how moderation works and what its disagreements actually diagnose, and
why self-assessment on its own carries no assessment weight — together with the three things it is
genuinely good for.

**Who this is for.** Anyone who assesses competence and has to defend the decision: certification
assessors, heads of function running internal competency schemes, and employers whose promotion and
deployment decisions rest on a rating someone produced.

---

## 1. What an assessment is claiming, and how it fails

Every competence decision has the same shape. A person's capability across an entire working life is
unobservable. A sample of it is observed — some artefacts, an examination, a conversation — and from that
sample an inference is drawn about behaviour in situations that were not sampled.

Two properties determine whether the inference holds.

**Validity** — is the evidence about the thing being claimed? A written examination on forecasting methods
is valid evidence of knowledge of forecasting methods. It is weak evidence of whether the candidate will
hold a forecast under commercial pressure, because that situation is not in the sample. Most assessment
failures are validity failures wearing the clothes of rigour: the instrument is precise, consistent and
measuring something adjacent to the claim.

**Reliability** — would the same evidence produce the same decision with a different assessor, on a
different day? An unreliable process is unfair in a way that is invisible to everyone inside it, because
each assessor believes their own judgement to be the standard.

The two failure directions have asymmetric costs, and the asymmetry should be decided deliberately rather
than absorbed by default:

- A **false pass** puts an unqualified person into a role where the harm is borne by third parties — a
  project, a client, a lender, a workforce — and it devalues the credential for everyone who holds it.
- A **false fail** harms one identified individual, who is usually in front of you, and who will appeal.

Because the false fail is visible and the false pass is not, assessment systems drift toward passing. The
counterweight is not severity; it is written standards, evidence rules and moderation.

## 2. Evidence types, and what each can carry

The hierarchy is set out in *CMP-02* §4. Here is what each type does under examination, and how each is
gamed.

| Evidence type | Carries | Cannot carry | Typical failure | Control |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Work product authored by the candidate | Method choice, judgement in real conditions, standard of output | Whether the conditions were hard; sole authorship | Team output presented as personal | Require the reasoning to be visible, and probe it in discussion |
| Work product contributed to | Capability within a defined scope | Ownership of decisions made by others | Contribution overstated in retrospect | Require identifiable contribution recorded at the time |
| Structured professional discussion | Depth behind the artefact; reasoning under questioning | Anything the questioner did not probe | Interview skill rewarded over competence | Question from the candidate's own artefacts only; use a common question frame |
| Direct observation | Behaviour and interaction, which artefacts never capture | Anything outside the observed window | Performance for the observer | Observe routine work, not set-piece events |
| Attestation by a named referee | Corroboration of scope, duration and standing | Detail; independence from the referee's judgement | Reciprocal or reputational attestation | Require the referee's relationship, period and specific observation |
| Knowledge examination | Applied knowledge and reasoning against a common standard, reliably and at scale | Behaviour under real pressure | Treated as sufficient on its own | Combine with evidence types above |
| Self-report | A map of where to look | Capability | Uniformly favourable | Use for routing only — see §6 |

Two rules that resolve most disputes. **Where types conflict, the higher-ranked type wins** unless there
is a stated reason to prefer the lower. And **absence of evidence is not evidence of absence** — it is
absence of evidence, and the assessment record should say "not evidenced" rather than assigning a low
level. The distinction matters at appeal, and it is the single most common recording error.

## 3. Writing a rubric that two people read the same way

Most rubrics fail in one of four ways, and all four are visible on the page before anyone is assessed.

**Adjectival ladders.** "Basic / good / strong / excellent understanding." Nothing is described; the
assessor supplies the meaning, and two assessors supply different meanings.

**Global impression.** One overall rating for a whole competency area. It correlates with how articulate
the candidate was, and it cannot be defended clause by clause at appeal.

**Double-barrelled criteria.** "Selects an appropriate forecasting method **and** communicates it to
stakeholders." A candidate who does the first well and the second badly cannot be scored, and different
assessors resolve the conflict differently.

**Unbounded top levels.** "Exceptional insight." Nobody can say what would satisfy it, so it is awarded on
impression or never awarded at all.

The construction that works has three parts in every descriptor: **an observable action, a condition, and
a standard.**

> *Action:* selects a forecasting method — *condition:* where procedure does not prescribe one —
> *standard:* and records the method, the alternatives rejected and the assumptions the answer depends on.

Four supporting rules:

1. **One criterion, one judgement.** Split anything containing "and" that could be met by halves.
2. **Every level is bounded above and below.** An assessor must be able to say why this is a 3 and not a 4.
3. **Include an insufficient-evidence outcome distinct from the lowest level.** Otherwise "nothing
   submitted" is scored as "foundation", which is a different claim.
4. **Write the descriptors so that the difference between adjacent levels is a difference in the work, not
   in the adverb.** If levels 2 and 3 differ only by "consistently", the rubric will not moderate.

### 3.1 A worked rubric: *Forecasting and EAC* (PCL-AI competency 5)

Estimate at completion (EAC). Assessed from candidate-authored artefacts and a structured discussion of
them.

| Level | Descriptor | Evidence that satisfies it |
|---|---|---|
| **0 — Insufficient evidence** | No artefact authored or materially contributed to by the candidate, or the contribution cannot be identified. | — |
| **1 — Foundation** | Computes a forecast using the method prescribed by procedure, and identifies inputs that appear inconsistent with the project position. | A completed forecast to procedure; a query raised on an input that was subsequently corrected. |
| **2 — Practitioner** | Produces the periodic forecast for an owned scope, selecting from the methods the procedure permits, and explains the movement since the previous period in terms of its drivers. | Consecutive periodic forecasts with movement analysis; a forecast relied on without correction by the next reviewer. |
| **3 — Professional** | Selects the forecast method where procedure does not prescribe one; records the method, the alternatives rejected and the assumptions on which the answer depends; states a range where a range is warranted; and maintains the forecast under challenge, changing it on evidence and not on pressure. | A dated method-selection note; a forecast revised in a period when no external event forced it, with the basis; a documented challenge and its resolution. |
| **4 — Leader** | Sets the forecasting standard applied by others across projects — method rules, review cadence, treatment of undisclosed exposure — and can demonstrate the standard operating, including a case where it produced an unwelcome answer that was nonetheless reported. | An issued forecasting standard in use, with the candidate's authorship; back-test evidence across projects; a documented escalation route used at least once. |

Note what is doing the work: level 3 is distinguished from level 2 by **who chose the method and whether
the choice is recorded**, and level 4 from level 3 by **whether others apply the rule**. Neither
distinction requires an assessor's opinion about quality.

## 4. Scoring: compensatory, conjunctive, and the choice between them

**Compensatory** scoring allows strength in one criterion to offset weakness in another — typically an
average across criteria against a threshold. **Conjunctive** scoring requires a minimum on each criterion
independently; no amount of excellence elsewhere compensates.

Compensatory scoring is appropriate where the competencies are genuinely substitutable in the role.
Conjunctive scoring is required where a specific weakness is unacceptable regardless of other strengths —
and in this framework at least two areas qualify: **responsible AI** and **human validation or human
accountability**. A person who is outstanding at analysis and does not validate output is not a net
positive, and an average conceals exactly that.

The practical construction most schemes should use is **conjunctive on a named subset, compensatory on the
remainder**, with the subset published in advance. §8 shows how a compensatory average conceals a profile
that no assessor would accept if they saw it laid out.

**How PCI's own examination scores, stated plainly.** It is criterion-referenced, not graded on a curve:
candidates are measured against a standard, not against each other, so a strong cohort does not raise the
bar and a weak one does not lower it. The examination is 90 minutes, four-option multiple choice with a
single best answer, written across recall, application and analysis and weighted toward application, and
results are returned immediately with a domain breakdown. The pass mark configured in the platform is
**65 %**, and the definitive standard will be set by a **modified-Angoff standard-setting study** — a
method in which subject-matter experts judge, item by item, the probability that a minimally competent
candidate answers correctly, and the aggregate of those judgements sets the mark. **The number of items is
not published, because it has not been decided**; it will be confirmed by a formal job-task analysis.
*EXB-05* and *EXB-06* own these two studies.

## 5. Moderation: what it is for and what it finds

Moderation is not a second opinion. It is a control on the assessment process, and its outputs diagnose
the instrument at least as often as the candidate.

The mechanics that make it work:

- **Independent first.** Two assessors score without seeing each other's marks. A "review" of an existing
  mark anchors to it and detects almost nothing.
- **A defined sample.** Full double-marking is rarely affordable. A stated proportion is moderated, plus
  every decision near a threshold, every fail, and every appeal
  [CONFIRM: moderation sampling rate for PCI competence assessments].
- **Reconciliation by evidence, not by rank.** Disagreements are resolved by both assessors returning to
  the artefact and to the rubric. Where they cannot agree, a moderator or chair rules, and the ruling is
  recorded with its reasoning.
- **Disagreement is analysed, not just resolved.** Every disagreement has one of four causes: the rubric is
  ambiguous, an assessor is miscalibrated, the portfolio is disorganised so the two assessors read
  different evidence, or the case is genuinely borderline. Only the last is about the candidate, and only
  the last requires no corrective action.
- **Calibration before the cycle.** Assessors score the same anonymised sample and discuss divergence
  before assessing anyone real [CONFIRM: assessor qualification and calibration requirements].
- **Conflict of interest declared and acted on.** An assessor does not assess a person they manage, have
  trained, have been employed alongside recently, or have a commercial relationship with. Separation of
  training from assessment is a founding principle of personnel certification and is not a formality —
  it is what makes the decision worth anything to a third party.
- **Records retained.** The evidence considered, the rubric applied, the levels awarded, the disagreements
  and their resolution. An assessment that cannot be reconstructed cannot be appealed fairly, and
  *CER-07 — Results, scoring, appeals and complaints* depends on it.

## 6. Why self-assessment alone carries no weight

This is not a claim about honesty. It follows from three structural features of the act, none of which
improves with better intentions:

**There is no external referent.** The rater compares their work to their own experience of the work. A
person whose entire exposure to forecasting has been one organisation's method rates themselves against
that method, and cannot rate themselves against practice they have never seen.

**The rater has an interest in the outcome.** Where a self-assessment feeds a promotion, a fee or a
credential, the rating is a submission in a process, and submissions are optimised.

**The specific failure is invisible from inside.** To know that your method selection is inadequate you
must know what an adequate one looks like. A person who has never seen a method-selection note recorded
does not experience its absence as a gap; they experience their own practice as normal. This is why
self-assessment is least reliable exactly where it would be most useful — at the boundary between
practitioner and professional.

Self-assessment nevertheless has three genuine uses, and a scheme that discards it loses them:

1. **Routing.** It tells an assessor where to look and lets a candidate assemble evidence efficiently.
2. **Structuring a professional discussion.** "You rated yourself professional here — show me the artefact"
   is a productive opening, and the answer is informative whichever way it goes.
3. **The gap between self and manager rating.** The disagreement is the signal. A large gap in either
   direction is worth an hour of conversation, and the direction tells you which hour it is: a person
   rating themselves well above their manager may be misreading the standard, and a person rating
   themselves well below may be evidencing something that is never seen.

Used for those three purposes, self-assessment is valuable. Used as a rating that stands on its own, it
produces a number that reflects confidence and role identity, and it should not be recorded as an
assessment outcome.

## 7. How this goes wrong

**The rubric is written after the assessments.** Standards are then reverse-engineered from decisions
already made, which is unappealable and, when it surfaces, indefensible.

**"Not evidenced" is recorded as "foundation".** Two different findings. One says the candidate cannot do
it; the other says nobody looked, or the candidate did not submit. Conflating them produces both unfair
fails and unjustified passes, and it destroys the diagnostic value of the record.

**Moderation is a signature.** A moderator who reviews rather than independently scores anchors to the
first mark. Agreement rates then look excellent and mean nothing.

**Disagreement is resolved by seniority.** The more senior assessor's view prevails without returning to
the evidence. The scheme has now moved from criterion-referenced to person-referenced, and the analysis in
§5 that would have found the rubric defect never happens.

**A compensatory average hides an unacceptable profile.** See §8. Averages are convenient and they conceal
precisely the pattern a conjunctive rule exists to catch.

**Assessors are never calibrated.** Each is internally consistent and they differ from one another by half
a level, which is invisible until a candidate assessed by the strict one appeals and cites a colleague
assessed by the lenient one.

**Interview performance is scored as competence.** The structured discussion probes artefacts. When it
becomes a general conversation, it measures fluency, which the assessment does not claim to certify and
which correlates with things a fair process must not select for.

**The assessment cannot be reconstructed.** No record of what evidence was considered against which
criterion. The first appeal exposes it, and the scheme's credibility does not recover quickly.

**The examination is treated as the whole assessment.** A knowledge examination is reliable, scalable and
valid for what it samples. It does not sample behaviour under pressure. Scheme designers who forget this
end up certifying knowledge and describing it as competence.

## 8. Worked example: a moderation reconciliation

*Illustrative figures.* Two assessors independently rate one candidate against five competencies on the
four-level scale, coded 1 = foundation, 2 = practitioner, 3 = professional, 4 = leader.

| # | Competency | Assessor A | Assessor B | Difference (A − B) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cost management | 3 | 3 | 0 |
| 2 | Forecasting and EAC | 3 | 2 | +1 |
| 3 | Predictive analytics | 2 | 2 | 0 |
| 4 | Project controls governance | 4 | 3 | +1 |
| 5 | Human validation | 2 | 3 | −1 |

**Agreement measures**

Exact agreement: `2 criteria of 5 = 40 %` (criteria 1 and 3).
Adjacent agreement, meaning within one level: `5 of 5 = 100 %`.
Mean absolute difference: `(0 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 1) ÷ 5 = 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6 levels`.
Mean signed difference: `(0 + 1 + 0 + 1 − 1) ÷ 5 = 1 ÷ 5 = +0.2 levels` — assessor A is running
two-tenths of a level higher than assessor B on this candidate, which is a calibration observation and not
yet a finding, because one candidate is not a sample.

**Reconciliation, and what each disagreement diagnosed**

- *Criterion 2.* Assessor A cited a dated method-selection note; assessor B had not seen it, because it sat
  in an appendix not referenced from the competency claim. Agreed at **3**. Cause: **portfolio
  organisation**. Action: require an evidence index mapping each artefact to the competencies it supports.
- *Criterion 4.* Assessor A treated authorship of a governance procedure as leader level. Assessor B applied
  the rubric, which requires the rule to be in use by others and a failure to have been owned; neither was
  evidenced. Agreed at **3**. Cause: **assessor calibration**. Action: calibration note issued to all
  assessors with this rubric row as the example.
- *Criterion 5.* Assessor B credited a validation record that assessor A read as a bare sign-off. On
  reading it together, the record named a date and a reviewer but not what was checked against what. Agreed
  at **2**. Cause: **rubric ambiguity** — the descriptor said "records the validation" without stating the
  minimum content. Action: rubric amended to require the independent source, the coverage and the finding.

Agreed profile: **3, 3, 2, 3, 2**.

**Why the scoring rule matters.** Suppose the decision rule is conjunctive on criteria 1, 2 and 4 at
professional (3) or above, and practitioner (2) or above on the rest. The agreed profile meets it.

Under a compensatory rule instead — a mean of at least 2.6 — the same profile passes:
`(3 + 3 + 2 + 3 + 2) ÷ 5 = 13 ÷ 5 = 2.6`. But so does the profile `4, 4, 1, 3, 1`:
`(4 + 4 + 1 + 3 + 1) ÷ 5 = 13 ÷ 5 = 2.6` — a candidate at foundation level in predictive analytics **and**
in human validation, passing on the strength of two leader-level ratings elsewhere. That is the profile a
conjunctive rule exists to stop, and the average made it invisible.

**The finding that matters.** Of three disagreements, one was about the candidate and two were about the
assessment system. That ratio is normal in a young scheme, and it is the reason moderation output is
reviewed by whoever owns the rubric — not filed with the candidate's result.

## 9. Checklist

Before running an assessment, and before defending one:

- [ ] Is every criterion written as an observable action, a condition and a standard?
- [ ] Does any criterion contain an "and" that could be half met?
- [ ] Is each level bounded above and below, so an assessor can say why not the next one up?
- [ ] Is there an insufficient-evidence outcome distinct from the lowest level, and is it being used?
- [ ] Which criteria are conjunctive, and was that published before the assessment?
- [ ] Do the evidence types actually sample the claim, or only the knowledge adjacent to it?
- [ ] Did two assessors score independently before comparing?
- [ ] Are moderation disagreements classified by cause — rubric, calibration, portfolio, genuine borderline?
- [ ] Does the moderation output go to whoever owns the rubric?
- [ ] Were assessors calibrated on a common sample this cycle?
- [ ] Are conflicts of interest declared, and is anyone assessing someone they trained or manage?
- [ ] Could a third party reconstruct this decision from the record in twelve months?
- [ ] Is any self-assessment being recorded as an outcome rather than used for routing?
