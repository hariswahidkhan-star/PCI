# Behavioural and leadership competencies

> The behaviours that decide whether a correct number changes anything.

**In one paragraph.** A correct forecast that nobody acts on has changed nothing. This document sets out
the five behaviours that determine whether a controls, finance or delivery professional is listened to —
raising a number that is unwelcome, holding a position under pressure and releasing it on evidence, stating
what is not known, making a trade-off legible rather than winning an argument, and owning delegated and
AI-derived output — with the observable indicators and counter-indicators for each, the escalation ladder
that supports them, and the organisational conditions without which none of them survives.

**Who this is for.** Project controls managers, cost and planning leads, project and programme managers,
and the executives who decide whether these behaviours are rewarded or quietly penalised in their
organisations.

---

## 1. Behaviour is not a separate framework

The Institute does not publish a behavioural competency framework alongside the technical ones, and the
omission is deliberate. Behaviour is inside the published sets, because it is inseparable from the
technical work.

It appears explicitly. PML-AI names *project leadership*, *stakeholder leadership*, *governance* and
*human accountability*. PCL-AI names *project controls governance* and *human validation*; PFL-AI names
*human validation*. These are behavioural competencies with technical content, not soft additions.

It also appears implicitly, in every level descriptor at professional and above. *CMP-02* §2.3 defines
professional level as deciding the method where procedure is silent **and defending the decision to
someone who wanted a different answer**. That second clause is a behaviour. The scale cannot be climbed by
technical capability alone, because the level is defined partly by conduct under pressure.

There is a structural reason this matters more in project controls, project finance and delivery
management than in many disciplines. The output of the work is a number that constrains other people's
plans, budgets, bonuses and reputations. The number is produced by someone who usually does not hold
authority over the project, is often junior to the people it constrains, and frequently has to deliver it
to the person whose performance it describes. That configuration puts consistent pressure in one
direction: the forecast is asked to be lower, the risk to be smaller, the date to be earlier. Nobody has
ever been asked to make a forecast worse.

## 2. The five behaviours

Each is stated with observable indicators, counter-indicators, and the evidence that would demonstrate it.
Indicators are observable actions, not attitudes; nobody can assess "integrity", and everybody can assess
whether a forecast moved before or after the client found out.

### 2.1 Raising a number that will be unwelcome

**What it is.** Reporting a deterioration at the point you are confident of it, rather than at the point it
becomes undeniable — while there is still time for the information to be useful.

**Observable indicators.** The forecast moved in a period when no external event forced it. The escalation
is dated earlier than the event that confirmed it. The person raised the issue in a written record, not
only in a corridor. They brought the size of the problem and its basis, not a warning that things "may be
tight". They said what they wanted done about it.

**Counter-indicators.** Forecasts that move only in the month a client audit is announced. Deterioration
disclosed in a footnote or a verbal aside. A record showing the person "flagged concerns" without a number
or a date. A pattern of large single-period corrections, which is the signature of accumulated withholding.

**Evidence.** A dated forecast note showing an upward revision and its basis, together with the outcome. A
report qualification in writing. An escalation email with its date, and the subsequent record of what
happened.

### 2.2 Holding a position under pressure — and releasing it on evidence

**What it is.** Two behaviours that only count together. Maintaining a position when it is challenged by
someone with more authority and no new evidence; and changing it promptly and publicly when new evidence
arrives.

**Observable indicators.** The person can state, in advance, what would change their mind — a specific
piece of evidence, not a threshold of pressure. When challenged, they ask what is being asserted rather
than restating their number louder. When they concede, they say what changed and update the record. When
they do not concede, they offer a route to resolve it: an independent check, a sample verification, a
joint walk of the work.

**Counter-indicators.** Positions that change after a meeting with a senior person and no new information,
with no record of why. Positions that never change, which is not integrity but a different failure — the
person has stopped processing evidence. Conceding privately while maintaining the original position in the
record, or the reverse.

**Evidence.** A challenge and its resolution, documented on both sides. A position reversed with the
reason recorded contemporaneously. A dispute escalated to an independent check, and the outcome.

### 2.3 Saying what you do not know

**What it is.** Distinguishing, out loud and in the record, between what has been verified, what has been
assumed, and what is unknown — and saying what it would take to know it.

**Observable indicators.** Reports carry an explicit assumptions section that changes between periods
rather than being copied forward. The person answers a question with "I don't know; I can find out by
Thursday" and then does. Ranges are used where the situation warrants a range, with the drivers of the
spread named. Unverified inputs are labelled as unverified in the document, not just in the author's mind.

**Counter-indicators.** Confident single-point answers to questions the data cannot support. An
assumptions section identical to last quarter's. Precision used as a proxy for confidence — a forecast
quoted to the nearest unit when the underlying accrual is an estimate.

**Evidence.** Reports with live assumption registers. A documented refusal to provide a number on
inadequate data, with the alternative offered and the date it was delivered. A stated range with its
basis, later compared to outturn.

### 2.4 Making the trade-off legible rather than winning the argument

**What it is.** Presenting a decision-maker with the actual choice and its consequences, in a form they can
decide from, rather than advocating for the answer you prefer and treating disagreement as a failure of
theirs.

**Observable indicators.** Options are presented with consequences on the dimensions the decision-maker
cares about — cost, date, scope, risk exposure — not only the one the analyst cares about. The
recommendation is stated and separated from the analysis, so the decision-maker can accept the analysis and
reject the recommendation. The person can articulate the strongest version of the option they are advising
against. After the decision, they implement it and record any residual concern once, rather than relitigating
it monthly.

**Counter-indicators.** One real option and two straw men. Analysis that stops precisely where it would
have become inconvenient. Escalation of a decision that has already been properly made, in the hope of a
different answer. A residual concern raised for the first time after the outcome is known.

**Evidence.** An options paper with genuine alternatives and comparable consequence statements. A decision
record showing the recommendation, the decision taken and any recorded dissent. A decision you disagreed
with, implemented, and whose consequences you tracked honestly.

### 2.5 Owning the output, including what a system produced

**What it is.** Being the person answerable for a number, including when the analysis came from a model, a
tool or a team — and declining to release what you cannot stand behind.

**Observable indicators.** The person can explain a figure produced by an AI-assisted or automated process
in their own words, including its limitations. They can name what they checked and against what. They have
declined to issue something within the reporting deadline because it could not be verified, and said so
rather than issuing it silently. They do not attribute an error to a tool or to a junior colleague in a
forum where neither is present.

**Counter-indicators.** "The model produced it." "That comes from the system." A signature on a document
whose method its signatory cannot describe. Errors traced downward in public and credit traced upward in
private.

**Evidence.** A validation record naming what was checked and found. A withheld or qualified deliverable
with the reason recorded. A post-incident review in which the person owned a failure in a process they
designed. See *CMP-08* §7 for the validation mechanics and *CMP-05* §4.9 for accountability at leader
level.

## 3. The escalation ladder

Behaviour needs a route, or it becomes a choice between silence and resignation. The ladder below moves
only when the previous rung has been tried and recorded, and each rung leaves evidence.

1. **Fix it in the record.** Correct the number, note the correction and its basis. Most issues stop here.
2. **Raise it with the person who owns the number.** Directly, with the basis, and with what you want
   changed. Confirm the conversation in writing the same day — not as a threat, but because a
   contemporaneous note is the only version that survives.
3. **Qualify the report in writing.** If a report must be issued and you cannot sign it cleanly, say what
   you could not verify and what the number excludes. §7 gives the wording. This is the most
   under-used rung on the ladder and the most powerful, because it is factual, proportionate and
   permanent.
4. **Escalate to the next governance tier.** The project board, the steering committee, or the function's
   head — whichever the governance framework names. Bring the number, the basis, the attempts already
   made, and the specific decision you are asking for.
5. **Escalate to the accountable officer, formally.** In writing, with the record. At this rung the matter
   has stopped being a disagreement about a number and has become a governance failure.
6. **Treat it as a conduct matter.** Where a number is being misstated knowingly, or where you are being
   instructed to misstate one, this is no longer a controls question. *ETH-01 — The PCI code of ethics and
   professional conduct* and *ETH-03 — Reporting numbers under pressure* own this ground, including the
   protections and the obligations.

Two rules govern the ladder. **Proportionality:** starting at rung 4 for a rung-2 problem destroys the
credibility you will need later. **Documentation at every rung:** the record is not for a tribunal, it is
for the ordinary case where three months later everyone remembers the conversation differently.

## 4. The conditions that make these behaviours possible

An organisation cannot certify a behaviour it punishes, and most organisations punish these behaviours
without deciding to. Four conditions determine whether the behaviours in §2 are available:

- **A forecast can move without someone being blamed for the movement.** Where a deteriorating forecast is
  treated as the forecaster's failure, forecasts stop deteriorating. The observable test: when a forecast
  worsened last quarter, was the first question *what changed?* or *who allowed this?*
- **The controls function reports somewhere other than solely to the person whose performance it
  measures.** Complete dependence on the project manager for appraisal, allocation and progression makes
  independent reporting a personal-cost decision every month. A dotted line to a functional head is a
  structural control, not an organisational preference.
- **There is time to verify.** A reporting cycle that leaves two days between data cut-off and board pack
  makes physical verification impossible, and then treats its absence as an individual failing.
- **Bad news is received, not punished, in the forum where it is delivered.** People calibrate to what
  happens to the last person who raised something. Everyone in the room learns from that, and they learn
  it in one meeting.

For an employer using this framework, the practical consequence is uncomfortable: if you are assessing
staff against §2 while running an organisation that fails §4, you are assessing their appetite for
personal cost rather than their competence.

## 5. Evidencing behaviour

Behaviour is evidenced badly by testimonial and well by contemporaneous record. Four artefact types carry
almost all of it:

- **The dated note.** A forecast note, an escalation email, a file note of a conversation, written the same
  day. Its value is entirely in its date.
- **The qualification.** A written statement in a deliverable of what could not be verified or what is
  excluded. It is public, permanent and hard to reinterpret later.
- **The reversal.** A position changed with the reason recorded, which evidences §2.2 more strongly than
  any number of held positions.
- **The reconciliation over time.** What you said in month 6 set against what happened by month 12. Very
  few candidates offer this, and it is the single most persuasive behavioural evidence available, in both
  directions: a professional who was wrong and says so has demonstrated more than one who was right.

A behavioural claim with no contemporaneous artefact is a story. Not necessarily untrue — but not
assessable, and *CMP-10* §2 explains why an assessor cannot give it weight.

## 6. How this goes wrong

**Behaviour is assessed as personality.** "Confident communicator", "team player", "strong presence" —
none of which is observable in the sense required, and all of which correlate with things a competency
framework must not select for. Assess actions with dates.

**Escalation is confused with courage.** Escalating everything is not a behavioural strength; it is a
failure of the judgement in §3, and it exhausts the credibility needed for the escalation that matters.

**Holding a position is confused with being right.** A person who never changes their view is not reliable;
they have stopped updating. The competency is the pair of behaviours in §2.2, and evidence for only one
half of it is evidence of the wrong thing.

**The qualification is used as a weapon.** A report qualified in aggressive or accusatory terms invites a
fight about tone and buries the substance. The wording in §7 is deliberately flat: it states what was and
was not verified, and nothing about anyone's motives.

**Bad news is delivered at the wrong size.** A three-week slip presented with the gravity of a project
failure trains the audience to discount you; a serious problem mentioned in passing under "any other
business" is a disclosure that will not protect anyone, including its author.

**The organisation certifies behaviour it structurally prevents.** See §4. A behavioural rating scale
deployed into an environment that punishes the behaviour produces uniformly high ratings and no
information.

**Nobody records anything until it is a dispute.** By then the record is written by people who know how it
turned out, and the version most favourable to its author is the one that gets written. The whole
behavioural evidence base depends on notes made before anyone knew the ending.

## 7. Worked example: the wording of a qualification

*Illustrative wording; the figures within it are illustrative.* A cost report must be issued to a project
board on the due date. Two problems remain unresolved: the December progress claim has not been verified
against physical progress, and two instructed variations are unagreed. The professional cannot delay the
report and cannot sign it cleanly.

The qualification, placed in the report's summary rather than in an appendix:

> **Basis and limitations of this report.** The forecast at completion of 4,565,000 cost units assumes the
> remaining work is performed at the cost performance index achieved to date (0.909) and that no further
> scope is instructed. It includes agreed variations of 150,000 cost units. It **excludes** two instructed
> but unpriced variations, assessed by the project at 90,000 cost units against the contractor's submitted
> 140,000 cost units, and any cost consequence of the extension-of-time claim intimated on 12 November,
> which is not yet quantified. The December progress claim has not been verified against physical
> progress; verification is scheduled for 14 January. If the claim proves overstated, the earned value and
> therefore the forecast will move adversely.

Four properties make this work, and they are worth copying:

1. **It states the number and its assumptions in the same breath**, so nobody can quote the forecast
   without the condition attached.
2. **It separates what is included from what is excluded**, and gives the excluded items a range rather
   than a silence.
3. **It names a date for resolution.** A qualification with no closing action becomes permanent furniture
   and stops being read.
4. **It attributes nothing to anyone.** No motive, no blame, no adjectives. It is a statement about the
   report, which makes it very difficult to argue with and very easy to act on.

The consequence of writing it is that the board can no longer treat 4,565,000 as a settled figure — which
is the entire point, and the reason the paragraph is harder to write than the model that produced the
number.

## 8. Checklist

For a professional deciding how to handle a number nobody wants:

- [ ] Am I confident of this, or confident that it is likely? Which am I about to say?
- [ ] Is it early enough for the information to be useful, and if not, why did it take this long?
- [ ] Do I have the number, the basis and the specific decision I am asking for?
- [ ] Have I written it down today, with a date?
- [ ] What evidence would change my mind, and have I said so out loud?
- [ ] If I must issue this, what exactly can I not verify, and does the report say so in its summary?
- [ ] Which rung of the escalation ladder is proportionate — and have I tried the one below it?
- [ ] Am I presenting a decision or advocating a preference? Have I stated the strongest case against my
      own recommendation?
- [ ] For any AI-assisted or delegated figure: can I explain it in my own words, and did I check it?
- [ ] After the decision, will I implement it properly, or relitigate it monthly?

For an employer using this document to assess others: answer §4 first.
