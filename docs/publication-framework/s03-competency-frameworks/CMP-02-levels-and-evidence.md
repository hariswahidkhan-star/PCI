# Competency levels and how they are evidenced

> The four-level scale, the evidence that moves a person up it, and the two other ladders it is not.

**In one paragraph.** PCI describes competence on a four-point scale — foundation, practitioner,
professional and leader — applied one competency at a time. This document defines each level by the
decisions it carries rather than by adjectives, sets out the evidence hierarchy that moves a claim from
assertion to demonstration, and separates the competency scale from the credential level and from the
membership grades, which are different things that unfortunately share vocabulary. After reading it you
should be able to place a real person on the scale for a real competency and say what evidence would
change your placement.

**Who this is for.** Line managers writing development plans and appraisals, candidates assembling a
portfolio of evidence, and assessors who must apply one scale consistently across people they have never
met.

---

## 1. Why levels have to be defined by decisions

Most competency scales fail in the same place. They are written with adjectives — "basic", "good",
"strong", "expert" — and adjectives cannot be tested. Two managers rating the same person against
"strong cost management" will disagree, discover they disagree only at moderation, and settle it by
seniority rather than by evidence.

The scale below is written with **decisions and their consequences**. Each level answers four questions:

1. What does the person decide, and what do they escalate?
2. Who checks the output before it is relied upon?
3. What happens when the situation falls outside procedure?
4. Who carries the error when it turns out to be wrong?

Question four is the sharpest. It is possible to perform sophisticated work at practitioner level;
what makes the level professional is that when the number is wrong, the professional is the one who has
to explain why — and had the authority to make the choice that produced it.

## 2. The four levels

The scale applies to **one competency at a time**. A person is not "a practitioner"; a person is at
practitioner level in planning and scheduling and at professional level in commercial and contract
controls. Uneven profiles are the norm and carry the information that matters.

### 2.1 Foundation

Works within a defined procedure, on a defined part of the work, with the output checked before anyone
relies on it. Uses the established method because it is the established method. Recognises when
something looks wrong and escalates rather than resolving it. The organisation carries the error.

The distinguishing test: *can this person produce a correct output when the situation is normal?*

### 2.2 Practitioner

Owns a defined scope end to end — a control account, a discipline schedule, a section of a financial
model, a work package. The output is relied on without routine correction. Chooses between established
methods where the procedure prescribes the choice, and applies the procedure to cases it plainly covers.
Escalates the cases it does not.

The distinguishing test: *does the work leave this person's hands in a state where the next person builds
on it rather than checks it?*

### 2.3 Professional

Decides the method where procedure is silent, and defends the decision to someone who wanted a different
answer. Owns the number under challenge: can state the basis, the alternatives rejected, the sensitivity
of the result to each assumption, and the point at which they would change their view. Detects and
corrects error in other people's work, including work produced by a system. Sets the working standard
for a team.

The distinguishing test: *when the case is not covered by procedure and the answer is unwelcome, does
this person produce a defensible position — and hold it for the right reasons and release it for the
right reasons?*

### 2.4 Leader

Sets the rule that others apply, across projects rather than within one. Accountable for the integrity of
the system that produces the numbers, including when that system fails — which means owning the failure,
diagnosing whether it was an individual error or a design fault, and changing the design. Decides where a
method must **not** be used, including where an automated or AI-derived method must not be used, and
carries the consequence of that decision. Builds capability deliberately, so that the function survives
the departure of any individual.

The distinguishing test: *if this person left, would the method survive them — and did they design it so
that it would?*

### 2.5 The verb grammar

Level descriptors throughout this series are written with a controlled vocabulary, so that a reader can
tell the level from the verb even out of context.

| Level | Verbs | Ownership of error |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | assists · prepares · checks against a rule · escalates | The organisation |
| Practitioner | performs · owns a scope · applies · reconciles | The individual, within scope |
| Professional | decides · defends · challenges · corrects others | The individual, including the method |
| Leader | sets · governs · withholds · designs · answers for | The system and its failures |

"Understands", "is familiar with", "is aware of" and "has exposure to" appear nowhere in this framework.
They describe a mental state that no evidence can reach.

## 3. Three ladders that share vocabulary

This is the section to read twice, because the collisions are real and they mislead people about what
they hold.

| Ladder | What it measures | Values | Who assigns it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competency level | Depth of capability in one named competency | foundation · practitioner · professional · leader | Assessment against evidence |
| Credential level | The positioning of a whole credential | Leader (all three PCI credentials) | The certification scheme |
| Membership grade | Standing within the Institute | SPCI · APCI · MPCI · FPCI | Membership rules |

**Credential level.** All three credentials — PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI — are positioned at **Leader**
level. That is a statement about the credential, not a claim that every holder is at leader level on
every competency in its set. A credential samples a set of competencies and certifies that the holder met
the standard on the sample; it does not certify a uniform ceiling.

**A recorded inconsistency, stated rather than resolved.** The platform database records all three
credentials at level *Leader*. One public page (`certification.html`) renders the level as
*Professional*. This series uses **Leader**, in line with the platform record, and treats the
competency-scale word *professional* as an unrelated concept — a point on the scale in §2.3, never a
credential position. The discrepancy is genuine and is logged rather than quietly corrected:
[CONFIRM: single authoritative credential-level wording across platform database and public pages].

**Membership grade.** Student Member (SPCI), Associate Member (APCI), Professional Member (MPCI) and
Fellow (FPCI) are standings within the Institute. Professional Member requires an active PCI
certification; Fellow is by nomination. **A grade is not a competence measure and never evidences a
competency.** The traps are specific and both directions occur: reading MPCI as "assessed at professional
level" (it is not — it is a membership standing that happens to require a current certification), and
reading FPCI as a superlative competence rating (it is a nomination-based standing recognising
contribution). A fellow who has not earned a PCI credential holds no PCI certification.

## 4. The evidence hierarchy

A competency claim is worth what its evidence is worth. Evidence types are not interchangeable, and the
ordering below is the one an assessor should apply when two sources conflict.

| Rank | Evidence type | What it can show | What it cannot |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | An artefact you produced, dated, with your reasoning visible in it | Method choice, judgement under the conditions of the day | That you would repeat it under different conditions |
| 2 | An artefact you materially contributed to, with your contribution identifiable | Capability within a defined scope | Sole ownership, or the decisions someone else made |
| 3 | A structured professional discussion in which you are questioned on your own artefact | Depth behind the artefact; whether you can defend it | Anything the questioner did not think to probe |
| 4 | Direct observation of the work | Behaviour and interaction that artefacts never capture | Anything outside the observed period |
| 5 | Attestation by a named person with named standing who saw the work | Corroboration, scope, duration | Detail; and it inherits the attester's judgement |
| 6 | Examination performance | Applied knowledge and reasoning against a common standard | Whether the person does it under real pressure |
| 7 | Self-report | Where to look next | Capability. See §7 of `CMP-10` |

Two practical rules follow.

**Reasoning must be visible in the artefact, not reconstructed afterwards.** A forecast that records the
method used, the alternatives rejected and the assumption the answer hangs on is rank-1 evidence. The same
forecast without that record is a number, and a number can be produced by a spreadsheet someone else
built. The habit of writing the basis into the artefact at the time is the single cheapest thing a
candidate can do to make their own competence provable later.

**Contribution must be identifiable at the time, not asserted later.** "I built the risk model" is
uncheckable two years on. A model with a version history, a named owner per section, and an issue log
carrying your name against specific resolutions is checkable.

## 5. Evidence by level

The type of evidence changes as the level rises, because the thing being demonstrated changes.

| Level | What must be shown | Representative evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | Correct output under normal conditions, and escalation of the abnormal | Completed work to procedure; a raised query that turned out to be right; a checked-and-accepted deliverable |
| Practitioner | An owned scope, relied on without correction | A control account or model section you own; a month-end pack you produce; a reconciliation you sign |
| Professional | A defended decision where procedure was silent | A dated method-selection note; a forecast you revised upward against pressure, with the basis; a correction you made to a superior's number, and the outcome |
| Leader | A rule others apply, and accountability when it fails | A procedure or standard you authored and that is in use; a decision to withhold a method or tool, with reasoning; a post-incident change to the system after a failure you owned |

Note the shape of professional-level evidence: it usually documents **friction**. If a candidate's entire
portfolio is smooth — every forecast accepted, every position unchallenged — the portfolio is showing
practitioner-level work performed pleasantly, or a professional whose contemporaneous record is missing.
*CMP-09 — Behavioural and leadership competencies* covers what that friction looks like when handled well.

## 6. Currency: when evidence expires

Competence decays, and it decays unevenly. Judgement about contract mechanics survives a decade; fluency
in a reporting toolchain or an AI-assisted method does not survive two years of disuse.

The Institute's working position:

- Evidence more than **three years** old is treated as historical. It supports a claim; it does not carry
  one alone. Three years is chosen deliberately to align with the certification cycle.
- Evidence for any competency naming AI, automation or digital delivery is treated as
  **fast-decaying**: the method changes, and unexercised familiarity is not capability. Recertification
  therefore carries a mandatory AI-currency component, and the platform's continuing professional
  development category satisfying it is named exactly that. *CER-08* owns the recertification rules,
  including the binding continuing professional development requirement, which is not yet published
  [CONFIRM: binding CPD hours per three-year cycle — the student portal shows a 30-hour target, which is
  not a rule].
- A competency exercised on one project type only is evidenced for that context. Say so rather than
  generalising it; an assessor will find the boundary anyway, and finding it themselves costs the
  candidate credibility they did not need to lose.

## 7. How this goes wrong

**Levels are assigned by seniority.** A manager is rated professional because they are a manager. The
rating then becomes unfalsifiable, because nobody is going to rate their own manager down, and the scale
stops carrying information within a cycle.

**One level is assigned to a whole person.** The single most common misuse. It destroys the uneven
profile, which is the actionable part: the person who is professional in earned value and foundation in
automation has an obvious, cheap development plan, and "practitioner overall" hides it.

**"Professional" is read as a rank rather than a scale point.** Someone at professional level in three
competencies believes they hold a professional standing, or believes they are equivalent to a Professional
Member (MPCI). They are different ladders (§3). Where both appear in one document, label both.

**Leader level is claimed for scope rather than for rule-setting.** Managing thirty people is a span of
control. Leader level requires that a method exists because you decided it should, that others apply it,
and that you answered for it when it failed. Headcount is not evidence.

**Evidence is gathered retrospectively at application time.** The candidate reconstructs reasoning from
memory, and the reconstruction is uniformly favourable, because nobody remembers the assumption they
failed to test. This is not dishonesty; it is how memory works. The counter is contemporaneous recording,
which costs a paragraph at the time.

**A training certificate is filed as evidence of a competency.** It evidences attendance. It becomes
evidence of competency only when accompanied by work produced afterwards that the training explains.

**Currency is never tested.** A portfolio is accepted whole, including a demonstration from a project
that closed six years ago, on a delivery model the organisation abandoned. Ask when, not just whether.

**The scale is imported into pay banding unchanged.** The moment a level determines a salary band, every
rating conversation becomes a negotiation, evidence standards fall, and the framework's diagnostic value
is gone. Use it for development and assessment; if it must inform reward, put a moderation step
(*CMP-10* §5) between the rating and the money.

## 8. Checklist

For a levelling conversation about a real person:

- [ ] Are we levelling **one competency at a time**, by name?
- [ ] For each level claimed, which of the four questions in §1 have we actually answered — decision,
      check, procedure-silence, ownership of error?
- [ ] Is the evidence rank 1 or 2 in §4, or are we leaning on rank 5–7?
- [ ] Is the reasoning visible **inside** the artefact, or reconstructed in the conversation?
- [ ] For a professional-level claim, can we point to a decision made where procedure was silent, and to
      how it was defended?
- [ ] For a leader-level claim, can we name the rule, the people applying it, and a failure that was owned?
- [ ] How old is the evidence, and does the competency decay fast (§6)?
- [ ] Has anyone in the room used "professional" to mean the membership grade, or "leader" to mean
      seniority?
- [ ] Would a second assessor, given the same folder and this document, reach the same level?
